Module 9 CER Writing Assignment
From Synapse to Addiction: Tracing a Drug Through the Brain
Claim · Evidence · Reasoning · Integration · 12 pts · counts toward the CER writing category
📖 WHAT THIS IS
This is the writing that pulls Module 9 together. You have studied how the body governs itself — homeostasis and feedback, the neuron and its action potential, the synapse where one nerve cell speaks to the next. Now you follow a single drug into that system and trace what it does: how it acts at the synapse, what physiological effect that produces, and how repeated use can lead to the biological basis of addiction. This CER is assigned at the start of Week 26 and due at the end of Week 29, so the whole module can inform your argument. Your teacher will read and score this one personally — it is not auto-graded.
🧠 THIS IS A CONTESTED, OPEN-ENDED CER
This is the least-scaffolded essay of the year. You are not filling in a template — you are building the mechanism yourself and taking a reasoned position on what it means. Beyond tracing the biology, you will weigh a genuinely contested question: if addiction is rooted in the brain’s own chemistry, what does that tell us about self-government and responsibility? There is no single “right” answer to that last part — only arguments that are well-reasoned and grounded in the biology, or arguments that are not.
🎯 YOUR TASK
Choose one drug. Trace its mechanism of action from the synapse, through its physiological effect, to the biological basis of addiction — and then argue, from that biology, what addiction is and what it implies about self-government.
💊 STEP 1 · CHOOSE YOUR DRUG
Pick one of the well-documented examples below to keep your mechanism accurate. (You may choose a different drug only with your teacher’s approval, and only if you can describe its synaptic action correctly.) Each acts on the synapse in a specific way — that specificity is your starting evidence.
Drug How it acts at the synapse Short-term physiological effect
NicotineAgonist — mimics acetylcholine and binds nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, which triggers dopamine release in the brain’s reward pathway.Alertness, mild pleasure/reward, raised heart rate.
CaffeineAntagonist — blocks adenosine receptors. Adenosine normally makes you drowsy; blocking it removes the “slow-down” signal.Wakefulness, reduced fatigue (a milder dependence than the others).
Cocaine / amphetaminesReuptake blocker / releaser — stops dopamine from being cleared from the synapse (and amphetamines push more out), so dopamine piles up and keeps signaling.Intense but short-lived euphoria, energy, elevated heart rate.
Opioids
(morphine, heroin)		Agonist — binds opioid receptors, dampening pain signals and indirectly boosting dopamine in the reward pathway.Pain relief, sedation, strong reward (high dependence risk).
Notice the common thread: several of these end at the same place — a flood of dopamine in the reward pathway. That convergence is the key to the addiction part of your argument.
🧬 YOUR CONCEPT BANK
These are the ideas you studied this module. Use the ones that build your mechanism — a strong essay chains them together, cause to effect.
Concept What it says · how it connects
The synapseThe gap between neurons where the electrical signal becomes chemical. Neurotransmitters are released, cross the gap, and bind receptors on the next cell — then are cleared by reuptake or enzyme breakdown.
Agonist / antagonist / reuptake blockerThree ways a drug hijacks the synapse: mimic a neurotransmitter, block its receptor, or stop its clearance so the signal lingers. Which one is your drug?
Dopamine reward pathwayDopamine signaling that normally rewards survival behaviors (eating, connection). Many addictive drugs artificially flood this pathway far beyond natural levels.
Tolerance & dependenceTolerance: the same dose produces less effect, so more is needed. Dependence: the body now needs the drug to feel normal, and its absence produces withdrawal.
Neuroadaptation (homeostasis!)Faced with a repeated dopamine flood, the brain does exactly what a homeostatic system does — it pushes back: it reduces its own dopamine and downregulates receptors to restore balance. That very adaptation is what produces tolerance, dependence, and craving. Homeostasis, turned against the person.
The Na⁺/K⁺ pump (Module 3)The active-transport pump that sets up the resting potential every action potential depends on — the electrical groundwork beneath all of this. A useful backward link if your reasoning reaches for it.
📝 BUILD YOUR ARGUMENT IN FOUR MOVES
Write in connected paragraphs, not a list. The wording is yours — these are the moves a strong CER makes.
1
Claim. In one or two sentences, name your drug and state what addiction to it fundamentally is — for example, that it is a predictable biological consequence of repeatedly overstimulating the brain’s reward chemistry, not simply a lack of willpower. Make it a claim you will defend.
2
Evidence. Lay out the specific biology, in order: (a) how your drug acts at the synapse (agonist, antagonist, or reuptake blocker — name the neurotransmitter and receptor); (b) the physiological effect that action produces; (c) what happens in the brain with repeated use (the dopamine flood, then neuroadaptation). Use the correct terms — they are your evidence.
3
Reasoning — connect the mechanism to addiction. This is the heart of the essay. Explain why the mechanism leads to addiction: the drug drives dopamine far past natural levels; the brain, a homeostatic system, adapts by dialing down its own dopamine and receptors; that adaptation is exactly what produces tolerance (needing more) and dependence (feeling bad without it). Show the chain — synapse → effect → neuroadaptation → addiction — as cause and effect, not a list. If you choose caffeine, it does not flood the dopamine pathway the way the other drugs do; instead trace adenosine-receptor blocking, the brain’s adaptation (more adenosine receptors), and the resulting tolerance and withdrawal.
4
Integration — the contested question. Tie the biology to the module’s big idea. The same homeostasis that keeps you alive (Module 9) is what the brain uses to adapt to a drug — and that adaptation is what traps a person. So take a reasoned position: given that addiction has a real biological basis, what does that mean for self-government and responsibility? Is understanding the mechanism a reason for compassion, for prevention, for both? A strong essay does not dodge the tension — it reasons through it from the biology. Finally, state the strongest counterargument or limit of your own explanation (for example, factors beyond the synapse such as genetics, environment, or individual differences) and respond to it.
✨ THE VIRTUE THIS PRACTICES · SELF-GOVERNMENT
This module’s virtue is self-government — the quiet discipline by which the body regulates itself without your noticing. Addiction is what happens when that same self-regulating machinery is captured and turned against the person it was built to protect. Understanding the mechanism is not an excuse; it is the opposite. To know how a drug hijacks the reward pathway, and how quickly the brain adapts, is to see clearly why the wisest form of self-government is often the decision never to hand the controls over in the first place. Knowledge here is a kind of freedom.
✅ SUCCESS CRITERIA
☐
A clear claim states what addiction to the chosen drug fundamentally is (a biological consequence, not merely a failure of will).
☐
Evidence names the drug’s synaptic action correctly (neurotransmitter + receptor + agonist/antagonist/reuptake), its physiological effect, and what repeated use does.
☐
Reasoning explains the full causal chain to addiction — the dopamine flood (for caffeine: adenosine-receptor blocking and adaptation), the brain’s homeostatic neuroadaptation, and how that produces tolerance and dependence. Integration also names and answers the strongest counterargument or limit.
☐
Integration reads as one connected argument, ties addiction to homeostasis/self-government, and takes a reasoned position on the contested question — (~400–700 words).
📊 CER SCORING RUBRIC (3 PTS × 4 CRITERIA = 12 PTS)
Criterion Strong (3) Developing (2) Beginning (1)
Claim Specific and defensible; names the drug and states what addiction to it fundamentally is. States a position but vague about the drug or about addiction. Missing, or just restates the prompt.
Evidence Synaptic action named correctly (neurotransmitter, receptor, mechanism) plus effect and repeated-use change. Some correct biology, but a step is missing or a term misused. Little accurate biology, or mechanism largely wrong.
Reasoning Explains the full causal chain synapse → effect → neuroadaptation → tolerance/dependence. Links mechanism to addiction but the neuroadaptation step is thin or partly wrong. Describes the drug without explaining why addiction follows.
Integration One coherent argument; ties addiction to homeostasis/self-government and reasons through the contested question; within word range. Components present but the connection or the position is loose. Listed independently; no position taken.
Total possible: 12 pts
