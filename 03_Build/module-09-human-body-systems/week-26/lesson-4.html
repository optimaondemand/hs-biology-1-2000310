Lesson 26.04
Skeletal & Muscular Systems
Lesson · The Frame, the Engine, and How Muscles Pull
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A plant stands on cellulose and water pressure. An animal stands on bone — and moves it with muscle. How does that partnership work?
This lesson closes Week 26 on support and movement. Today you learn the skeleton's five jobs, the difference between a ligament and a tendon, the three muscle types, and the big idea of how a muscle contracts — the sliding-filament model, and the rule that muscles only pull, never push.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to list the skeleton's five functions and distinguish a ligament (bone-to-bone) from a tendon (muscle-to-bone).
2
I will be able to name the three muscle types (skeletal, cardiac, smooth) and their traits.
3
I will be able to explain the sliding-filament idea and why muscles must work in antagonistic pairs.

🧷 PART 1 · THE SKELETON, AND WHAT HOLDS IT TOGETHER

Five jobs, and two cords to keep straight

Bone is living connective tissue that does five jobs: support, protection, movement (as levers), mineral (calcium) storage, and blood-cell production in marrow. Bones meet at joints, held by two tough cords students often confuse. Sort each case below.

🧩 SORT IT · LIGAMENT, TENDON, OR BONE JOB
A tough cord holds the two bones of your knee joint together, bone to bone. What is it?
When your calf muscle contracts, it pulls on your heel bone through a tough cord (the Achilles). What kind of cord connects a muscle to a bone?
Red blood cells that carry oxygen are made inside certain bones. Which skeletal function is this?
When blood runs low on a certain mineral, bone releases its stored supply into the blood. Which mineral does bone bank in this way?
💪 PART 2 · HOW A MUSCLE CONTRACTS

Sliding filaments, and the pull-only rule

Inside a muscle cell, thin actin and thick myosin filaments slide past one another, shortening the cell — the sliding-filament model. Calcium triggers it; ATP (from Module 4) powers it. The key consequence: a muscle can only pull (shorten), never push. Reason through what that requires below.

🧠 THINK IT THROUGH · ANTAGONISTIC PAIRS
Your biceps contracts to bend your elbow. Since a muscle cannot push, how does your arm straighten again?

Because muscles pull and never push, they come in antagonistic pairs — biceps to bend, triceps to straighten. Every movement is two muscles taking turns to pull on a bony lever.

🧠 PART 3 · SEPARATE THE THREE MUSCLE TYPES

Two questions place any muscle

To sort any muscle, ask two things: is it voluntary or involuntary, and is it striated or smooth? Skeletal = voluntary + striated (moves bones); cardiac = involuntary + striated (the heart only); smooth = involuntary + not striated (hollow organs). Use the clue to slide to the right type.

⚡ PLACE IT · NAME THE MUSCLE TYPE
A muscle is involuntary, striated, and found only in the heart. Which type is it?
A muscle is voluntary and striated, attaches to bones by tendons, and moves the body. Which type is it?
A muscle is involuntary and not striated, and it lines the walls of the stomach and blood vessels. Which type is it?

Backward link: the ATP that powers every one of these contractions is the energy currency you traced in Module 4, and the calcium that triggers them is the mineral the skin's vitamin D helped absorb (Lesson 26.03) and that bone stores. Notice too that cardiac and smooth muscle run without any conscious command — quiet self-government, the module's virtue, keeping your heart beating and your gut moving while you think about something else.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

Skeleton and muscle are one partnership: bones give the levers, muscles supply the pull, tendons join them, ligaments brace the joints. Muscles only pull, so they work in antagonistic pairs. This closes Week 26 — from four tissues to a body that stands, senses, holds itself steady, and moves. Every level built from the one below, and the whole thing quietly governing itself.
