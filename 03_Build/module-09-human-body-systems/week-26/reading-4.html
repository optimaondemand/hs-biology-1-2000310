Reading 26.04
Frame and Engine: The Skeletal and Muscular Systems
Anchor Reading · Bones, Joints, Ligaments & Tendons · Muscle Types · Sliding Filaments
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
A plant holds itself up with cellulose walls and turgor pressure, as you saw last module. An animal does it differently — with a hard internal skeleton and the muscles that pull on it. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 26.04, and it closes the week on the theme of support and movement. By the end you should be able to list what the skeleton does, tell a ligament from a tendon, name the three types of muscle, and explain the big idea of how a muscle contracts — the sliding-filament model — including the crucial rule that muscles can only pull, never push.

The skeleton: far more than a frame

It is tempting to think of the skeleton as a dead scaffold, but bone is living connective tissue with a hard, mineral-rich matrix, and it does five jobs at once. It provides support — the framework that holds the body up and gives it shape. It provides protection — the skull shields the brain, the ribs cage the heart and lungs, the vertebrae guard the spinal cord. It enables movement, working as a system of levers that muscles pull on.

Two jobs are hidden inside the bone. Bone is the body's bank for minerals, chiefly calcium and phosphorus, releasing them into the blood when needed — a quiet homeostatic role. And inside many bones, the soft marrow is a factory for blood cells, producing the red cells that carry oxygen and white cells that fight infection. Support, protection, movement, mineral storage, and blood-cell production: one organ system, five functions.

Joints, ligaments, and tendons: keep them straight

Bones meet at joints, and joints are what make a rigid skeleton able to move. Some joints are fixed (the plates of the skull); many are freely movable, like the hinge of your knee or the ball-and-socket of your shoulder, where smooth cartilage cushions the bone ends.

Two tough connective cords hold the moving parts together, and students mix them up constantly, so fix the difference now: a ligament connects bone to bone (holding a joint together), while a tendon connects muscle to bone (so a muscle's pull can move the bone). A memory hook: a ligament glues bones together; a tendon ties a muscle to a bone.

Three kinds of muscle

You met the three muscle types briefly in Lesson 26.01; here is each with its key traits. Skeletal muscle attaches to bones by tendons and moves the body. It is voluntary (you consciously control it) and striated (striped in appearance under a microscope). Cardiac muscle makes up the wall of the heart. It is striated but involuntary — it contracts rhythmically on its own, without your command, for your entire life.

Smooth muscle lines the walls of hollow organs — the stomach, intestines, bladder, and blood vessels. It is involuntary and not striated, and it does slow, sustained work like pushing food through the gut. Voluntary and striped for the skeleton; involuntary and striped for the heart; involuntary and smooth for the organs — three muscles, each matched to its task.

How a muscle contracts: the sliding-filament idea

Inside a muscle cell run two kinds of protein filament: thin actin and thick myosin. When a muscle contracts, the filaments do not themselves shorten — instead the myosin grabs the actin and pulls, so the filaments slide past one another, overlapping more and shortening the whole cell. This is the sliding-filament model. The signal to start is calcium, released inside the cell, and the pulling is powered by ATP — the same energy currency you traced back in Module 4, now spent to move a body.

One rule follows from all this, and it matters: muscles can only pull, never push. A muscle contracts (shortens) to pull a bone, but it cannot lengthen itself to push the bone back. So muscles work in antagonistic pairs — one pulls the joint one way, its partner pulls it back. In your arm, the biceps contracts to bend the elbow; to straighten it again, the biceps relaxes and the triceps contracts and pulls the other way. Every movement you make is two muscles taking turns to pull.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Skeleton and muscle are one working partnership: bones give the levers and the muscles supply the pull, joined by tendons and braced by ligaments. Notice how the module keeps connecting — bone stores the calcium that the skin's vitamin D helped absorb (Lesson 26.03) and that also triggers muscle contraction; muscles burn the ATP of Module 4; and cardiac and smooth muscle run without conscious control, quiet examples of the body's self-government. This closes Week 26's tour of organization, homeostasis, and support. From four tissues to a body that stands, senses, holds itself steady, and moves — every level built from the one below.
