⚖️ PART 1 · THE LOOP THAT OPPOSES CHANGE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A thermostat turns the heat on when a room gets cold and off when it gets warm. Before you read on, does “homeostasis” mean your body keeps a value perfectly constant — or something a little different?

Four parts, one self-correcting loop

Homeostasis is keeping a stable internal environment despite a changing outside one. Its main mechanism is negative feedback, where the response opposes the change. The loop has four parts: a stimulus (something changes) is picked up by a receptor (a sensor), reported to a control center (usually the brain), which directs an effector (a muscle or gland) to produce a response that opposes the change — pulling the body back toward its set point. “Negative” does not mean bad; it means opposing.

📚 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF SCIENCE · THE DISCOVERY OF THE STEADY INSIDE
The idea that the body actively holds its own inner conditions steady was slow to arrive. In the 1850s and 1860s, the French physiologist Claude Bernard noticed that the cells of complex animals do not really live in the outside world — they are bathed in the body's own fluids. He argued that these fluids form an internal environment, the milieu intérieur, and that free, independent life depends on keeping that inner sea constant even as the outside world changes. It was a profound reframing: the body is not merely exposed to its surroundings; it builds and defends a world of its own within. More than half a century later, in 1926, the American physiologist Walter Cannon gave the phenomenon its name. He coined “homeostasis” from Greek roots meaning “similar” and “standing” — chosen carefully to mean a steady state, not a frozen one, because the body holds its balance through constant small adjustments. Two scientists, seventy years apart, slowly uncovered the body's quiet discipline of governing itself — this module's virtue, written in physiology.
🧩 PART 2 · NAME THE PART OF THE LOOP

Two worked loops: temperature and blood sugar

Each item below is one piece of a real negative feedback loop. Identify what it is.

🧩 MATCH IT · DESCRIPTION → ROLE
On a hot day you sweat and your skin vessels widen, cooling you down. What does this tell you about the response in a negative feedback loop?
Temperature receptors report to a brain region that compares the reading to the set point and orders sweating or shivering. Which part of the loop is this?
After you eat, blood glucose rises, and the pancreas releases a hormone that tells cells to store glucose, lowering blood sugar. Which hormone is it?
Hours after eating, blood glucose drops too low. The pancreas releases a hormone that tells the liver to release stored glucose. Which hormone is it?
🧠 PART 3 · NEGATIVE VS. POSITIVE FEEDBACK

Stabilize, or amplify?

Most of the body runs on negative feedback (opposes change, stabilizes). A few processes use positive feedback, where the response amplifies the change and drives it to a finish — like childbirth (oxytocin) and blood clotting. Sort the case below.

🧠 THINK IT THROUGH
During childbirth, each contraction triggers the release of more oxytocin, which causes stronger contractions, until delivery. Which kind of feedback is this, and why?

Keep the contrast sharp: negative feedback opposes and stabilizes (the everyday rule of homeostasis); positive feedback amplifies and finishes (a rare exception for events like birth and clotting). One loop — stimulus, receptor, control center, effector — run over and over is how a body of trillions of cells holds its inside world steady.
