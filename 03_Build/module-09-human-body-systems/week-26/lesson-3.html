The Integumentary System — Skin
Lesson · The Body's Largest Organ, and Homeostasis You Can Feel
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You wear your largest organ on the outside. What is skin actually doing besides holding you in?
Skin looks like a simple wrapper, but it is a layered, multi-tissue organ — the biggest one you have. Today you meet its three layers (epidermis, dermis, hypodermis) and its five jobs, and you watch the temperature loop from Lesson 26.02 happen in tissue you can touch: sweating, and blood vessels widening and narrowing.

🧠 PART 1 · SKIN IS AN ORGAN
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
People often call skin “the body's largest organ.” Before you read on, does skin really count as an organ, or is it just a covering?

Three layers, from the surface down

The epidermis is the thin, tough outer layer — epithelial tissue, waterproofed with the protein keratin and colored/shielded by melanin. Below it, the thicker dermis (connective tissue) holds the working machinery: blood vessels, nerve endings, and sweat glands. Deepest is the hypodermis (subcutaneous layer), mostly fat, which insulates and cushions. Together with hair, nails, and glands, these form the integumentary system.

🔍 PART 2 · THE FIVE JOBS OF SKIN

Tap each card to reveal what the skin does

Skin does five jobs at once. Tap each card to reveal how — and notice how many trace back to a structure in one of its layers.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · THE FIVE JOBS OF THE SKIN
PROTECTION (barrier) — tap to reveal
The continuous, keratin-filled epidermis is a physical barrier: it keeps microbes and injury out, blocks some ultraviolet light with melanin, and — just as importantly — keeps water in.
THERMOREGULATION — tap to reveal
Sweat glands release sweat that cools by evaporation, and dermal blood vessels dilate to shed heat or constrict to keep it in. This is the temperature negative feedback loop of Lesson 26.02, happening in skin you can touch.
SENSATION — tap to reveal
Nerve endings in the dermis make skin a giant sense organ, reporting touch, pressure, temperature, and pain to the brain — pain being the warning that pulls you away from danger.
VITAMIN D — tap to reveal
When sunlight strikes the skin, it triggers vitamin D production. The body needs vitamin D to absorb calcium — the same mineral that hardens the bones you meet in the next lesson.
WATER RETENTION — tap to reveal
The waterproof epidermis seals the water inside your cells. Without it, that water would evaporate away — a homeostatic job that keeps the whole body from drying out.
🌡️ PART 3 · THERMOREGULATION IN THE SKIN

Watch the loop happen in real tissue

On a hot day the skin sweats and its blood vessels widen; on a cold day the vessels narrow and you may shiver. Tap the cards to see each response — and check that it opposes the change, exactly like the negative feedback loop from Lesson 26.02.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · TOO HOT VS. TOO COLD
When you overheat — tap to reveal
Sweat glands release sweat (evaporation carries heat away) and skin blood vessels DILATE, bringing warm blood to the surface to shed heat. The response cools you — it opposes the rise, returning you toward 37°C.
When you get cold — tap to reveal
Skin blood vessels CONSTRICT to keep warm blood deep in the core, the fatty hypodermis insulates, and muscles may shiver to generate heat. The response warms you — again opposing the change.
Why this is negative feedback — tap to reveal
In both directions the skin's response REVERSES the temperature change and pulls the body back toward its set point. That is the defining move of negative feedback — and here the effectors (sweat glands, blood vessels) are tissue you can see and feel.

Forward link: the skin's sunlight-driven vitamin D lets the gut absorb calcium — the mineral stored in bone and needed to trigger muscle contraction, both of which you meet in the next lesson. One organ's work quietly feeds the next system's.
