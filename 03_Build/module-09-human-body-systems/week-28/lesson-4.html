📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Nerves send fast, private messages. But how does the body send a slow message to every cell at once — the kind that manages growth, stress, and blood sugar?
Today you meet the body's second coordination system: the endocrine system, whose messengers, hormones, travel in the blood. You will learn how a hormone reaches every cell yet acts only on its target cells, name the major glands and hormones (including the pancreas, whose insulin and glucagon run the glucose feedback of Lesson 26.02), and compare nervous versus endocrine signaling — the two systems that keep a whole body governed.

💧 PART 1 · A MESSAGE IN THE BLOODSTREAM

How a hormone finds only its target

The endocrine system is a set of glands that release hormones into the blood, which carries them everywhere. Yet a hormone affects only its target cells — the ones with the matching receptor. Every other cell ignores the message. It is the same key-and-lock logic as the synapse in Lesson 28.03, now played out across the whole body. Because it travels in blood, the message is slower to arrive but longer-lasting. Match each gland to what it sends.

🧩 MATCH IT · GLAND → HORMONE / JOB
Which gland releases insulin and glucagon to manage blood glucose?
Which gland releases hormones that set the body's overall metabolic rate?
Which glands release adrenaline (a fast stress surge) and cortisol (a longer-term stress hormone)?
Which gland is called the "master gland" because it directs several others (and releases the ADH that controls the kidney)?

The pancreas is worth pausing on: when blood sugar is high, insulin tells cells to take glucose in (lowering it); when it's low, glucagon tells the liver to release stored glucose (raising it). That is the exact negative-feedback loop from Lesson 26.02 — the endocrine system is the machinery behind it.

📚 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF SCIENCE · BANTING, BEST, AND THE SUMMER THAT BEAT DIABETES
Before 1921, type-1 diabetes — a failure of the pancreas to make insulin — was a death sentence, often striking children. That year, at the University of Toronto, a young surgeon named Frederick Banting, with almost no research experience, persuaded the physiologist J.J.R. Macleod to lend him a laboratory and a medical-student assistant, Charles Best, for a single summer. Working through the heat, Banting and Best isolated insulin within months. When the purified hormone was first given to a dying teenage patient in early 1922, his blood sugar fell and he recovered — a result that astonished the medical world and turned a fatal disease into a manageable one almost overnight. Banting and Macleod received the Nobel Prize in 1923; Banting, believing Best deserved recognition too, shared his prize money with him. It is a striking case of the module's virtue: patient, honest, evidence-driven work — and the humility to share the credit — winning something that has since saved millions of lives.
🔄 PART 2 · TWO SYSTEMS, ONE GOVERNED BODY

Fast wires and slow broadcasts

The body keeps two coordination systems because each is suited to different jobs. The nervous system is fast, electrical, and brief — ideal for yanking your hand off a stove. The endocrine system is slow, chemical, and longer-lasting — ideal for growth or steady blood-sugar control. Think through the card, then flip it.

🔄 FLIP CARD · TAP TO REVEAL
You touch a hot pan and instantly pull your hand away — and moments later your heart is still pounding and you feel jittery for several minutes.
Which system produced the instant pull-back, and which produced the lingering jitters?
(Tap to flip.)
✅ THE ANSWER
Nervous, then endocrine.
The instant pull-back is the nervous system — a fast, electrical, brief reflex down specific neurons. The lingering pounding and jitters are the endocrine system — adrenaline released into the blood, slower to build and longer to fade. Same scare, two systems, two timescales: exactly why the body keeps both.

Both systems work the same underlying way: a chemical binds a receptor. The neurotransmitter of Lesson 28.03 is really the endocrine idea shrunk to a single tiny gap. And reach back to Module 8: plants coordinate their whole bodies with hormones too — sensing and responding with no nervous system at all. Chemical messaging is one of biology's oldest answers to a universal question: how does a living thing govern all its parts as one?
