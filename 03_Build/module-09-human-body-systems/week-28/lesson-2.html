Lesson · A Cell Built to Fire an Electrical Signal

Today you meet the body's fastest messenger, the neuron. You will name its parts and match each to its job, see how the sodium–potassium pump from Module 3 keeps the cell charged and ready, and trace the action potential — the all-or-none electrical wave that races down the axon.

🧠 PART 1 · A CELL SHAPED TO SEND A MESSAGE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A nerve signal is often called "electrical." Before today, what did you picture that meant — is it electricity like in a wire? (No wrong answer — just check in.)

Receive, carry, hand off

A neuron is built for one job. The dendrites receive signals; the cell body holds the nucleus and keeps the cell alive; the axon transmits the signal away; the myelin sheath insulates the axon so the signal travels faster; and the axon terminals hand the signal off to the next cell. Branched to gather many inputs, long and insulated to send one output far — form follows function.

🔌 PART 2 · THE RESTING POTENTIAL & A PUMP YOU ALREADY KNOW

Charged and waiting: the Module 3 pump returns

At rest, the inside of a neuron is slightly negative compared with the outside — the resting potential, the loaded spring behind every signal. What holds that charge is a protein you met back in Module 3 as an example of active transport: the sodium–potassium pump. Using ATP, it moves 3 Na⁺ out of the cell for every 2 K⁺ in. Because it pushes more positive charge out than it brings in, the inside stays negative. Same pump, same mechanism you already studied — here it is the foundation of thought itself.

🔗 CROSS-UNIT LINK · MODULE 3
You first met the sodium–potassium pump as the textbook example of active transport — a membrane protein spending ATP to move ions against their gradients (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in). Nothing about the pump has changed. What is new is its consequence: that steady imbalance of charge is exactly what lets a neuron fire.

💡 PART 3 · ALL-OR-NONE, AND DOWN THE AXON

Why a neuron is like a light switch

If a stimulus crosses threshold, the neuron fires a full-strength action potential; if it doesn't, nothing happens. There is no half-signal — that is what all-or-none means. A stronger stimulus doesn't make a bigger spike; it makes the neuron fire more often. Once fired, the signal triggers the next patch, then the next, traveling down the axon to the terminals.
