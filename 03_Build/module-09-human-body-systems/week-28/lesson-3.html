🔒 PART 1 · CROSSING THE GAP
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before today: when you hear that a substance "goes to your head" or "affects your brain," what do you imagine it's actually doing to a brain cell? (No wrong answer — just check in.)

Release, bind, clear

At a synapse, the action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal and triggers the release of neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft. They cross the gap and bind receptors on the postsynaptic cell — a key in a lock — passing the message on. Then they are cleared: taken back up (reuptake) or broken down by an enzyme, so the synapse resets. The brain's messengers include dopamine (reward and motivation), serotonin (mood), acetylcholine (muscle signals, attention), and GABA (calming).

🧩 PART 2 · DRUG MECHANISM → EFFECT (WEB-INTERACTIVE)

Three ways to change a synapse's signal

Because the synapse runs on molecules binding receptors, a drug can change the signal three ways: an agonist mimics a neurotransmitter (adds signal), an antagonist blocks a receptor (removes signal), or a reuptake inhibitor stops clearance (prolongs signal). Match each accurate, textbook example to its mechanism and effect. This is biology, not instruction.

🧩 MATCH IT · MECHANISM & EFFECT
Nicotine fits nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and activates them. What mechanism is this?
Caffeine binds adenosine receptors but does not activate them, so adenosine (which promotes drowsiness) cannot act. What mechanism, and what effect?
Cocaine and amphetamines block the reuptake of dopamine, so dopamine lingers in the synaptic cleft. What mechanism, and what effect on the signal?

🎯 PART 3 · THE REWARD PATHWAY & THE BASIS OF ADDICTION

Why different drugs lead to the same trap

Very different addictive drugs — nicotine, opioids (agonists at opioid receptors), cocaine and amphetamines — converge on one result: they flood the brain's dopamine reward pathway far beyond any natural reward. With repeated flooding, the brain adapts: it reduces its own receptors (downregulation). Now the same dose does less — tolerance — and the brain leans on the drug just to feel normal — dependence. This is the mechanistic chain your CER will trace: receptor binding → changed signal → flooded reward pathway → tolerance and dependence.

✨ FROM TODAY'S RESEARCH · RETHINKING ADDICTION AS A BRAIN CONDITION
For a long time addiction was treated mainly as a failure of willpower. Recent research suggests a different picture: many scientists now describe addiction as a chronic condition of the brain's reward circuitry, in which repeated drug exposure produces lasting changes in dopamine signaling and in the circuits that govern self-control. Researchers are currently exploring treatments that target this biology — medications that ease withdrawal or reduce craving, and behavioral therapies studied alongside them. The findings are still developing and no approach works for everyone, but the shift in view matters: understanding addiction as a brain condition, rather than a simple moral failing, is changing how it is studied and treated. It is a reminder that knowing the mechanism can make us both more careful and more compassionate.

The self-government thread: none of this is a set of instructions — it is the opposite. Seeing exactly how a molecule can capture the brain's reward system is what lets you make an informed, deliberate choice. Knowing the chemistry is part of governing yourself. Your Module 9 CER essay will put this understanding into an argument.
