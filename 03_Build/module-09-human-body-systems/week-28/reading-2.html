Reading 28.02
The Neuron: How a Cell Sends a Signal Down Its Length
Anchor Reading · Neuron Structure, Resting Potential, and the Action Potential
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Touch a hot stove and your hand pulls back before you have even thought about it. That speed comes from a special kind of cell built to carry an electrical signal — the neuron. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 28.02. By the end you should be able to name the parts of a neuron and say what each does, explain how a neuron sits at rest thanks to a pump you already know from Module 3 — the sodium–potassium pump — and describe the action potential, the wave of electrical change that races down the axon and carries a message.

A cell shaped for sending messages

A neuron is a nerve cell, and its whole shape is built for one job: receive a signal at one end and pass it along at the other. Trace it in order. The dendrites are branching fibers that receive incoming signals from other cells. They feed into the cell body, which holds the nucleus and keeps the cell alive. From the cell body extends a long fiber, the axon, which transmits the signal away toward the next cell. At the far end, the axon terminals hand the signal off.

Many axons are wrapped in a fatty sheath called myelin, laid down in segments along the fiber. Myelin acts like insulation on a wire, and it lets the signal jump from gap to gap so it travels much faster. Form follows function once more: the dendrites are branched to gather many inputs; the axon is long and insulated to carry one output quickly and far.

Resting potential: a charged cell, waiting

Even when it is doing nothing, a neuron is not idle — it is charged and ready. At rest, the inside of the neuron is slightly negative compared with the outside. This difference in charge across the membrane is called the resting potential, and it is the loaded spring that makes a fast signal possible.

What holds that charge in place? A protein you already met in Module 3: the sodium–potassium pump. Using ATP, it performs active transport across the membrane, moving 3 sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell for every 2 potassium ions (K⁺) in. Because it pushes more positive charge out than it lets in, the inside stays negative. This is the very same pump, doing the very same job, that you studied as an example of active transport — here it is the foundation of every thought and every heartbeat.

The action potential: a wave that carries the message

When a neuron is stimulated strongly enough — past a certain threshold — it fires. The sequence is quick and precise:

Depolarization. Gates in the membrane fly open and Na⁺ rushes in. The inside, which was negative, suddenly swings positive at that spot.

Repolarization. Those gates close and different gates open so that K⁺ flows out, and the inside returns to negative — resetting that patch of membrane.

This flip-and-reset happens at one spot, then triggers the next spot, then the next — so the signal travels down the axon like a line of falling dominoes, from cell body to terminals.

One rule matters most: the action potential is all-or-none. If the stimulus crosses threshold, the neuron fires a full-strength signal; if it does not, nothing fires. There is no half-signal. A stronger stimulus does not make a bigger action potential — it makes them fire more often. Like a light switch, a neuron is either on or off.

Where the neurons work: two divisions

Billions of these cells are organized into the nervous system, which has two parts. The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain and spinal cord — the processing center that interprets signals and decides on responses. The peripheral nervous system (PNS) is the network of nerves reaching everywhere else, carrying sensory signals inward to the CNS and motor commands back out to muscles and glands. Together they let information travel across the whole body in a fraction of a second.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

A neuron is structure serving function at its most beautiful: a cell shaped to receive, insulated to speed the message, and electrically loaded — by the Module 3 sodium–potassium pump — so it can fire an all-or-none signal down its length. But a signal that reaches the end of one axon still has to cross to the next cell. That gap, and the chemistry that bridges it, is the subject of the next lesson — and it is where drugs, for good and ill, do their work.
