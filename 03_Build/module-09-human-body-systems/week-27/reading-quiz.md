**1. (Reading 27.01 — enzyme specificity from protein shape)**
The enzyme amylase breaks down starch but has no effect on protein. What best explains why one enzyme cannot break down just any molecule?

- A) Enzymes are used up after a single reaction, so each can only act once
- B) An enzyme is a protein whose folded shape fits only its specific substrate, like a key in a lock **✓**
- C) Enzymes only work on the largest molecule available in the cell
- D) Each enzyme randomly attacks whatever molecule it bumps into first

*An enzyme is a protein, and its folded three-dimensional shape forms an active site that fits only one kind of substrate. Amylase's shape fits starch, not protein, so it is substrate-specific. This is protein structure (Module 2) determining function — form follows function at the molecular scale.*

**2. (Reading 27.01 — villi, surface area, and absorption)**
The lining of the small intestine is folded into millions of tiny villi and microvilli. What is the main advantage of this folding?

- A) It slows food down so digestion takes longer
- B) It produces the acid needed to kill microbes
- C) It greatly increases the surface area available for absorbing nutrients **✓**
- D) It reabsorbs water from waste before it leaves the body

*Villi and microvilli multiply the intestine's surface area many times over, so far more nutrients can be absorbed into the blood. This is the surface-area-to-volume principle from Module 3 — the same reason cells stay small and alveoli are numerous. Water reabsorption is mainly the large intestine's job.*

**3. (Reading 27.02 — arteries defined by direction, pulmonary-artery misconception)**
A student says, "All arteries carry oxygen-rich blood." Why is this incorrect?

- A) It is correct — every artery always carries oxygen-rich blood
- B) Arteries are defined as vessels that carry blood away from the heart, and the pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood to the lungs **✓**
- C) Arteries carry only oxygen-poor blood; it is the veins that are oxygen-rich
- D) Arteries do not carry blood at all; they only carry nerve signals

*An artery is defined by direction — it carries blood away from the heart — not by what it carries. The pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood away from the heart to the lungs to pick up oxygen. So "artery" does not mean "oxygenated." Veins, likewise, are defined as carrying blood toward the heart.*

**4. (Reading 27.02 — capillaries as the exchange site)**
In which blood vessel does the actual exchange of oxygen, nutrients, and wastes between the blood and the body's cells take place?

- A) In the large arteries, because they carry the most blood
- B) In the veins, because they move slowly
- C) In the capillaries, because their walls are only one cell thick **✓**
- D) In the heart's ventricles, because they are muscular

*Capillaries are the tiny vessels connecting the smallest arteries to the smallest veins, and their walls are just one cell thick. This thinness is what allows oxygen, nutrients, and wastes to cross between blood and cells. Arteries and veins are transport routes; capillaries are the actual delivery site.*

**5. (Reading 27.02 — valves prevent backflow)**
What is the function of the valves found in the heart and in the veins?

- A) They add oxygen to the blood as it passes through
- B) They act as one-way gates that keep blood from flowing backward **✓**
- C) They pump the blood forward using muscle contractions
- D) They filter waste products out of the blood

*Valves are one-way gates. When the heart squeezes, the valves snap shut behind the blood so it cannot slide backward, keeping blood moving in a single direction; veins have valves for the same reason. Pumping force comes from the ventricle muscle, and oxygen is added at the lungs — not by the valves.*

**6. (Reading 27.02 — Harvey and the discovery of circulation)**
The physician William Harvey concluded in 1628 that blood must circulate in a closed loop rather than being made fresh by the liver. What kind of evidence led him to this conclusion?

- A) He used a microscope to see blood cells moving in a loop
- B) He measured how much blood the heart pumps and showed the liver could not possibly make that much **✓**
- C) He relied on the ancient authority of Galen's teachings
- D) He guessed, with no evidence, and was later proven wrong

*Harvey estimated the blood pumped per heartbeat and multiplied by the beats in an hour, getting a total far larger than the liver could manufacture from food. The only explanation that fit was that the same blood is reused — it circulates. His careful measurement overturned about 1,400 years of Galen's authority: evidence over authority (Module 1).*

**7. (Reading 27.03 — atherosclerosis and how a blockage harms tissue)**
How does atherosclerosis lead to a heart attack?

- A) Plaque narrows an artery, a clot blocks it, and the heart muscle downstream is starved of oxygen and begins to die **✓**
- B) The heart simply beats too fast and wears itself out
- C) The lungs stop adding oxygen to the blood entirely
- D) The veins carry too much oxygen to the heart

*Over years, plaque narrows and stiffens an artery (atherosclerosis). When a plaque ruptures and a clot blocks the already-narrow artery, blood to the heart muscle downstream is cut off. Deprived of oxygen, that muscle cannot finish cellular respiration and begins to die — a heart attack. The same event in the brain is a stroke.*

**8. (Reading 27.04 — alveoli and surface area for gas exchange)**
Why do the lungs contain hundreds of millions of tiny alveoli rather than a few large air sacs?

- A) To store a much larger total volume of air
- B) To pump air in and out more forcefully
- C) To create an enormous surface area for fast gas exchange **✓**
- D) To warm the incoming air more quickly

*Hundreds of millions of tiny alveoli, each wrapped in capillaries and one cell thick, together create a vast surface area — spread flat, much of a tennis court. A large surface with a short crossing distance lets oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse quickly. This is the same surface-area-to-volume principle seen in cells (Module 3) and intestinal villi.*

**9. (Reading 27.04 — breathing is not the same as cellular respiration)**
Which statement correctly distinguishes breathing from cellular respiration?

- A) They are two names for exactly the same process
- B) Breathing is the physical movement of air by muscles; cellular respiration is a chemical energy-releasing process inside cells **✓**
- C) Breathing happens in the cells; cellular respiration happens in the lungs
- D) Breathing makes glucose; cellular respiration makes oxygen

*Breathing (ventilation) is the muscular movement of air in and out, driven mainly by the diaphragm. Cellular respiration is the chemical process inside cells that releases energy from glucose. They are connected — breathing supplies respiration — but they are not the same thing, a distinction students often blur.*

**10. (Reading 27.04 — oxygen as the final electron acceptor: why we breathe)**
Why does the body actually need the oxygen taken in by breathing?

- A) Oxygen is used to build new glucose molecules in cells
- B) Oxygen is the final electron acceptor at the end of the electron transport chain in cellular respiration **✓**
- C) Oxygen is exhaled unchanged and does no work in the body
- D) Oxygen carries carbon dioxide out of the cells

*The oxygen you inhale serves as the final electron acceptor at the end of the electron transport chain (Module 4). Without oxygen to accept the spent electrons, the chain backs up and energy production stops. The carbon dioxide you exhale is the waste of that same process. In short, you breathe because your cells respire.*
