Week 27 · VR-A (Build / Model)
Model Blood Flow Through a Four-Chamber Heart
Solo VR activity · ~30 minutes · 10 pts (completion)
🔬 WHAT YOU’RE DOING
You will build a giant, walk-through four-chamber heart and then trace one red blood cell all the way around both circuits: body → right atrium → right ventricle → lungs → left atrium → left ventricle → body. As you build, you will label the four chambers, the valves between them, and the major vessels, then record a walkthrough narrating the full loop. Building the heart as a space you can step through makes the double loop concrete — you can see why the pulmonary and systemic circuits never mix, and why the left ventricle is the thickest chamber of all. This is Lesson 27.02, built in space.
🔧 WHAT YOU’LL NEED
A VR headset (recommended) or the ENGAGE desktop app · your completed Reading 27.02 and Lesson 27.02 · your scientific notebook.
📍 HOW TO JOIN
Device: VR headset or desktop · Session type: temporary
Session ID: posted by your teacher at the top of this Canvas assignment
Location: Human Heart / Body Systems Lab (Optima VR library)
On arrival: go to the empty heart-frame marked by the Whiteboard IFX. A sticky note there gives your first instruction — build the chambers before you trace the flow.
📝 WHAT TO DO, IN ORDER
1
Build the four chambers. Assemble the two atria on top and the two ventricles below, with a wall separating the right side from the left. Label each: right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium, left ventricle. Make the left ventricle wall the thickest — it pumps the whole body.
2
Add the valves and vessels. Place a one-way valve between each atrium and ventricle and at each ventricle's exit. Attach the major vessels: the pulmonary artery (right ventricle → lungs) and the aorta (left ventricle → body), plus the veins returning blood to each atrium. Label them.
3
Trace a red blood cell — pulmonary loop. Grab a red-blood-cell marker and move it: body → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs. Mark where it drops off CO₂ and picks up O₂. Note: the blood leaving the right ventricle is oxygen-poor — the pulmonary artery is the artery that carries oxygen-poor blood.
4
Trace the systemic loop. Continue the same cell: lungs → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body. Mark where the body's cells take O₂ and load CO₂, returning the now oxygen-poor blood to the right atrium — closing the loop where you began.
5
Record your walkthrough. Start a spatial recording. In about 60–90 seconds, walk the full loop naming each chamber, valve, and vessel in order, and say where oxygen is picked up (lungs) and dropped off (body). End by naming which chamber has the thickest wall and why.
📤 WHAT TO SUBMIT
Upload your spatial recording to this Canvas assignment. If you can’t record, submit one full snapshot of your labeled heart (all four chambers, valves, and major vessels visible) plus a typed list tracing the red blood cell through the full loop in order, marking where O₂ is picked up and CO₂ dropped off.
⏱️ TIME · ABOUT 30 MINUTES IN-HEADSET
Facilitator note: Students externalize the double loop by building the heart and physically walking a red blood cell through it. Two common errors to watch for and correct: mixing the two sides (right side = oxygen-poor blood to the lungs; left side = oxygen-rich blood to the body; they never mix), and assuming the pulmonary artery carries oxygen-rich blood (it does not — it carries oxygen-poor blood to the lungs, the key exception that shows arteries are defined by direction, not oxygen). A strong submission traces the full loop in correct order and correctly identifies the left ventricle as thickest because it pumps the whole body.
✅ SUCCESS CRITERIA (COMPLETION)
☐
All four chambers, the valves, and the major vessels (pulmonary artery and aorta) are built and labeled, with the left ventricle shown as the thickest.
☐
A red blood cell is traced through the full loop in order (body → right atrium → right ventricle → lungs → left atrium → left ventricle → body), marking where O₂ is picked up and CO₂ dropped off.
☐
A spatial recording (or full snapshot + typed trace) is submitted, naming each chamber, valve, and vessel and explaining why the left ventricle wall is thickest.
