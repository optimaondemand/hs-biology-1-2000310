Reading 27.02
The Circulatory System: A Pump, Two Loops, and a River of Blood
Anchor Reading · The Heart, the Cardiac Cycle, Blood Vessels, and Blood
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
The digestive system absorbs nutrients into the blood — but blood must reach every cell for that to matter. The circulatory system is the body's transport network: a muscular pump, a branching set of vessels, and the blood itself. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 27.02. By the end you should be able to trace blood through the four-chambered heart and its two loops, tell an artery from a vein from a capillary, and name what plasma, red cells, white cells, and platelets each carry or do.

The heart: a four-chambered double pump

The heart has four chambers: two thin-walled atria on top that receive blood, and two thick-walled ventricles below that pump it out. The right side and the left side do different jobs, and blood does not mix between them — a wall separates the two sides. In effect, the heart is two pumps built into one organ.

Between each atrium and ventricle, and at the exit of each ventricle, sit one-way valves. When the heart squeezes, the valves snap shut behind the blood so it cannot flow backward. This is why blood moves in one direction only, and why a healthy heartbeat has its familiar two-part sound — the valves closing.

Two loops: the pulmonary and the systemic circuits

Blood travels two connected loops. In the pulmonary loop, the right side of the heart pumps oxygen-poor blood to the lungs, where it drops off carbon dioxide and picks up oxygen, then returns to the left side. In the systemic loop, the left side pumps this oxygen-rich blood out to the whole body, where cells take the oxygen and load their carbon-dioxide waste, and the now oxygen-poor blood returns to the right side. Then it starts again.

Follow one drop of blood: it enters the right atrium from the body, drops into the right ventricle, is pumped to the lungs, returns to the left atrium, drops into the left ventricle, and is pumped out to the body. The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it must push blood the farthest — all the way around the body.

One caution that trips up many students: the vessel carrying blood to the lungs (the pulmonary artery) carries oxygen-poor blood, and the vessels carrying blood from the lungs carry oxygen-rich blood. So “artery” does not mean “oxygen-rich.” An artery is defined by direction, not by what it carries — which is exactly the point of the next section.

📚 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF SCIENCE
William Harvey measures the heart

For nearly 1,400 years, European medicine followed the ancient physician Galen, who taught that the liver constantly manufactured fresh blood, which the body then used up. In 1628, the English physician William Harvey published a short book, De Motu Cordis (“On the Motion of the Heart”), and did something new: he measured. He estimated how much blood the heart pushes out with each beat and multiplied by the beats in an hour. The number was enormous — far more blood than the liver could possibly make from food in that time.

There was only one way to make the arithmetic work: the same blood must be used again and again. Blood must circulate in a closed loop, out through the arteries and back through the veins. Harvey was mocked at first — some colleagues called him “crack-brained” — but his measurements held, and within a generation he was proven right. It is one of biology's clearest lessons in the nature of science, the same lesson from Module 1: careful evidence can overturn even 1,400 years of authority.

Three kinds of vessel: arteries, veins, capillaries

Arteries carry blood away from the heart. Because the heart's push is strong, they have thick, elastic, muscular walls that stretch with each beat — the stretch you feel as a pulse.

Veins carry blood back toward the heart. The pressure here is low, so veins have thinner walls and contain one-way valves that keep blood from sliding backward as it works its way home.

Capillaries are the tiny vessels that connect the smallest arteries to the smallest veins. Their walls are just one cell thick — and this is where the real work of the circulatory system happens. Oxygen, nutrients, and wastes cross between blood and body cells only across capillary walls. Arteries and veins are highways; capillaries are the delivery doors.

Remember the definitions by direction, not by oxygen: arteries go away from the heart, veins go toward it, whatever they happen to be carrying.

What blood is made of

Plasma is the pale liquid — mostly water — that carries everything else: dissolved nutrients from digestion, wastes, hormones, and the blood cells themselves.

Red blood cells carry oxygen. They are packed with the iron-containing protein hemoglobin, which grabs oxygen in the lungs and releases it in the tissues. Their red color is the hemoglobin.

White blood cells are the body's defense — they hunt and destroy invading microbes and are part of the immune system.

Platelets are cell fragments that trigger clotting. When a vessel is cut, platelets gather at the wound and help form the plug that stops the bleeding.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

The circulatory system is the body's delivery service: a four-chambered pump drives blood through two loops, and every exchange — oxygen in, waste out, nutrients delivered — happens across one-cell-thick capillary walls. It connects backward to the nutrients absorbed by digestion (Reading 27.01) and forward to the lungs, where the blood is refreshed with oxygen (Reading 27.04). It also carries the seed of the next lesson: when the arteries that feed the heart itself become clogged, this whole system is put at risk.
