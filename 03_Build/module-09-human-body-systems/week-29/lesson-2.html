🧬 PART 1 · A LOCK FOR EVERY KEY
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Once you have had the measles (or its vaccine), you are protected for years. Before reading on, what do you think keeps that protection around so long?

B cells, T cells, and antigens

Adaptive immunity is specific (it targets one invader), slower the first time, and has memory. It recognizes an invader by its antigen — a specific molecule, usually a protein, on the invader's surface. Its cells are lymphocytes. B cells make antibodies — Y-shaped proteins whose tips are folded into a shape that fits one antigen like a lock fits one key (a payoff of the protein-shape idea from Module 2). T cells come in two kinds: helper T cells coordinate the whole response, and cytotoxic (killer) T cells destroy the body's own cells that a virus has infected.

📚 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF SCIENCE · A MILKMAID'S HANDS AND THE FIRST VACCINE
Long before anyone knew what a lymphocyte was, country doctors noticed something strange: milkmaids who caught cowpox, a mild disease, seemed never to get the deadly smallpox. In 1796, the English physician Edward Jenner put the observation to the test. He took material from a cowpox sore and introduced it into a boy, then later exposed him to smallpox — and the boy did not fall ill. Jenner had performed the first vaccination (the word comes from vacca, Latin for cow), decades before anyone understood why it worked. The why came later. In the 1860s–1880s, Louis Pasteur in France and Robert Koch in Germany built the case for germ theory — the idea that specific microbes cause specific diseases. Germ theory began to explain what Jenner had stumbled on, though the immune mechanism itself was worked out later still: he had shown the body a harmless relative of smallpox, and it had built the memory you are studying today. Careful observation came first; the mechanism came after — a pattern this course keeps meeting.

✅ THE ANSWER
Memory B cells and memory T cells.
After the battle, most cloned lymphocytes die, but some survive for years as memory cells. Antibodies themselves fade — they are proteins, and they break down. The lasting record of the enemy is stored in the memory cells. If the same antigen returns, the memory cells recognize it at once and make fresh antibodies, faster and stronger than the first time. Immunity lives in the cells, not in leftover antibodies.

This is the single most common exam misconception on this topic. Say it in your own words in your notebook: immunity is remembered through memory B and T cells, not through antibodies floating around forever.

🧲 PART 3 · CLONAL SELECTION

Why the first response takes days

Out of millions of different lymphocytes, only a few happen to match a given antigen. In clonal selection, the lymphocyte whose receptor matches the antigen is the one selected to multiply, dividing again and again into an army of identical clones — and later leaving memory cells behind. Finding and growing that one matching cell is exactly why the first response is slow. (Notice the echo of Module 6: the best-matched variant is the one that gets copied.)

The chain to remember: antigen appears → the matching lymphocyte is selected → it multiplies (clonal expansion) → some clones fight now, and some become memory cells → on re-exposure, memory cells respond faster and stronger. You will build this exact sequence in this week's VR-A.
