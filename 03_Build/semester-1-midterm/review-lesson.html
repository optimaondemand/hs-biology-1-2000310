📚 WHAT THIS WEEK IS
No new biology this week — just the chance to see how all of it connects.
For fourteen weeks you have been building one continuous argument, not five separate units. It started with how we know anything in science, moved to the atoms and molecules life is made of, then to the cell, then to how the cell handles energy, and finally to how it stores and passes on information. This review page is a grand tour of that whole chain. Read it slowly, do the two quick check-ins, then complete the Build the Spine review activity and sit the midterm. The exam rewards students who see the connections, not just the isolated facts.
🧬 THE SPINE OF SEMESTER 1
M1 · How we know → M2 · Atoms & molecules → M3 · The cell → M4 · Energy → M5 · Information & heredity
Each link makes the next one possible. Chemistry explains how cells can exist; cells explain how energy is captured and released; molecular genetics explains how cells reproduce and pass life on. Module 1 sits above the whole chain — the method you used to learn every link.

The Grand Tour — Module by Module
Each stop: the big idea, the must-know facts, and the bridge that carries you to the next module.
🔭 MODULE 1 · NATURE OF SCIENCE — the lens
Big idea: science earns trust through evidence, testing, and revision.

Must know: observation vs. inference; a fair test has one independent variable, a measured dependent variable, controlled variables, and a control group. A hypothesis is a testable proposed explanation; a theory is a broad, well-supported explanation backed by many lines of evidence (not “a guess that grew up”); a law describes what happens, while a theory explains why. The CER tool — Claim, Evidence, Reasoning — is how you argue from data. Redi and Pasteur retired spontaneous generation with controlled experiments.

→ The bridge: this is the lens for every unit that follows — and the CER framework is the tool behind every assignment and both midterm short-responses.
🧪 MODULE 2 · CHEMISTRY OF LIFE
Big idea: life is built from atoms and molecules, and structure determines function.

Must know: water’s bent, polar shape and hydrogen bonding give it cohesion and surface tension, a high specific heat that moderates temperature, floating ice, and its power as the universal solvent of life. The four macromolecules — carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids — are built by dehydration synthesis and broken by hydrolysis. The deepest rule of the module: a protein’s function comes from its folded shape; change the shape (heat, pH — denaturation) and you change what it can do.

→ The bridge: phospholipids and proteins become the cell membrane and the enzymes of Module 3 and 4; the shape→function rule returns in Module 5 to explain sickle-cell disease.
🧤 MODULE 3 · CELL STRUCTURE, FUNCTION & TRANSPORT
Big idea: the cell is life’s basic unit, and its membrane controls what enters and leaves.

Must know: the three tenets of cell theory; prokaryotic vs. eukaryotic; organelles by function; the fluid-mosaic membrane is a phospholipid bilayer studded with proteins. Diffusion and osmosis are passive (high→low, no energy) — osmosis is the movement of water. In a hypotonic solution a cell swells (and may burst); in a hypertonic one it shrivels; in an isotonic one it holds steady (why IV fluid is 0.9% saline). Active transport (the Na⁺/K⁺ pump) spends ATP to move things against the gradient.

→ The bridge: two organelles — the chloroplast and mitochondrion — are where Module 4’s energy reactions happen; the Na⁺/K⁺ pump returns as the neuron’s resting potential in Module 9.
⚡ MODULE 4 · CELL ENERGY
Big idea: cells capture, store, and release energy — energy flows, matter cycles.

Must know: ATP is the energy currency. Photosynthesis (in the chloroplast) uses light to build glucose: light-dependent reactions in the thylakoid make ATP/NADPH and release O₂ from splitting water; the Calvin cycle in the stroma builds sugar and does not need direct light. Cellular respiration (glycolysis → Krebs → electron transport chain) releases that energy; O₂ is the final electron acceptor. The two are not simple reverses of each other — plants do both, and respiration runs all the time.

→ The bridge: the carbon atoms moving through these reactions are the same ones that travel the global carbon cycle in Module 10 — the cell’s chemistry written at planet scale.
🧬 MODULE 5 · DNA, CELL DIVISION & GENETICS
Big idea: information is stored, copied, expressed — and inherited.

Must know: DNA is a double helix with A–T, C–G pairing; replication is semiconservative. Mitosis makes two identical cells; meiosis makes four genetically unique gametes (crossing over + independent assortment = variation). The central dogma: DNA is transcribed to mRNA (in the nucleus) and translated to protein (at the ribosome), three-letter codons at a time. A single base substitution can change one amino acid, change a protein’s shape, and cause disease — sickle cell (GAG→GUG, glutamic acid→valine). Mendel’s ratios, Punnett squares, and pedigrees predict inheritance. Biotech tools (gel electrophoresis — by size; PCR; CRISPR) read and edit this code.

→ The bridge: the heritable variation that meiosis and mutation create is exactly the raw material natural selection acts on — the first topic of Semester 2 (Module 6, Evolution).

📝 WHAT’S ON THE MIDTERM
The Week-15 Midterm is built in the same EOC format as your weekly quizzes — in fact, every multiple-choice question is drawn straight from those quizzes, so nothing on it is new.
50 multiple-choice
Spread across all five modules, weighted by how long each module was: more from the cell, energy, and genetics units. Re-do your weekly quizzes to study.
2 CER short-responses
One molecular/cellular (water’s properties or a red blood cell in different solutions) and one genetic (trace a mutation from DNA base to phenotype). Claim · Evidence · Reasoning — explain the mechanism.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

If you can walk the spine in your own words — how we know → atoms and water → the cell and its membrane → capturing and releasing energy → copying and inheriting information — you understand the first half of biology, because you understand how each idea makes the next one possible. Semester 2 picks the thread right back up: the heritable variation you just studied is what evolution acts on. Walk the spine, and you are ready.
