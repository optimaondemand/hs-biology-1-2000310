Before you begin: finish Reading 34.02 and work through Lesson 34.02, including the HIPPO drivers, climate change, and why habitat destruction (and fragmentation) is the largest cause.
Lesson focus: The pressures on biodiversity, and the one that leads
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
This assignment has three short parts: match each driver to how it harms biodiversity, name the single largest cause, and explain in a sentence or two why habitat fragmentation harms a species even when little total area is lost. It should take about 15–20 minutes.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
Of all the pressures on biodiversity, which one does the most damage — and how does each of the others work?
📝 STUDENT DIRECTIONS
1
Part A — Match driver to mechanism (5 pts, 1 each). Match each driver of biodiversity loss to how it does its damage.
Driver How it harms (write the letter)
Habitat destruction 
Invasive species 
Pollution 
Overexploitation (overharvesting) 
Climate change 
A. Removes the places organisms live — the single largest cause  ·  B. Non-native species spread without natural controls and out-compete or eat natives  ·  C. Degrades air, water, and soil; some toxins build up through the food chain  ·  D. Takes wild organisms faster than they can reproduce (overfishing, poaching)  ·  E. Shifts temperature and rainfall faster than species can adapt
2
Part B — Name the leader (2 pts). In one sentence, name the single largest cause of biodiversity loss worldwide and state why an organism cannot survive when it happens.
3
Part C — Why fragmentation hurts (3 pts). In one or two sentences, explain why breaking a habitat into small, isolated patches (fragmentation) harms a species even when the total area lost seems small. (Hint: think about small populations and genetic diversity.)
✅ SUCCESS CRITERIA
☐
Part A: habitat destruction → A, invasive species → B, pollution → C, overexploitation → D, climate change → E.
☐
Part B names habitat destruction as the single largest cause, and explains that an organism whose habitat is gone has nowhere to live and cannot survive.
☐
Part C explains that fragmentation isolates small populations, which lose genetic diversity, struggle to find mates, and are far more likely to die out — even when little total area is lost.
