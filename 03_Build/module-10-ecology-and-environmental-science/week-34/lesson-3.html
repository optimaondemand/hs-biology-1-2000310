🐍 PART 1 · WHAT MAKES A SPECIES INVASIVE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A garden tomato is not native to Florida, yet no one calls it invasive. Before reading on, why do you think that is?

Two conditions, one reason they thrive

An invasive species must meet two conditions: it is non-native (introduced where it did not naturally occur) and harmful (causing ecological or economic damage). A harmless introduced species is merely introduced; the harm earns the label. And the reason invaders spread so explosively is a single principle: they arrive without their natural controls. In their homelands, predators, parasites, diseases, and competitors held them in check. Moved to a new place, those checks are gone — and native species never evolved defenses against the newcomer. Freed from control, facing unprepared neighbors, the invader multiplies.

🤖 PART 3 · CAN AI HELP US TRACK THE INVADERS?

A tool worth using — and interrogating

Finding invaders across thousands of square miles of swamp and reef is a monitoring problem, and it is exactly the kind of task where machine learning now helps — the same species-identification idea you first met in Module 7. But a tool this useful still has to be treated the way you treat any scientific claim.

🤖 AI AWARENESS · CAN A MODEL SPOT AN INVADER?
The opportunity is real. Camera-trap image classifiers can sort millions of wildlife photos to flag a python or a lionfish; acoustic monitoring can pick a cane toad's call out of a marsh soundscape; and citizen-science apps like iNaturalist let thousands of people photograph what they find, with AI suggesting an identification and researchers tracking native and invasive species at a scale no team could match by hand. But the pitfalls deserve equal weight. A classifier can misidentify a look-alike — a harmless native snake tagged as a python, or the reverse. Its training data is biased toward the common and photogenic species people upload most, so rare or camera-shy organisms are under-represented and easily missed. And a confident label is still just a hypothesis: a flagged invader should be verified by an expert before anyone acts on it, because a wrong ID can waste scarce conservation effort or, worse, target the wrong animal. Treat an AI identification the way you treat any scientific claim — a useful lead to check, not a verdict to obey.
