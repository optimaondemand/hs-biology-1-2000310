**1. (Reading 34.01 — the three levels of biodiversity)**
A biologist studies the range of different alleles for disease resistance carried by individuals within a single wild rabbit population. Which level of biodiversity is she measuring?

- A) Species diversity
- B) Genetic diversity **✓**
- C) Ecosystem diversity
- D) Trophic diversity

*Biodiversity has three levels. Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles within a single species — exactly what is being measured here. Species diversity is the number and evenness of different species in a community, and ecosystem diversity is the variety of habitats across a region.*

**2. (Reading 34.01 — why diversity brings stability and resilience)**
Why does an ecosystem with high species diversity tend to be more stable and resilient than one with very few species?

- A) More species means less competition for food and space, so populations rarely lose any members
- B) Diverse ecosystems have fewer predators, so no prey population ever crashes in them
- C) Many species create redundancy in the food web, so if one is lost, others can fill its role **✓**
- D) More species means the ecosystem uses less energy overall, which leaves a bigger reserve

*Greater diversity generally brings greater stability and resilience because of redundancy: when several species fill similar roles, the loss of one can be absorbed as others take up its part. A simple web with only one species per role collapses when any one is lost, because there is no backup.*

**3. (Reading 34.02 — the leading cause of biodiversity loss)**
Which of the following is the single largest cause of biodiversity loss worldwide?

- A) Habitat destruction and habitat fragmentation **✓**
- B) Volcanic eruptions and large earthquakes
- C) The normal aging of individual organisms
- D) Seasonal changes in temperature and rainfall

*Habitat destruction — clearing forests, draining wetlands, and paving land — is the leading cause of biodiversity loss, because an organism with nowhere to live cannot survive. Other real drivers (invasive species, pollution, overharvesting, climate change) add to the loss, but habitat destruction leads.*

**4. (Reading 34.03 — definition of an invasive species)**
A non-native plant is introduced to Florida but causes no ecological or economic harm and simply blends into the landscape. Based on the scientific definition, is it "invasive"?

- A) Yes — any species that is non-native to an area is automatically considered invasive there
- B) Yes — all introduced plants count as invasive, though introduced animals are judged differently
- C) No — plants can never be classified as invasive, since only animals are able to spread
- D) No — an invasive species must be both non-native and cause ecological or economic harm **✓**

*An invasive species must meet two conditions: it is non-native, and it causes harm. A harmless introduced species is merely "introduced," not invasive. It is the ecological or economic damage that earns the label — which is why this quiet, harmless plant does not qualify.*

**5. (Reading 34.03 — why invasive species spread)**
The Burmese python and the lionfish both spread explosively in their new Florida-area habitats. What best explains why invaders like these can multiply so successfully?

- A) They arrive without the predators, parasites, and competitors that limited them at home, and natives lack defenses **✓**
- B) They reproduce more slowly than native species, so each one invests more care in its offspring and survives much longer
- C) They are physically larger than every native species, so natives cannot compete or escape them
- D) Florida's climate is too harsh for them, which forces them to reproduce faster to avoid dying out

*Invaders thrive mainly because they leave their natural controls behind. Freed from the predators, parasites, and competitors of their native range — and facing native species that never evolved defenses against them — they can multiply and spread unchecked.*

**6. (Reading 34.03 — Florida invasive examples and their mechanisms)**
Which pairing of a Florida invasive species with the way it harms native communities is correct?

- A) Lionfish — grows into dense stands that crowd out native plants by shading them
- B) Brazilian pepper tree — poisons the predators that try to eat it with a toxin in its skin
- C) Cane toad — devours native reef fish with the venomous spines along its back
- D) Burmese python — acts as an unchecked apex predator, eating native mammals in the Everglades **✓**

*The Burmese python functions as a new apex predator in the Everglades, eating native mammals such as raccoons and marsh rabbits. The lionfish strips native reef fish; the Brazilian pepper tree crowds out native plants; and the cane toad poisons predators with its skin toxin — each a different mechanism.*

**7. (Reading 34.02 / 34.04 — renewable vs. nonrenewable resources)**
Which list contains only renewable resources?

- A) Coal, oil, natural gas, and other fossil fuels
- B) Oil, natural gas, most metal ores, and mineral deposits
- C) Solar energy, wind, and a sustainably managed forest **✓**
- D) Coal, wind, and most mineral deposits in the ground

*Renewable resources are replenished on a human timescale — solar energy, wind, and, if used wisely, forests and fisheries. Nonrenewable resources exist in fixed amounts or form far too slowly to replace, including fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) and most minerals.*

**8. (Reading 34.04 — renewable resources can still be lost)**
A fishery is usually called a "renewable" resource, yet many fisheries have collapsed from overfishing. How is this possible?

- A) Fisheries are actually nonrenewable resources, so they were mislabeled before collapsing
- B) Renewable resources last only if harvested no faster than they can replenish themselves **✓**
- C) Fish stop reproducing whenever humans are present, so no new fish replace those harvested
- D) Overfishing converts the fish population into a mineral resource, which is nonrenewable

*"Renewable" is a promise with a condition. A fishery or forest can replenish itself only within limits; harvest it faster than it can reproduce or regrow and even a renewable resource collapses. Overfishing does exactly this — taking fish faster than the population can replace itself.*

**9. (Reading 34.04 — conservation strategies)**
If habitat destruction is the single largest cause of biodiversity loss, which conservation strategy most directly addresses that leading cause?

- A) Establishing protected areas and reserves that directly guard habitat **✓**
- B) Breeding single endangered species one at a time in zoos and wildlife centers
- C) Removing all predators from an ecosystem so prey populations can grow
- D) Introducing more non-native species into a habitat to increase its diversity

*Because habitat loss leads, protecting habitat directly — through parks, wildlife refuges, and marine reserves — is the most powerful conservation tool. Captive breeding helps one species at a time; removing predators or adding non-native species would harm biodiversity, not protect it.*
