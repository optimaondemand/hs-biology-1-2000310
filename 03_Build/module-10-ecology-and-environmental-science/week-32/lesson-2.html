Lesson 32.02
The Nitrogen Cycle: The Work of Invisible Bacteria
Lesson · Fixation, Nitrification, Assimilation, Ammonification, Denitrification
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The air is 78% nitrogen. You breathe it constantly. So why can a plant — or you — still starve for nitrogen?
Today you meet a cycle with a paradox at its center: organisms cannot use nitrogen gas (N₂) directly, even though they are surrounded by it. The solution is a crew of bacteria that fix, convert, and eventually release nitrogen through five steps — fixation, nitrification, assimilation, ammonification, and denitrification. It is a striking return of the “invisible” prokaryotes from Module 7.

🍃 PART 1 · THE NITROGEN PARADOX
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Nitrogen makes up most of the air, and living things need it to build proteins and DNA. Before reading on, how do you think a plant gets the nitrogen it needs?

A bond too strong to break

Atmospheric nitrogen is N₂ — two atoms held by a triple bond so strong that neither plants nor animals can pull it apart. This is the key fact of the lesson: you cannot get nitrogen just by breathing it, and a plant cannot get it just from the air around its leaves. The gas must be fixed — converted to a usable form — first. Almost every step of the cycle is done by bacteria, the very prokaryotes that Module 7 showed are easy to overlook and impossible to do without.

🔄 PART 2 · THE FIVE STEPS

Around the loop, one step at a time

1. Nitrogen fixation — bacteria (e.g., Rhizobium in legume root nodules) and lightning convert N₂ → ammonia/ammonium.
2. Nitrification — bacteria convert ammonium → nitrite → nitrate, the form plants prefer.
3. Assimilation — plants take up nitrate; animals get nitrogen by eating.
4. Ammonification — decomposers return nitrogen from waste and dead matter to ammonium.
5. Denitrification — bacteria convert nitrate → N₂, sending it back to the air.

Notice how the loop closes: fixation pulls nitrogen out of the air, and denitrification returns it. Everything in between passes nitrogen through soil and living bodies — matter cycling once again.

🔄 FLIP CARD · TAP TO REVEAL
A field of clover grows lush and green without any fertilizer, and the soil around its roots ends up richer in nitrogen than before.
What is happening in those roots?
(Tap to flip.)
✅ THE ANSWER
Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the root nodules.
Clover is a legume. Rhizobium bacteria live in nodules on its roots and fix N₂ from the air into ammonium the plant can use — a mutualism in which the plant feeds the bacteria sugar. Because the bacteria fix more nitrogen than the plant uses, the leftover enriches the soil. This is why farmers plant legumes to restore worn-out fields — the bacteria, not the plant, do the fixing.

Say it in your own words in your notebook: plants can't use N₂ directly — nitrogen-fixing bacteria must convert it first. That single sentence is the most tested idea in the nitrogen cycle.

📚 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF SCIENCE · THE FARMERS WHO KNEW WITHOUT KNOWING WHY
Long before anyone had seen a bacterium, farmers knew that planting beans, peas, or clover somehow “fed” the soil. Roman writers advised rotating grain crops with legumes to keep fields productive, and the practice was followed for centuries without an explanation. The why arrived only in the 1880s, when the German scientists Hermann Hellriegel and Hermann Wilfarth showed that legumes thrive on atmospheric nitrogen while other plants cannot — and within a few years the bacteria in the root nodules, later named Rhizobium, were identified as the true fixers; the Dutch microbiologist Martinus Beijerinck grew them in pure culture in 1888. We now recognize the arrangement as a mutualism (Lesson 30.02): the plant supplies sugar, and the bacteria supply usable nitrogen. Here is the same pattern this course keeps meeting: a reliable practice discovered by patient observation, its mechanism explained only much later. The farmers were right; it simply took microbiology to reveal that the credit belonged to invisible partners living in the roots.
🦠 PART 3 · WHY THIS MATTERS FOR YOU

The nitrogen in your muscles came from a microbe

Trace it backward. The protein in your muscles contains nitrogen; you got it by eating; the food traces back to a plant; the plant assimilated nitrate from the soil; and that nitrate began as N₂ gas that a bacterium fixed. Every protein in every living body depends on microbes most people never think about. When you draw the cycle in VR-A this week, you are drawing the supply chain for your own body's building blocks.
