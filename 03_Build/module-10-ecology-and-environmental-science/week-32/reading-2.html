Reading 32.02
The Nitrogen Cycle: Surrounded by It, Unable to Use It
Anchor Reading · Fixation, Nitrification, Assimilation, Ammonification, Denitrification
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Here is a puzzle. Nitrogen is everywhere — it makes up about 78% of every breath you take. Living things need it desperately, because it is a building block of proteins and DNA. And yet plants surrounded by an ocean of nitrogen gas can starve for lack of it. This anchor reading for Lesson 32.02 explains why, and introduces the crew of microscopic workers — almost all of them bacteria — that make nitrogen usable and keep it moving through the living world. By the end you should be able to name the five main steps of the nitrogen cycle and say what happens at each.

The nitrogen paradox: N₂ is locked shut

The nitrogen in the air is nitrogen gas (N₂) — two nitrogen atoms held together by an extraordinarily strong triple bond. That bond is so tough to break that neither plants nor animals can use N₂ directly. This is the single most important idea in the whole lesson. You cannot get nitrogen simply by breathing it in, and a plant cannot get it simply by having air around its leaves. The gas must first be “fixed” — converted into a usable chemical form — before any organism can build it into proteins or DNA.

Almost every step that follows is carried out by bacteria. This is a striking payoff from Module 7: the “invisible” prokaryotes, so easy to overlook, turn out to run one of the most essential cycles on Earth. Without them, the biosphere would run out of usable nitrogen and grind to a halt.

Step 1 — Nitrogen fixation: unlocking the gas

Nitrogen fixation is the step that breaks the triple bond and converts N₂ into ammonia (NH₃) or ammonium (NH₄⁺) — forms life can actually use. The great fixers are nitrogen-fixing bacteria. Some live free in the soil; others, such as Rhizobium, live inside root nodules on the roots of legumes (beans, peas, clover). This is a mutualism you met with the plant microbiome: the plant feeds the bacteria sugar, and the bacteria hand the plant fixed nitrogen.

A smaller amount of nitrogen is fixed by lightning, whose enormous energy can split N₂ and let it combine with oxygen, forming compounds that rain down into the soil. But the living workhorses of fixation are the bacteria.

Step 2 — Nitrification: making nitrate

In nitrification, another team of soil bacteria converts ammonium (NH₄⁺) first into nitrite (NO₂⁻) and then into nitrate (NO₃⁻). This matters because nitrate is the form of nitrogen that plants take up most readily through their roots. Nitrification, in other words, turns fixed nitrogen into the version plants prefer to eat.

Step 3 — Assimilation: nitrogen enters living bodies

In assimilation, plants absorb nitrate (and some ammonium) from the soil through their roots and build the nitrogen into their own proteins and DNA. Animals cannot take nitrogen from the soil at all — they get theirs the only way they can: by eating plants or other animals. Every protein in your body traces its nitrogen back through your food to a plant that assimilated it from the soil.

Step 4 — Ammonification: decomposers give it back

When organisms produce waste or die, the nitrogen in their bodies is not lost. Decomposers — bacteria and fungi — break down the dead matter and waste in a step called ammonification, returning nitrogen to the soil as ammonium. That ammonium can then be nitrified and taken up by plants all over again. This is the recycling heart of the cycle: yesterday's fallen leaf becomes tomorrow's fertilizer.

Step 5 — Denitrification: closing the loop

Finally, a group of bacteria in low-oxygen soils and sediments carry out denitrification: they convert nitrate (NO₃⁻) back into nitrogen gas (N₂), which escapes into the atmosphere. This returns nitrogen to the air reservoir where the cycle began, closing the loop. Fix it out of the air, pass it through living things and the soil, and eventually send it back to the air — a complete circle, run almost entirely by bacteria.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Five steps, one loop: fixation unlocks the gas, nitrification makes nitrate, assimilation builds it into bodies, ammonification returns it to the soil, and denitrification sends it back to the air. Keep the master idea in view: matter cycles, and here bacteria are the workers who keep it moving. It is a humbling thought — the proteins that make up your muscles depend on microbes you will never see.
