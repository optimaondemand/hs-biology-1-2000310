**1. (Reading 32.03 — the water cycle; targets "water is used up")**
Which statement best describes how water moves through the water cycle?

- A) Water is used up by living things when they drink it and must be replaced by new water formed in the atmosphere
- B) Water moves only from the land to the ocean and never returns to the land, so continents slowly lose their water
- C) Water evaporates and is released by plants (transpiration), condenses into clouds, falls as precipitation, and returns as runoff or infiltration **✓**
- D) Water falls as precipitation, then condenses into clouds, then evaporates from the ground, and finally returns to the oceans as runoff

*Water is not used up; it cycles. The Sun's heat drives evaporation (and plants add water vapor by transpiration), vapor condenses into clouds, water falls as precipitation, and it returns to the sea or soaks into groundwater, ready to begin again. The order in choice D is reversed, and water does return to the land as precipitation.*

**2. (Reading 32.01 — the biological engine of the carbon cycle)**
Which pair of processes moves carbon into and out of living things, forming the heart of the carbon cycle?

- A) Cellular respiration removes CO₂ from the air; photosynthesis returns CO₂ to the air
- B) Photosynthesis removes CO₂ from the air; cellular respiration returns CO₂ to the air **✓**
- C) Both photosynthesis and cellular respiration remove CO₂ from the air and store it in sugar
- D) Neither process involves CO₂; carbon moves only through the decay of dead organisms

*Photosynthesis pulls CO₂ out of the atmosphere and locks its carbon into organic molecules (the great removal). Cellular respiration breaks those molecules down for energy and releases CO₂ back to the air (the great return). Together they are the biological engine of the carbon cycle.*

**3. (Reading 32.01 — fossil fuels)**
Why does burning fossil fuels add carbon dioxide to the atmosphere so quickly?

- A) Burning fossil fuels creates brand-new carbon atoms that did not exist on Earth before, adding extra mass to the air
- B) Fossil fuels are made mostly of pure oxygen, which is converted into carbon dioxide gas when it is heated
- C) Burning fossil fuels speeds up photosynthesis, which releases CO₂ into the air much faster than usual
- D) Fossil fuels hold carbon from ancient photosynthesis, and burning releases in a short time what took millions of years to store **✓**

*Fossil fuels — coal, oil, and natural gas — are the buried remains of organisms that pulled carbon from the air by photosynthesis long ago. That carbon took hundreds of millions of years to store. Burning returns it to the atmosphere in a few centuries — far faster than the cycle removes it. No new carbon is created; old carbon is released quickly.*

**4. (Reading 32.02 — organisms cannot use N₂ directly; the central misconception)**
The air is about 78% nitrogen gas (N₂). Why can't plants and animals simply use this nitrogen to build their proteins and DNA?

- A) N₂ has a very strong triple bond that plants and animals cannot break, so bacteria must first fix it **✓**
- B) The air contains very little nitrogen gas, so organisms must obtain all of their nitrogen from rocks
- C) Nitrogen gas is poisonous to plants and animals, so they have evolved to avoid absorbing it
- D) Plants absorb all the nitrogen gas before animals can reach it, leaving animals none for proteins

*The nitrogen in the air is N₂, held together by a triple bond so strong that neither plants nor animals can break it. Before any organism can use nitrogen, bacteria (nitrogen-fixing bacteria) — or lightning — must "fix" it into ammonia/ammonium. This is the single most important idea of the nitrogen cycle.*

**5. (Reading 32.02 — the roles of nitrogen-cycle bacteria)**
A field of clover grows well without fertilizer, and its soil ends up richer in usable nitrogen. What is happening in the clover's roots?

- A) The clover's roots are absorbing nitrogen gas directly from the air spaces in the soil
- B) The clover is making new nitrogen atoms by photosynthesis and releasing them into the soil
- C) Denitrifying bacteria around the roots are converting soil nitrate into ammonium the clover can use
- D) Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the root nodules are converting N₂ gas into a usable form **✓**

*Clover is a legume. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (such as Rhizobium) live in nodules on its roots and convert atmospheric N₂ into ammonium the plant can use — a mutualism in which the plant supplies sugar. Because the bacteria fix more than the plant uses, the soil is enriched. The bacteria, not the plant, do the fixing.*

**6. (Reading 32.02 — ordering the nitrogen cycle)**
Decomposers break down a dead animal and release its nitrogen back into the soil as ammonium. What is this step of the nitrogen cycle called?

- A) Nitrogen fixation
- B) Ammonification **✓**
- C) Denitrification
- D) Assimilation

*Ammonification is the step in which decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down waste and dead matter, returning nitrogen to the soil as ammonium. Fixation converts N₂ to ammonium; assimilation is uptake by plants; denitrification returns nitrate to the air as N₂.*

**7. (Reading 32.03 — phosphorus has no gas phase; targets the cycle contrast)**
How does the phosphorus cycle differ from the carbon and nitrogen cycles?

- A) Phosphorus has no significant gas phase, so it cycles through rock, soil, water, and sediment **✓**
- B) Phosphorus is the only one of the three with a major gas phase, so the atmosphere is its main reservoir
- C) Phosphorus is not used by living things, so it cycles only through rock and never enters organisms
- D) Phosphorus cycles faster than carbon and nitrogen because it moves quickly through the atmosphere

*Carbon (as CO₂) and nitrogen (as N₂) both have a gas phase, so the atmosphere is a major reservoir and they can move quickly. Phosphorus has essentially no gas phase — it is released by the weathering of rock and cycles through soil, water, organisms, and sediment. This makes its cycle slow and earthbound.*

**8. (Reading 32.03 — the limiting nutrient)**
In many ecosystems phosphorus is described as the "limiting nutrient." What does this mean?

- A) Phosphorus is toxic to most organisms, so its presence caps how many can survive there
- B) Phosphorus blocks the amount of sunlight an ecosystem receives, which limits photosynthesis
- C) Because phosphorus is often scarce, its shortage caps how much the ecosystem can grow **✓**
- D) Phosphorus is so abundant that it is never in short supply, so other factors limit growth

*Because phosphorus moves slowly and is not refilled from the air, it is often in short supply. A scarce nutrient becomes the limiting nutrient — the one whose shortage caps growth. Even with plenty of sunlight, CO₂, and water, growth stops when phosphorus runs out. This is also why a sudden flood of phosphorus (from fertilizer) can trigger explosive algal growth.*

**9. (Reading 32.04 — the eutrophication mechanism; targets "what removes the oxygen")**
Fertilizer runoff triggers an algal bloom in a lake; soon after, the fish suffocate in a "dead zone." What directly causes the water to lose its oxygen?

- A) The living algae use up all the dissolved oxygen through respiration while they are blooming
- B) Decomposers respire as they break down the mass of dead algae, using up the dissolved oxygen **✓**
- C) The fertilizer reacts chemically with the dissolved oxygen and destroys it as soon as it enters
- D) The extra nitrogen and phosphorus displace the dissolved oxygen by taking up space in the water

*In eutrophication, nutrient runoff frees algae from their nutrient limit, so they bloom explosively and then die. Decomposers (bacteria) consume the dead algae, and their respiration uses up the water's dissolved oxygen — creating a hypoxic dead zone in which fish and other animals suffocate. The key point: it is the decomposers, not the living algae, that strip out the oxygen.*
