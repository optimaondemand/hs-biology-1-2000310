Reading 32.01
The Carbon Cycle: The Same Atoms, Used Again and Again
Anchor Reading · Reservoirs, Photosynthesis and Respiration, Oceans, and Fossil Fuels
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Last week you learned that energy flows through an ecosystem in one direction — it enters as sunlight, passes up the food chain, and is lost as heat at every step, never to be reused. Matter behaves differently. The atoms that make up living things — carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus — are not lost. They are used, released, and used again, traveling in great loops called biogeochemical cycles (bio = life, geo = earth, chemical = the elements themselves). This anchor reading for Lesson 32.01 follows the most famous of these loops: the carbon cycle. By the end you should be able to trace a single carbon atom out of the air, into a living body, and back again.

The one idea to carry all week: matter cycles, energy flows

Hold these two words side by side. Energy flows: it makes a one-way trip from the Sun, through producers and consumers, and out into space as heat — which is exactly why every ecosystem needs a constant new supply of sunlight. Matter cycles: the Earth is very nearly a closed system for atoms, so the carbon in your breath today may once have been inside a dinosaur, a fern, or the air a Roman senator exhaled. Nothing new is created; the same finite pool of atoms is reshuffled endlessly among the air, the water, the rocks, and living things.

A cycle moves matter between reservoirs — the places where an element is stored. For carbon, the great reservoirs are the atmosphere (as carbon dioxide gas), living organisms (as the sugars, proteins, and fats of their bodies), the oceans (as dissolved carbon), and the rocks and fossil fuels underground. The cycle is simply the set of processes that move carbon from one reservoir to another.

The biological engine: photosynthesis and respiration

You already know the two reactions that drive this cycle — you studied them in Module 4. In photosynthesis, plants, algae, and some bacteria pull carbon dioxide out of the air and use the energy of sunlight to lock its carbon into organic molecules such as glucose. This is the great removal: carbon leaves the atmosphere and enters the living world. In cellular respiration, organisms break those molecules back down to release energy, and in doing so they exhale carbon dioxide right back into the air. This is the great return.

Photosynthesis and respiration are the beating heart of the carbon cycle — one breathing carbon in to the living world, the other breathing it back out. Every producer, every consumer, and every decomposer respires. When you exhale, you are personally returning carbon to the atmosphere that a plant had removed from it.

Decomposition and combustion: two more returns

Carbon does not stay locked in a body forever. When an organism dies, decomposers — bacteria and fungi — break down its remains, respiring as they feed and releasing the stored carbon back to the air and soil. Decomposition is the recycler that keeps carbon from being trapped in dead matter.

Combustion — burning — is another fast return. When wood, or any once-living fuel, burns, its carbon combines with oxygen and rushes back into the atmosphere as carbon dioxide. A forest fire, a campfire, and a car engine all do the same chemical thing: they take carbon that was stored in organic matter and put it back in the air.

The ocean: the planet's great carbon sponge

Look at any carbon-cycle diagram and one reservoir dwarfs the air: the ocean. Carbon dioxide dissolves directly into seawater, so the oceans absorb an enormous amount of it — far more than the atmosphere holds. Some of that dissolved carbon is taken up by marine algae for photosynthesis; some is used by shellfish and corals to build calcium-carbonate shells; and when those organisms die, their shells sink and can be buried in sediment, slowly forming rock over millions of years.

When carbon dioxide dissolves in water it forms carbonic acid, the same weak acid that puts the fizz in soda. This is worth remembering, because it connects to a concern you will meet later in the module: as the air holds more carbon dioxide, the oceans absorb more of it, and recent research suggests this is making seawater gradually more acidic — a change scientists are watching closely for its effects on shell-building life. We will treat that carefully when we reach human disruption; for now, simply note that the ocean is both a huge carbon store and a chemically active one.

Fossil fuels: ancient sunlight, locked underground

Here is one of the most beautiful connections in all of biology. Fossil fuels — coal, oil, and natural gas — are the buried remains of organisms that lived hundreds of millions of years ago. That means the carbon in a lump of coal is ancient photosynthetic carbon: it was pulled out of the air by living things long ago, stored in their bodies, buried before it could fully decompose, and pressed by heat and time into fuel. Every tank of gasoline is, quite literally, stored sunlight from the age of the dinosaurs and before.

In the natural cycle, this carbon would return to the air only over immense spans of time. When we burn fossil fuels, we undo that slow storage in an instant — taking carbon that took millions of years to bury and releasing it back to the atmosphere in a few centuries. The carbon cycle can move carbon in both directions; the trouble, as we will see in Lesson 32.04, comes from moving it in one direction far faster than the cycle moves it back.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

The carbon cycle is the clearest proof of this week's big idea: the atoms of life are used over and over. Photosynthesis removes carbon from the air; respiration, decomposition, and combustion return it; the oceans store vast amounts; and fossil fuels hold ancient carbon underground. Notice how tightly this leans on Module 4 — photosynthesis and respiration are not just cell reactions but the engine of a planet-sized loop. In the next lesson we follow a very different element, nitrogen, whose cycle depends not on green leaves but on the invisible bacteria you met in Module 7.
