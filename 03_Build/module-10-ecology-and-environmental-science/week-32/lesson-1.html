The Carbon Cycle: The Journey of a Single Atom
Lesson · Photosynthesis, Respiration, Oceans, and Fossil Fuels
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The carbon atom you just exhaled might have been inside a fern 300 million years ago. Where has it been — and where will it go next?
Last week's rule was that energy flows and is lost. This week's rule is its partner: matter cycles. Today you trace carbon around its great loop — out of the air by photosynthesis, back to the air by respiration, decomposition, and combustion, dissolved in the oceans, and locked underground as fossil fuels. This is Module 4 reappearing at planet scale.

🔄 PART 1 · MATTER CYCLES, ENERGY FLOWS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Last week you learned energy is lost as heat at every step of a food chain. Do you think the atoms in an ecosystem get “used up” the same way? (No wrong answer — just check in.)

Two words, side by side

Energy flows one way — in as sunlight, up the food chain, out as heat — which is why ecosystems need a constant new supply of sunlight. Matter cycles — the same atoms are used, released, and reused among the reservoirs where they are stored. These looping paths are called biogeochemical cycles. For carbon, the great reservoirs are the atmosphere (CO₂), living organisms, the oceans, and rocks and fossil fuels.

🌱 PART 2 · THE BIOLOGICAL ENGINE

In by photosynthesis, out three ways

The heart of the carbon cycle is a pair of reactions from Module 4. Photosynthesis pulls CO₂ out of the air and locks its carbon into organic molecules — the great removal. Carbon returns to the air three biological/physical ways: cellular respiration (every organism exhaling CO₂), decomposition (decomposers respiring as they break down the dead), and combustion (burning wood or fuel). The oceans also absorb enormous amounts of dissolved CO₂ — the planet's great carbon sponge — and dissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid.

📚 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF SCIENCE · WEIGHING THE AIR
The idea that an invisible gas could weave through living things was hard-won. In the 1770s the English clergyman Joseph Priestley found that a plant could “restore” air that a candle or a mouse had spoiled — the first hint that plants and animals exchange gases in opposite directions. The French chemist Antoine Lavoisier then showed that respiration is a kind of slow combustion, consuming the same life-giving gas (oxygen) that burning does and releasing “fixed air” (carbon dioxide). The Dutch physician Jan Ingenhousz added a key detail in 1779: plants restore the air only in the light, and only with their green parts. Later chemists and geologists, tracing carbon through burning, breathing, and the buried plant matter that became coal, fitted these pieces into a cycle. Piece by piece, from a candle under a jar to precise chemistry, scientists assembled the loop you can now draw in a single diagram — the carbon cycle. As so often in this course, patient observation came first, and the grand picture assembled slowly afterward.
⛏ PART 3 · ANCIENT SUNLIGHT UNDERGROUND

Fossil fuels and the direction of the cycle

Fossil fuels — coal, oil, natural gas — are the buried remains of organisms from hundreds of millions of years ago. Their carbon is ancient photosynthetic carbon: pulled from the air by long-dead life, buried before it could decompose, and pressed into fuel. A tank of gasoline is stored sunlight from a vanished world. In the natural cycle this carbon would return to the air only across immense time. Burning it returns that carbon fast — and the trouble in Lesson 32.04 is exactly that: moving carbon one way faster than the cycle moves it back.

The loop to remember: CO₂ in the air → photosynthesis locks it into organisms → respiration, decomposition, and combustion return it → oceans store vast amounts → some carbon is buried as fossil fuels → burning fuels returns ancient carbon to the air. You will build this exact loop, with labeled flows, in this week's VR-A.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

Carbon is never lost — it travels. Photosynthesis removes it from the air; respiration, decomposition, and combustion return it; the oceans store vast amounts; and fossil fuels hold ancient carbon underground.
