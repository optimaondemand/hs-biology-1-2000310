Silent Spring: When a Poison Climbs a Food Web
Lesson · DDT, Biomagnification, Eggshell Thinning & a CER Practice
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A pesticide sprayed on fields and marshes was, at that spot, far too dilute to harm a bird. So why did the eagles and falcons nearly disappear?
We open the final unit with a true story that gathers up almost everything you have learned about ecology. A chemical named DDT climbed the food web, growing more concentrated at every step, until it thinned the eggshells of the birds at the top. One scientist — Rachel Carson — read the evidence, built the argument, and changed the law of a nation. Today you will learn the mechanism (bioaccumulation and biomagnification), meet Carson, and then practice the CER argument yourself.

☣️ PART 1 · A POISON THAT WOULD NOT LEAVE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A pesticide is present in lake water at a very low, seemingly harmless concentration. Before reading on, which animal in the lake do you expect to carry the highest concentration in its body?

Two properties that made DDT dangerous

DDT is a synthetic pesticide that was sprayed widely from the 1940s on. Two properties turned a useful chemical into an ecological problem. It is persistent — it does not break down quickly, so it lingers for years. And it is fat-soluble — it dissolves into an organism's fatty tissue and stays there rather than washing out. When one organism keeps building up a stored toxin faster than it can shed it, we call that bioaccumulation.

The greater danger comes when that stored poison rides up a food web. Recall from earlier this module that only about 10% of energy passes from one trophic level to the next — energy is lost at each step. A persistent, fat-soluble toxin does the opposite: it is not lost. It is passed along with the meal and concentrated. This step-by-step rise in concentration up the trophic levels is biomagnification, and it is exactly why the animals at the top of the web are hit hardest — they inherit the accumulated poison of every level below.

📚 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF SCIENCE · RACHEL CARSON AND SILENT SPRING (1962)
Rachel Carson was a marine biologist and a rare kind of scientist — one who could also write beautifully. Reading scattered reports of dying songbirds and failing eagle and falcon nests, she assembled the evidence into a single careful argument and published it in 1962 as Silent Spring, imagining a spring morning with no birdsong left. She did not claim DDT was useless; she argued that releasing a persistent poison into a food web had consequences no one had measured, and that we were responsible for finding out. The chemical industry attacked her as hysterical and unqualified. She was, at the time, quietly dying of cancer, yet she testified before the United States Congress and held to her evidence. It held up. The United States banned most uses of DDT in 1972, and over the following decades bald eagle, osprey, and peregrine falcon populations recovered — a living measurement that her reasoning had been sound. Silent Spring is widely regarded as the birth of the modern environmental movement: a case of science communicated so clearly that it changed policy and saved species. Careful observation and evidence came first; the power to persuade followed — a pattern this whole course has kept meeting.
🔄 PART 2 · WHY THE EGGS FAILED

Test yourself before you flip

Read the question on the card, decide your answer, then tap to reveal.

🔄 FLIP CARD · TAP TO REVEAL
DDT did not poison adult eagles outright. Yet their populations still crashed, because almost no chicks were hatching.
What did the built-up DDT actually do to prevent the next generation?
(Tap to flip.)
✅ THE ANSWER
It thinned their eggshells.
The accumulated DDT interfered with how female raptors laid down calcium in their shells. The shells came out so thin that a parent cracked its own eggs simply by sitting on them to keep them warm. Few chicks hatched, and populations of bald eagles, ospreys, and peregrine falcons crashed — a reproductive failure, not a mass poisoning. The species at the top of the web paid the highest price because they carried the highest biomagnified dose.

Say it in your own words in your notebook: a fat-soluble, persistent toxin biomagnifies up the web, so top predators carry the most and, in raptors, that dose thinned eggshells until chicks stopped hatching.

✍️ PART 3 · IN-LESSON CER PRACTICE

Was Carson's “silent spring” claim scientifically sound?

Now put your reasoning to work. Below is a scaffold for building a short Claim–Evidence–Reasoning argument — the same skill you use in every module essay. Draft your answer to the question in your notebook, following the three steps.

🧠 CER SCAFFOLD · DRIVING QUESTION
“Using food webs and bioaccumulation, evaluate whether Carson's claim that DDT was causing a ‘silent spring’ was scientifically sound.”
1
CLAIM — a one-sentence answer
State your position clearly. Starter: “Carson's claim was scientifically sound — the mechanism of biomagnification explains the crash of top-predator birds.”
2
EVIDENCE — give at least two specific facts
Pull from this lesson. For example: DDT is persistent and fat-soluble, so it is stored, not shed; concentration rises at each trophic level (biomagnification), leaving top predators with the highest load; raptor eggshells thinned and populations crashed; and after the 1972 ban, those populations recovered.
3
REASONING — connect the evidence back to the claim
Explain why the evidence supports the claim. Because energy is lost up a web but a stored toxin is not, the poison concentrates upward; the birds at the top therefore received the heaviest dose, which explains why they were the ones failing to reproduce. The recovery after the ban is a natural experiment strongly supporting DDT as the cause — strong scientific reasoning, not a guess.
Note: This is practice for the CER skill — a short paragraph or three sentences in your notebook. It is not the graded Module 10 CER essay. Assignment 35.01 will ask you to write this practice up briefly.

Looking back: notice that this argument reuses the 10% energy rule and food web ideas from earlier this module, plus the fat-soluble molecule idea from Module 2 — the one long argument in action.
