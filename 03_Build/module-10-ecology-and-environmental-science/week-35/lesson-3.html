Ten modules can look like ten separate subjects. What if they are really one argument, told from the smallest scale to the largest?
Today introduces no new mechanism. Instead we step back far enough to see the whole year at once. This course has climbed a single staircase — atoms → molecules → cells → energy → DNA → evolution → diversity → plants → the body → ecosystems — each step resting on the one below. You will trace that spine, make the bridges between units explicit, and leave with a map for next week's Final.

🧱 PART 1 · THE STAIRCASE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Looking back over the whole year, what is the single biggest idea you would use to tie biology together? Pick the answer closest to your thinking.

Microscopic to macroscopic, one step at a time

Atoms and water (Module 2) built the macromolecules of life. Those built cells and membranes (Module 3). Cells learned to capture and release energy (Module 4). To copy themselves they needed DNA, cell division, and inheritance (Module 5). Inherited variation fed natural selection and common descent (Module 6), producing the ordered diversity of life (Module 7). Among that diversity, plants became the photosynthetic foundation (Module 8), while the human body integrates all these systems at once (Module 9). Finally, every organism lives inside ecosystems, joined in webs of energy and matter (Module 10).

The point is not the list. It is that each level depends on the one before — nothing in biology stands alone.

🔗 PART 2 · THREE BRIDGES BETWEEN UNITS

The connections are the architecture

Three bridges make the unity concrete:

Bridge 1 — a carbon atom. A chloroplast builds it into sugar by photosynthesis (M3, M4, M8); an animal's mitochondria break the sugar down in respiration (M4), releasing carbon dioxide back into the carbon cycle (M10), ready for another leaf. And both organelles were once free-living bacteria (endosymbiosis, M6).
Bridge 2 — a DNA molecule. It is the molecule of inheritance (M5); its mutations are the variation natural selection acts on (M6); and its shared sequences let us classify life and draw the tree (M7). One molecule, three units.
Bridge 3 — form follows function. Water's bent shape (M2), a membrane's structure (M3), a leaf's layers (M8), a food-web pyramid (M10) — the same habit at every scale: ask what is it and what does it do?
