Reading 35.01
Silent Spring: How a Poison Climbed a Food Web
Anchor Reading · DDT, Bioaccumulation, Biomagnification, and a Book That Changed the Law
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
This is the last unit of the year, and we begin it with a story that pulls together almost everything you have learned about ecology. In the middle of the twentieth century, a chemical meant to kill insects began, quietly, to kill birds — and one careful scientist figured out why. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 35.01. By the end you should be able to explain what DDT is, what bioaccumulation and biomagnification mean, why the animals at the top of a food web are hit hardest, and how Rachel Carson turned that evidence into a book that changed a nation's law.

A chemical that would not leave

DDT is a synthetic pesticide first used widely in the 1940s. It worked spectacularly well against mosquitoes and crop pests, and for a time it seemed like a miracle. But DDT has two properties that turned it into a slow disaster. First, it is persistent — it does not break down quickly, so it lingers in soil and water for years. Second, it is fat-soluble: it dissolves into the fatty tissues of living things rather than washing out in urine or water. An organism that takes in DDT tends to store it and keep it.

When a single organism stores a chemical in its tissues over time, we call it bioaccumulation — the amount builds up inside one body faster than the body can get rid of it. That alone would be a problem. But the truly dangerous part happens when this stored poison moves through a food web — the same energy-and-matter webs you studied earlier in this module.

Biomagnification: the poison concentrates upward

Recall from earlier in this module that only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level passes to the next, which is why food chains are short and top predators are few. A persistent, fat-soluble toxin behaves in the opposite way from energy: it does not get lost at each step. Instead it moves up with the meal and stays.

Picture a lake. DDT washes in at a low concentration and is absorbed by algae (the producers). Tiny zooplankton eat enormous numbers of algae, and the DDT from all of them collects in each zooplankton's fat. Small fish eat many zooplankton and gather all of their DDT. Large fish eat many small fish. And a fish-eating bird — an osprey, a bald eagle, a peregrine falcon — eats many large fish across its whole life. At each step, the predator takes in the combined poison of everything it eats. This step-by-step increase in the concentration of a stored toxin as it moves up trophic levels is called biomagnification.

The result is stark: a concentration far too low to matter in the water can become thousands of times higher in the body of the top predator. This is the key idea to carry away — the animals at the top of the food web are hurt worst, not because they meet more poison directly, but because they inherit the accumulated poison of every level beneath them.

Thin shells and empty nests

In predatory birds, the built-up DDT interfered with how the females laid down calcium in their eggshells. The shells came out dangerously thin — so thin that a parent bird cracked its own eggs simply by sitting on them to keep them warm. Few chicks hatched. Across the 1950s and 1960s, populations of bald eagles, ospreys, and peregrine falcons crashed; in some regions these birds nearly vanished.

Notice how many threads of this course meet here: a molecule's chemistry (fat-solubility), a physiological process (eggshell formation), the 10% rule of energy transfer, and the structure of a food web all combine to explain a single observable fact — a spring with fewer birds singing.

The woman who connected the dots

Rachel Carson was a marine biologist and a gifted writer. Reading reports of dying songbirds and failing raptor nests, she assembled the scattered evidence into a single, careful argument and published it in 1962 as Silent Spring — a title that imagined a spring morning with no birdsong at all. She did not claim DDT was useless; she argued that a persistent poison released into a food web has consequences no one had measured, and that we had a responsibility to find out before spreading it everywhere.

The chemical industry attacked her fiercely, calling her hysterical and unqualified. She was, at the time, quietly dying of cancer, yet she testified before the United States Congress and held to her evidence. It held up. In 1972 the United States banned most uses of DDT. In the decades that followed, bald eagle, osprey, and peregrine falcon populations recovered — a living measurement that her reasoning had been sound.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

This one episode is a model of the whole course. Careful observation (missing birds) led to a testable claim, supported by evidence (rising DDT levels up the web, thinning shells) and tied together by reasoning (biomagnification of a fat-soluble, persistent toxin). It also shows why this module ends the year on the virtue of responsibility: we are powerful enough to move a chemical through every living thing in a lake, which means we are responsible for understanding what we release. In the lesson, you will practice building exactly this argument in claim–evidence–reasoning form.
