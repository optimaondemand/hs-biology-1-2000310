**1. (Reading 35.01 — biomagnification; why top predators are hit hardest)**
DDT washes into a lake at a very low concentration, yet fish-eating birds such as ospreys end up with by far the highest concentration in their bodies. Why?

- A) The birds drink far more lake water than any other animal
- B) DDT is attracted to feathers, so only birds absorb it
- C) The birds spray the chemical directly onto their nests
- D) A persistent, fat-soluble toxin is not lost between trophic levels but concentrates upward, so the top predator inherits the accumulated toxin of everything below it **✓**

*This is biomagnification. Unlike energy, which is mostly lost at each step of a food web, a persistent, fat-soluble toxin is stored and passed along with the meal. A top predator eats many prey across its life, so it accumulates the combined toxin of every level beneath it — the highest concentration of all.*

**2. (Reading 35.01 — eggshell thinning; targets "DDT poisoned adults directly")**
DDT did not usually kill adult raptors outright, yet their populations still crashed. What was the main reason?

- A) The built-up DDT interfered with calcium in the females' eggshells, making shells so thin that few chicks hatched **✓**
- B) DDT made the adult birds too heavy to fly
- C) DDT turned the birds' feathers a color that attracted predators
- D) The adults simply stopped eating fish

*The accumulated DDT disrupted how female raptors laid down calcium in their eggshells. The shells came out dangerously thin, so parents cracked their own eggs while incubating them. The crash was a reproductive failure driven by thin shells, not a mass poisoning of adults.*

**3. (Reading 35.01 — bioaccumulation vs. energy transfer)**
A toxin is described as "persistent and fat-soluble." How does such a toxin behave in a food web compared with energy?

- A) Both the toxin and energy are lost at each step
- B) Energy is lost at each step (about 90%), but the stored toxin is not lost — it concentrates as it moves up **✓**
- C) Both the toxin and energy increase at each step
- D) The toxin is lost at each step, but energy increases

*Energy transfer is inefficient: only about 10% passes to the next trophic level, so energy is steadily lost upward. A persistent, fat-soluble toxin does the opposite — because it is stored in fat and does not break down, it is passed along and concentrated, so it increases up the web. That contrast is the heart of biomagnification.*

**4. (Reading 35.02 — ecosystem service categories; regulating)**
A coastal wetland filters pollutants out of water and buffers a town from storm surge. These benefits are best classified as which category of ecosystem service?

- A) Provisioning services
- B) Cultural services
- C) Regulating services **✓**
- D) Supporting services

*Regulating services are the ways ecosystems keep natural processes steady and safe — water purification, flood and storm control, climate regulation, and pollination. Filtering water and buffering storm surge are classic regulating services. Provisioning would be a harvested good; cultural would be recreation or beauty; supporting would be a slow foundation like nutrient cycling.*

**5. (Reading 35.02 — ecosystem service categories; supporting vs. others)**
Nutrient cycling and soil formation are called *supporting* services rather than provisioning or cultural services. Why?

- A) Because they are the physical goods people harvest and carry home
- B) Because they are the slow, underlying processes that make the other services possible **✓**
- C) Because they provide recreation and beauty
- D) Because they only occur in the ocean

*Supporting services are the foundational processes — nutrient cycling, soil formation, primary production — on which every other service depends. You cannot harvest a crop (provisioning) or filter water (regulating) unless soil forms and nutrients cycle first. They are the ecological equivalent of a foundation beneath a building.*

**6. (Reading 35.02 — definition of sustainability)**
Which statement best defines sustainability?

- A) Using as many natural resources as quickly as possible to help the present economy
- B) Leaving nature completely untouched and harvesting nothing at all
- C) Meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs **✓**
- D) Replacing all natural ecosystems with managed farmland

*Sustainability is fundamentally about time: it means using resources in a way that does not rob future generations. A fishery is sustainable if we catch no faster than the fish reproduce. It is neither reckless maximum use (A) nor total non-use (B) — it is use that can continue indefinitely.*

**7. (Reading 35.04 — humans as both members and shapers)**
Biology supports the view that humans are *both* members and shapers of ecosystems. Which pair of statements best captures this dual role?

- A) We stand entirely outside nature; and we cannot affect it
- B) We are heterotrophs whose carbon cycles like any organism's (member); and we can move chemicals through whole food webs and alter the climate (shaper) **✓**
- C) We are producers that make our own food; and we never change our surroundings
- D) We are immune to biomagnification; and we only ever restore ecosystems, never harm them

*Humans are members of the living world — heterotrophs in food webs, our carbon riding the carbon cycle, and not exempt from biomagnification. We are also uniquely powerful shapers, able to move toxins through webs (as with DDT), change the atmosphere, and both damage and restore ecosystems. This dual role is exactly why the module's virtue is responsibility.*

**8. (Cumulative synthesis — trace a carbon atom; Modules 4, 8, 10)**
Follow one carbon atom. It leaves the air as carbon dioxide, becomes part of a sugar in a leaf, is eaten by an animal, and later returns to the air. Which sequence of processes does it pass through?

- A) Photosynthesis builds it into sugar, then cellular respiration breaks the sugar down and releases it back to the air as part of the carbon cycle **✓**
- B) Cellular respiration builds it into sugar, then photosynthesis releases it to the air
- C) It is built into DNA by photosynthesis, then released by digestion
- D) It stays locked in the sugar forever and never returns to the air

*In the leaf's chloroplast, photosynthesis captures CO₂ and builds the carbon into sugar. When an animal eats the plant, its mitochondria break that sugar down in cellular respiration, releasing the carbon again as CO₂ — returning it to the carbon cycle, ready to be captured by another leaf. One atom threads through cells, energy pathways, and a global cycle.*

**9. (Cumulative synthesis — the DNA thread; Modules 5, 6, 7)**
The same molecule, DNA, ties together three units of the course. Which statement correctly matches DNA's role in genetics, evolution, and classification?

- A) DNA carries energy in genetics, stores fat in evolution, and builds walls in classification
- B) DNA is inherited and copied (genetics); its mutations are the variation natural selection acts on (evolution); and its shared sequences reveal how organisms are related (classification) **✓**
- C) DNA is important only in genetics and has nothing to do with evolution or classification
- D) DNA is made by ribosomes in genetics, by chloroplasts in evolution, and by mitochondria in classification

*DNA is one molecule doing one job across time. In genetics it is inherited and copied, with occasional mutations. In evolution those mutations are the raw variation that natural selection acts on, and shared sequences are strong evidence of common descent. In classification, comparing shared DNA sequences reveals relationships and lets us build the tree of life — the reason molecular data can reveal the domains of life.*
