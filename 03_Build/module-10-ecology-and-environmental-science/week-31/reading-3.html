📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
You now know that energy thins as it climbs a food web. Today you meet two consequences of that structure. First, some poisons do the opposite of energy — they grow more concentrated at each higher level, a process called biomagnification. Second, you will meet keystone species and trophic cascades — the reason a single predator can hold an entire web in balance. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 31.03, and it sets up the wolf story in the next lesson.

Biomagnification: poison that climbs the levels

Biomagnification (also called biological magnification) is the increasing concentration of a persistent toxin at each higher trophic level. It happens with substances that the body cannot break down or excrete — they are stored, usually in fat, rather than removed. Two classic examples are the pesticide DDT and the metal mercury (in its dangerous form, methylmercury).

The logic follows directly from the 10% rule. A tiny amount of toxin in each producer is not diluted as it moves up — because a consumer must eat many organisms from the level below to get enough energy, and the toxin from every one of those meals stays in its body. Eat a thousand contaminated fish over a lifetime and you carry the toxin of a thousand fish. So the poison becomes most concentrated in the top predators, which eat the most from the levels below them.

This is why top predators — ospreys, eagles, large fish, and humans who eat them — are hit hardest by pollution that seems harmless in trace amounts at the bottom. You will study the most famous case, DDT and the thinning of eagle eggshells, in Week 35 with Rachel Carson. For now, hold the pattern: toxins concentrate upward; the animals at the top pay the highest price.

Keystone species: small in number, huge in effect

The name comes from architecture: the keystone is the wedge-shaped stone at the top of an arch that holds all the others in place — remove it and the arch collapses. A keystone species is one whose effect on its community is disproportionately large relative to its abundance. It is not necessarily common or big; it is influential. Take it away, and the whole community reorganizes, often losing much of its diversity.

Two well-studied examples: sea otters eat sea urchins; where otters are removed, urchins explode in number and graze the kelp forests down to barren rock, taking countless other species with them. And the sea star Pisaster, in the experiments that first defined the idea, keeps mussels from crowding out every other species on a rocky shore — remove the sea star and the mussels take over, and diversity crashes. In both cases a single predator, never very numerous, is holding the community open for everyone else. (Notice the deep link to Module 6: an organism's ecological role is shaped by the pressures of evolution and natural selection.)

Trophic cascades: ripples down the web

A trophic cascade is what happens when a change at the top of a food web ripples downward through the levels below. Remove a top predator and its prey multiplies; that swollen prey population then over-consumes its food, and the effect passes down level by level, often reshaping the plants and even the physical landscape.

Add the predator back and the cascade can run in reverse: prey numbers and behavior shift, the plants they fed on recover, and the species that depend on those plants return. This is exactly why keystone predators matter so much — their presence or absence does not stay local; it cascades through the entire community. The clearest real-world example of a cascade is the story you will read next: the wolves of Yellowstone.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

A food web is not a loose collection of independent creatures; it is a tightly linked system in which a trace of poison can climb to a lethal dose and the loss of one modest predator can unravel an entire community. Both ideas — biomagnification and the keystone/cascade — are arguments for stewardship: what we release into an ecosystem, and what we remove from it, echoes far beyond where it began. Next lesson turns that principle into a documented case: wolves returning to Yellowstone.
