Reading 31.04
The Wolves of Yellowstone: A Trophic Cascade in the Wild
Anchor Reading · A Documented Keystone Predator and Its Cascade
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Yesterday's ideas — keystone species and trophic cascade — can sound abstract. Today they become a real place with a real history. The gray wolves of Yellowstone National Park were wiped out, then brought back seventy years later, and scientists have been watching what happened ever since. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 31.04, and it is the concrete payoff of everything you learned this week.

A predator removed

Gray wolves were once common across the American West. But through the late 1800s and early 1900s they were hunted, trapped, and poisoned as threats to livestock, and by the 1920s they had been exterminated from Yellowstone. For about seventy years, the park had no wolves.

With the top predator gone, the elk — the wolves' main prey — grew abundant and, just as important, grew bold. Nothing was hunting them, so they lingered wherever the food was best, including the open valleys and streambanks. There they browsed young willow, aspen, and cottonwood heavily, and in many places those young trees could not grow tall. The web had lost its keystone, and the effects were rippling down.

A predator returned

In 1995, after long debate, gray wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone. Their return set off a trophic cascade — the reverse of the one their absence had caused. The wolves preyed on elk, and elk numbers fell in places; but researchers noticed something subtler and arguably more important. The elk began to change their behavior, avoiding the exposed valleys and streambanks where wolves could easily catch them.

Relieved of constant browsing, young willow, aspen, and cottonwood recovered in some of those areas. The taller vegetation brought other changes: more beavers (which need willow to build and feed), more songbirds nesting in the growing trees, and more stabilized streambanks where roots held the soil. One returning predator had rippled all the way down to the plants, the animals that depend on them, and even the shape of the streams.

A true story — told carefully

The Yellowstone cascade is one of the best-documented examples of a keystone predator in the world, and the broad pattern — wolves back, elk behavior shifting, some vegetation recovering — is well supported. But good science stays honest about its limits. Scientists continue to study and debate the magnitude of the downstream effects: how much of the vegetation recovery is due to wolves versus other factors (climate, drought, bears, and cougars, changing elk numbers for other reasons). The recovery has also been uneven, strong in some valleys and weak in others.

You may have heard the dramatic claim that “wolves changed the course of the rivers.” It makes a wonderful story, but it overstates what the evidence firmly shows. The responsible summary is this: the wolves' return triggered a real, measurable cascade whose full reach scientists are still working out. That is not a weakness of the story — it is what careful ecology looks like.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Yellowstone shows the week's whole argument in one landscape: a keystone species whose removal and return sent a trophic cascade rippling through a food web from predator to plant. It is also a lesson in responsibility — the theme of this module. What we remove from an ecosystem, and what we choose to restore, changes far more than the single species we were thinking about. Stewardship means reasoning about the whole web, not just one strand of it.
