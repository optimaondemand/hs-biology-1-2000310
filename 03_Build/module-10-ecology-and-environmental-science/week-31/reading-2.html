Reading 31.02
The 10% Rule: Why Energy Flows and Is Never Recycled
Anchor Reading · Trophic Levels, Energy Pyramids, the One-Way Flow of Energy
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Yesterday you learned who eats whom. Today you learn how much gets through. Energy does not pass up a food chain untouched — most of it is lost at every step. This reading explains trophic levels, the energy pyramid, and the famous 10% rule, and it settles the single most important — and most misunderstood — idea in this unit: energy flows one way and is ultimately lost as heat, while matter cycles. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 31.02.

Trophic levels: the rungs of the ladder

A trophic level is a feeding position in a food chain (from the Greek trophe, “nourishment”). Producers occupy the first trophic level. Primary consumers — the herbivores — are the second. Secondary consumers are the third, tertiary consumers the fourth, and so on. Each level eats the one below it and, in turn, may be eaten by the one above.

The question ecologists ask is deceptively simple: when energy is passed from one trophic level to the next, how much of it makes the trip? The answer reshapes how you see every ecosystem on Earth.

The 10% rule: most energy is lost

On average, only about 10% of the energy stored at one trophic level is captured and stored in the next level up. The other ~90% is lost — used to power the organism's own life processes (moving, growing, staying warm) and radiated away as heat, plus the portions never eaten or never digested. This is the 10% rule.

Put numbers on it. If the producers in a meadow store 10,000 units of energy, the herbivores that eat them capture only about 1,000. The carnivores that eat the herbivores get about 100. The predators at the fourth level get about 10. Each step upward keeps only a tenth of what the step below had.

This is why food chains are short — rarely more than four or five links. After a few steps there is simply not enough energy left to support another level. It is also why top predators are few: it takes an enormous base of producers to feed a single hawk or shark. The energy pyramid is wide at the bottom because everything above rests on that broad foundation of captured sunlight.

The energy pyramid: a picture of loss

Draw the energy at each level as a stacked block and you get an energy pyramid: a broad base of producers, a smaller band of primary consumers, a smaller one still of secondary consumers, and a narrow cap of top predators. Each band is about a tenth the size of the one beneath it. The pyramid narrows precisely because ~90% of the energy is lost at every step. The shape is the 10% rule made visible.

The pyramid also explains a practical fact of feeding the world: eating lower on the pyramid (plants) delivers far more of the Sun's captured energy to you than eating high on it (meat), because fewer 90% losses stand between you and the producers.

The idea to get exactly right: energy flows, matter cycles

Here is the point most students get wrong — so read it slowly. Energy is not recycled. It enters an ecosystem as sunlight, flows one direction up the trophic levels, and is steadily lost as heat until none is left in usable form. Heat cannot be gathered back up and fed to a plant. That is why the Sun must keep shining: an ecosystem needs a constant new supply of energy because the energy it already has is always leaking away.

Matter behaves the opposite way. The atoms of carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus are not lost — decomposers (from yesterday's reading) return them to the soil, where producers take them up again. The same atoms are used over and over. So the honest summary of every ecosystem is this: energy flows through and is lost; matter cycles round and is reused. Confusing the two is the classic exam mistake — do not make it.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

The 10% rule quietly governs the size and shape of every ecosystem: how long food chains can be, how many predators the land can hold, and how much a small change at the bottom matters at the top. Hold onto the pyramid and the one-way flow of energy — you will need both in the next lesson, where a trace of poison at the bottom of a web turns deadly by the time it reaches the animals at the top.
