A hawk, a mushroom, and a blade of grass are all in the same story. What role does each one play in moving energy through a living place?

🌱 PART 1 · THE THREE ROLES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of the last meal you ate. Trace it back one step at a time — where did that energy first enter the living world?

Producers, consumers, decomposers

Producers (autotrophs) make their own food. On land and in sunlit water they are the photosynthesizers — plants, algae, cyanobacteria — and they form the base of the ecosystem, the entry point for the Sun's energy (this is the photosynthesis of Module 4). Consumers (heterotrophs) cannot make food and must eat others: primary consumers are herbivores that eat producers; secondary and tertiary consumers are carnivores that eat other consumers; omnivores eat both. Decomposers — mostly bacteria and fungi (the kingdoms of Module 7) — break down the dead and return the matter to the soil, so producers can use it again.

📚 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF SCIENCE · CHARLES ELTON AND THE INVENTION OF THE FOOD WEB
For most of history, naturalists catalogued animals one species at a time — what a creature looked like, where it lived. The young English ecologist Charles Elton asked a different question: not what is this animal? but what does it do, and who does it depend on? In his 1927 book Animal Ecology, Elton gave ecology some of its most important tools. He described food chains and food cycles (what we now call food webs), showing that the animals of a place are bound together by who eats whom. He introduced the idea of an ecological niche — an organism's “job” or role in the community, not just its address. And he noticed that when you count organisms at each feeding level, you get a pyramid of numbers: many small organisms at the bottom, few large ones at the top — an early glimpse of the energy pyramid you will study tomorrow. With these ideas Elton helped turn the study of animals into a quantitative science of relationships. Much of the vocabulary in this week's lessons is his.
🔄 PART 2 · CHAINS AND WEBS

Test yourself before you flip

A food chain is a single path; a food web is many chains interconnected. Read the card, decide your answer, then tap to reveal.

🔄 FLIP CARD · TAP TO REVEAL
A disease wipes out the frogs in a pond. In a single food chain (algae → insect → frog → heron), the heron is cut off from its food.
Why does a real food web handle this loss so much better?
(Tap to flip.)
✅ THE ANSWER
Because a web gives every eater more than one option.
In a food web the heron also eats fish, snakes, and small mammals, so losing frogs does not starve it — it shifts to other prey. In a lone chain there is no alternative, so a single loss cuts everything above it. The many cross-links of a web let the system absorb a loss and keep functioning. More connections means more stability.

Say it in your own words in your notebook: a food web is more realistic and more stable than a single food chain because most organisms have several food sources and several predators.

🔗 PART 3 · MATTER COMES BACK AROUND

Why decomposers matter more than they look

It is easy to picture energy climbing from grass to grasshopper to hawk and forget where it all goes when things die. Decomposers close the loop. By breaking down dead producers and consumers, bacteria and fungi release nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrients back into the soil, where producers take them up again. Without them, an ecosystem would bury itself in the dead and eventually run out of the raw materials of life.

The idea to carry into tomorrow: decomposers hint at a distinction we will make sharp next lesson — matter cycles (atoms are reused), but energy flows one way and is lost as heat. You will build both a food web and an energy pyramid in this week's VR-A.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

Every ecosystem runs on three roles: producers capture the Sun's energy, consumers pass it along, and decomposers return the matter. Draw the connections and you have a food web — a map of dependence that is stronger for being tangled. Tomorrow we ask the harder question: as energy climbs that web, how much actually survives each step? The answer is the 10% rule, and it governs the shape of the living world.
