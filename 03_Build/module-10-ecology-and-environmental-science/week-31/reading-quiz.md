**1. (Reading 31.02 — energy flow vs. matter cycling; the central misconception)**
Which statement correctly describes how energy and matter move through an ecosystem?

- A) Both energy and matter are recycled through the ecosystem and used over and over again
- B) Energy flows one way and is ultimately lost as heat, while matter cycles and is reused **✓**
- C) Energy is recycled by decomposers, while matter flows one way and is lost as heat
- D) Energy and matter both flow one way through the ecosystem and are lost as heat

*This is the key idea of the unit. Energy enters as sunlight, flows one direction up the trophic levels, and is steadily lost as heat that cannot be reused — that is why the Sun must keep supplying more. Matter (atoms of carbon, nitrogen, and so on) is different: decomposers return it to the soil and producers take it up again, so it cycles. Energy flows and is lost; matter cycles.*

**2. (Reading 31.02 — the 10% rule; calculation)**
The producers in a field store 20,000 units of energy. Using the 10% rule, about how much energy is available to the secondary consumers (the third trophic level)?

- A) 20,000 units
- B) 18,000 units
- C) 2,000 units
- D) 200 units **✓**

*Only about 10% of the energy at one level is stored in the next. From 20,000 units in the producers, the primary consumers get about 2,000 (10% of 20,000), and the secondary consumers get about 200 (10% of 2,000). Each step keeps roughly a tenth; the other ~90% is lost, mostly as heat.*

**3. (Reading 31.02 — why food chains are short)**
Why do most food chains have only four or five links, with very few top predators?

- A) About 90% of the energy is lost at each step, so too little remains to support more levels **✓**
- B) Predators choose not to hunt beyond the fourth level because larger prey are too dangerous
- C) There are not enough kinds of animals in most ecosystems to build chains any longer
- D) Energy increases at each higher level, so the top becomes crowded with too many predators

*Because only ~10% of energy passes to each higher level, the energy available shrinks dramatically with each step (for example 10,000 → 1,000 → 100 → 10). After four or five links there is simply not enough energy left to support another level, which is also why an ecosystem can support only a few top predators.*

**4. (Reading 31.01 — producer vs. consumer vs. decomposer roles)**
In a forest, a mushroom growing on a dead log breaks the log down into simple nutrients that return to the soil. What role is the mushroom playing?

- A) Producer, because it makes its own food from sunlight using chlorophyll
- B) Primary consumer, because it feeds on living plants and grasses
- C) Decomposer, because it breaks down dead material and recycles matter **✓**
- D) Secondary consumer, because it eats primary consumers for energy

*Decomposers — mainly fungi (like the mushroom) and bacteria — break down dead organisms and waste, releasing the matter locked inside back into the soil for producers to use again. A producer makes its own food by photosynthesis; a consumer eats other organisms. The mushroom is decomposing, so it is a decomposer.*

**5. (Reading 31.01 — food web more stable than a single chain)**
Why is a food web generally more stable than a single food chain?

- A) A food web contains fewer species than a single chain, so there is less that can go wrong when one is lost
- B) Most organisms in a web have several food sources, so predators can shift to other prey if one is lost **✓**
- C) A food web has no producers at its base, so it does not depend on the Sun for its energy supply
- D) Energy is not lost as heat in a web, because organisms feed at several levels and reuse it

*In a single chain, losing one species cuts off everything above it. In a food web, most organisms feed at several points and are eaten by several others, so the loss of one species can be absorbed as predators switch to other prey. The many cross-links make the web more realistic and more stable than a lone chain.*

**6. (Reading 31.03 — biomagnification; targets "where is the toxin worst")**
A persistent pesticide is present at a very low level in the algae of a lake. In the food chain algae → small fish → large fish → osprey, where will the pesticide be MOST concentrated, and why?

- A) In the osprey, because it eats many contaminated fish and the toxin accumulates up the levels **✓**
- B) In the algae, because that is where the pesticide enters and the base supports everything above
- C) In the small fish, because they are the most numerous animals and absorb the toxin fastest
- D) Equally across all four levels, because the toxin spreads evenly as organisms eat one another

*This is biomagnification. A persistent toxin is not broken down or excreted, so it stays in the body. Because each consumer eats many organisms from the level below, the toxin piles up and becomes more concentrated at each higher level. The top predator — the osprey — eats the most contaminated prey and accumulates the highest concentration. (Note this is the reverse of the energy pyramid, where energy is greatest at the bottom.)*

**7. (Reading 31.03 — keystone species)**
Sea otters are not very numerous, yet when they are removed from a kelp forest, sea urchins multiply and graze the kelp down to bare rock, and many other species disappear. This makes the sea otter a good example of a —

- A) producer, because it supports the kelp forest and the species that depend on the kelp
- B) decomposer, because it recycles nutrients from dead urchins back into the water
- C) primary consumer, because it feeds directly on kelp and so removes plant material
- D) keystone species, because its effect on the community is far larger than its numbers suggest **✓**

*A keystone species is one whose effect on its community is disproportionately large relative to its abundance — like the keystone holding an arch together. Sea otters are not abundant, but by eating urchins they keep the kelp forest and all the species it supports intact. Remove them and the whole community reorganizes and loses diversity.*

**8. (Reading 31.03 / 31.04 — trophic cascade)**
What is a trophic cascade?

- A) The steady loss of energy as heat at each trophic level, leaving less for each higher consumer
- B) The buildup of a persistent toxin in the tissues of organisms as it moves up the food chain
- C) A change at the top of a food web, such as losing a top predator, that ripples down through lower levels **✓**
- D) The one-way flow of water from land to the ocean through a watershed during heavy rainfall

*A trophic cascade occurs when a change at the top of a food web spreads downward: remove a top predator and its prey multiplies, that prey over-consumes its own food, and the effect passes down level by level. Adding the predator back can run the cascade in reverse. (Losing energy as heat is the 10% rule; a toxin building up is biomagnification.)*

**9. (Reading 31.04 — the Yellowstone wolves case study)**
After gray wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone in 1995, which sequence best describes the trophic cascade that followed?

- A) Wolves returned → elk numbers fell and elk avoided streambanks → willow and aspen recovered → beavers and songbirds increased **✓**
- B) Wolves returned → elk numbers rose and elk crowded streambanks → willow and aspen declined → beavers and songbirds decreased
- C) Wolves returned → willow and aspen died off → elk numbers rose → streambanks eroded and beavers declined
- D) Wolves returned → elk numbers stayed constant → willow and aspen were unchanged → no other species responded

*With wolves back, elk numbers fell in places and, importantly, elk avoided the exposed streambanks where wolves could catch them. Relieved of heavy browsing, young willow, aspen, and cottonwood recovered in some areas, which brought more beavers, songbirds, and stabilized streambanks. It is a well-documented cascade, though scientists still debate the magnitude of some downstream effects.*
