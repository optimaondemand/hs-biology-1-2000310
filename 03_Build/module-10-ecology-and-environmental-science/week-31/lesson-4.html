🐺 PART 1 · REMOVED, THEN RETURNED

Seventy years without wolves

Gray wolves were hunted and poisoned out of Yellowstone by the 1920s. With the top predator gone, elk grew abundant and bold, lingering in the open valleys and streambanks and browsing young willow, aspen, and cottonwood so heavily that in many places the trees could not grow tall. In 1995, wolves were reintroduced. Elk numbers fell in places, but the subtler change mattered more: elk began avoiding the exposed streambanks where wolves could catch them. Relieved of constant browsing, the willows and aspens recovered in some areas — and that recovery rippled outward to beavers (which need willow), songbirds (nesting in taller trees), and more stabilized streambanks.

Step 1 — Wolves return. What is the direct effect on the elk?
Step 2 — With elk browsing the streambanks less, what happens to the young willow, aspen, and cottonwood?
Step 3 — As the streambank trees recover, which downstream effects were observed?

That is a trophic cascade: one predator at the top, rippling all the way down to plants, beavers, birds, and the shape of the streams.

⚖️ PART 3 · TELL IT TRUE, NOT TOO BIG

Where careful science draws the line

The Yellowstone cascade is one of the best-documented keystone stories in the world, and the broad pattern — wolves back, elk behavior shifting, some vegetation recovering — is well supported. But good ecology stays honest about its limits. Scientists continue to debate the magnitude of the downstream effects: how much of the recovery is due to wolves versus climate, drought, bears and cougars, and changing elk numbers. The recovery has also been uneven — strong in some valleys, weak in others.

You may have heard that “wolves changed the course of the rivers.” It is a wonderful line — and it overstates what the evidence firmly shows. The responsible summary: the wolves' return triggered a real, measurable cascade whose full reach scientists are still working out.

A nature-of-science habit (from Module 1): resisting the too-tidy story is not a weakness — it is exactly the honesty this course prizes. State the strong evidence and the open questions. That is what careful ecology sounds like.
