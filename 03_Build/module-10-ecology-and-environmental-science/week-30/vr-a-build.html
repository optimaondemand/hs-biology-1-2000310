Week 30 · VR-A (Build / Model)
Build a Florida Ecosystem with Biotic & Abiotic Factors
Solo VR activity · ~30 minutes · 10 pts (completion)
🔬 WHAT YOU’RE DOING
You will construct a walk-through Florida ecosystem — either the Everglades or a mangrove shore — by placing and labeling its two kinds of parts. You will drop in the biotic (living) components sorted by their role — producers, consumers, and decomposers — and the abiotic (nonliving) components — water, sunlight, soil, and temperature. Then you will record a short walkthrough that separates the biotic from the abiotic out loud. Building the ecosystem as a space you can stand inside makes Lesson 30.01's biotic/abiotic distinction concrete: you will see that a living community is always stitched into a nonliving stage. This is Lesson 30.01, built in space.
🔧 WHAT YOU’LL NEED
A VR headset (recommended) or a desktop VR app · your completed Reading 30.01 and Lesson 30.01 · your scientific notebook.
📍 HOW TO JOIN
Device: VR headset or desktop · Session type: temporary
Session ID: posted by your teacher at the top of this Canvas assignment
Location: Florida Ecosystem Builder / Everglades & Mangrove Scene (Optima VR library)
On arrival: go to the empty build platform marked by the Whiteboard IFX. A sticky note there gives your first instruction — choose Everglades or mangrove, then start with the abiotic stage.
📝 WHAT TO DO, IN ORDER
1
Lay the abiotic stage. Place and label the four nonliving factors first: water (the marsh sheet or tidal water), sunlight (the light source overhead), soil (the muck or sandy bottom), and temperature (a warm, subtropical setting). Tag each one ABIOTIC.
2
Add the producers. Place the living things that make their own food and tag them BIOTIC · Producer: sawgrass and algae for the Everglades, or red mangrove trees for the mangrove shore. Note that they capture the sunlight you placed — the link back to photosynthesis (Module 4).
3
Add the consumers. Place animals that eat other organisms and tag them BIOTIC · Consumer: an alligator, a wading bird (heron or egret), and fish for the Everglades; small fish, shrimp, or crabs among the mangrove roots.
4
Add the decomposers. Place bacteria and fungi (on a decaying log or in the muck) and tag them BIOTIC · Decomposer. Note that they recycle dead material back into the soil — a bridge between the biotic and abiotic parts.
5
Record your walkthrough. Start a spatial recording. In about 60–90 seconds, walk your ecosystem and name each part, clearly saying whether it is biotic or abiotic — and, for the living ones, whether it is a producer, consumer, or decomposer. End by naming the one abiotic factor that most defines your ecosystem (the water cycle for the Everglades; salt for the mangrove).
📤 WHAT TO SUBMIT
Upload your spatial recording to this Canvas assignment. If you can’t record, submit one full snapshot of your labeled ecosystem (all parts visible) plus typed labels sorting each part into biotic (producer/consumer/decomposer) or abiotic, and a sentence naming the defining abiotic factor.
⏱️ TIME · ABOUT 30 MINUTES IN-HEADSET
Facilitator note: Students externalize Lesson 30.01's biotic/abiotic distinction by building an ecosystem stage-first, then populating it. Two common errors to watch for and correct: calling a dead log or the soil “abiotic” when it contains living decomposers (the once-living log is treated as biotic; the mineral soil itself is abiotic — have students be precise), and lumping all animals together instead of distinguishing producers, consumers, and decomposers by how they get energy. A strong submission shows a labeled ecosystem with all four abiotic factors placed, at least one producer, consumer, and decomposer correctly tagged, and a walkthrough that cleanly separates biotic from abiotic and names the defining factor.
✅ SUCCESS CRITERIA (COMPLETION)
☐
All four abiotic factors (water, sunlight, soil, temperature) are placed and labeled ABIOTIC.
☐
At least one producer, one consumer, and one decomposer are placed and correctly labeled as biotic by role.
☐
A spatial recording (or full snapshot + typed labels) is submitted that distinguishes biotic from abiotic and names the ecosystem's defining abiotic factor.
