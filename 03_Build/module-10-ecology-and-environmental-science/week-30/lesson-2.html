Lesson 30.02
Help, Harm, and Compete: How Species Live Together
Lesson · Predation, Competition, Mutualism, Commensalism, Parasitism
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A tick on a deer, a bee on a flower, a barnacle on a whale — all three are “living together.” So why is one harmful, one helpful, and one neither?
Today you learn to name every relationship between two species with one simple habit: score each partner + (helped), − (harmed), or 0 (unaffected). That bookkeeping turns predation, competition, and the three symbioses — mutualism, commensalism, and parasitism — into an easy sort.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to describe predation and competition and identify who is helped and who is harmed in each.
2
I will be able to distinguish mutualism, commensalism, and parasitism using the +/−/0 effect on each partner.
3
I will be able to classify a real-world example (bee & flower, barnacle & whale, tick & deer) into the correct interaction.

🧊 PART 1 · EATING AND COMPETING
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A remora fish rides attached to a shark, eating scraps the shark leaves behind — without helping or harming the shark. Before reading on, what would you call that relationship?

Predation (+ / −) and competition (− / −)

Predation is one organism killing and eating another: the predator is helped (+), the prey harmed (−). A panther and a deer, a heron and a fish. Over generations it drives an arms race — faster prey, sharper predators — which is natural selection (Module 6) at work. Competition is two organisms needing the same limited resource; both are harmed (− / −), because each would do better without the other. Two oak seedlings reaching for the same sunlight are competing — and competition is exactly why two species cannot share the same niche (Lesson 30.01) in one place for long.

🦄 PART 2 · THE THREE SYMBIOSES

Same word, three outcomes

Symbiosis means a close, long-term relationship between two species. The +/−/0 scorecard splits it three ways: mutualism (+ / +, both benefit — bee & flower, or coral & its zooxanthellae algae), commensalism (+ / 0, one benefits and the other is unaffected — barnacle on a whale), and parasitism (+ / −, the parasite benefits and the host is harmed — tick on a deer). A parasite differs from a predator in that it usually lives on or in its host and harms it slowly rather than killing it outright.

Try it — match each example to its interaction. Score each partner in your head first, then choose.
✅ MATCH THE INTERACTION
A bee sips nectar from a flower and carries its pollen to the next bloom. (Bee: +, Flower: +)
A tick attaches to a deer and feeds on its blood, weakening it. (Tick: +, Deer: −)
A barnacle rides on a whale's skin, reaching richer feeding waters; the whale is unaffected. (Barnacle: +, Whale: 0)
