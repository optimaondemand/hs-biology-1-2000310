Home Ground: The Ecosystems of Florida
Lesson · Everglades, Coral Reefs, Longleaf Pine, Mangroves
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
One forest in Florida cannot survive without regular fire. One reef lives or dies by the temperature of the water. What makes each of Florida's ecosystems the place it is?
Today ecology comes home. You will visit four Florida ecosystems — the Everglades, the coral reefs of the Keys, the longleaf pine savanna, and the coastal mangroves — and for each ask the two questions from Lesson 30.01: which abiotic factor defines it, and which living things have built their niches around that factor.

🌿 PART 1 · WATER, SEA, FIRE, SALT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN

The Everglades and the coral reef

The Everglades is a vast, shallow freshwater wetland — a “river of grass” of sawgrass, defined by a seasonal wet/dry water cycle. Alligators dig gator holes that hold water through the dry season, and wading birds nest when shrinking pools concentrate fish. Off the Florida Keys lies the continent's only extensive coral reef tract, defined by warm, clear, sunlit saltwater. The reef rests on the coral–algae mutualism from Lesson 30.02; when water grows too warm, coral expels its algae and starves — coral bleaching.

Longleaf pine and mangroves

The longleaf pine savanna is fire-dependent: frequent low fires clear brush and open ground, and the pines are built to survive them. The gopher tortoise is its keystone species — a species whose effect far outweighs its numbers — because its burrows shelter hundreds of other animals; the endangered red-cockaded woodpecker nests in old living pines. The coastal mangroves are defined by high salinity; their tangled roots (red, black, and white mangroves, sorted by how wet they stand) form a fish nursery and a storm buffer.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

Four ecosystems, four defining abiotic factors — the Everglades' water cycle, the reef's warm sea, the pine savanna's fire, the mangrove's salt — each with a living community built around it. This is the biotic/abiotic weave of Lesson 30.01, made local, and the place where the module's virtue of responsibility stops being abstract: these are the lands and waters we are asked to steward. Next lesson we zoom back out to see how these local patterns fit the world's great biomes — and how climate draws the whole map.
