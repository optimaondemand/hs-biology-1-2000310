Biomes of the World: How Climate Draws the Map of Life
Anchor Reading · Terrestrial & Aquatic Biomes, Climate, Climatographs
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Why is one part of the world a rainforest and another a desert, though both bake under the same sun? The answer is climate — the long-term pattern of temperature and precipitation. This anchor reading for Lesson 30.04 introduces the world's major biomes (from Lesson 30.01, a biome is a large region defined by its climate and characteristic community), shows how two abiotic factors — temperature and precipitation — decide which biome forms, and teaches you to read a climatograph, the simple graph scientists use to identify a biome from its weather alone.

Two numbers draw the map

A biome is not defined by which exact species live there but by its climate and the kind of community that climate produces. And climate comes down mostly to two abiotic measurements: average temperature and annual precipitation (rain and snow). Give an ecologist those two numbers for a place and they can usually name its biome without ever visiting — because plants, and the animals that depend on them, can only grow where warmth and water allow.

This is the deepest idea of the lesson: climate determines biome. It is also why the same biome type can appear on different continents — wherever the temperature-and-precipitation recipe repeats, a similar community arises.

The major terrestrial (land) biomes

Biome Climate (temp + precipitation) Character
Tropical rainforestHot & very wet, all yearThe richest biodiversity on Earth
DesertVery dry (hot or cold)Water-storing plants; sparse life
GrasslandModerate temp, seasonal moderate rainGrasses, few trees; grazers
Temperate forestWarm summers, cold winters, plentiful rainDeciduous trees; four seasons
Taiga (boreal forest)Long cold winters, moderate precipitationCone-bearing evergreens
TundraFrigid & dry; frozen subsoil (permafrost)No trees; low mosses & lichens

Read the table down and you can watch the two knobs turn: as places get colder and drier, the forest thins to grassland, then to desert or tundra. Florida, warm and wet, sits near the tropical/subtropical end of this range.

Aquatic biomes: freshwater and marine

Most of Earth is water, and aquatic biomes split by one abiotic factor above all — salinity. Freshwater biomes (lakes, ponds, rivers, and wetlands like the Everglades) have little dissolved salt. Marine biomes (oceans, coral reefs, and the salt-and-fresh mixing zone of estuaries) are salty. In water, the factors that shape life are salinity, temperature, and how deep the sunlight can reach — because below the sunlit surface, no photosynthesis is possible.

Reading a climatograph

A climatograph (or climate diagram) is a single graph that shows a place's climate month by month. It combines two data series on one set of months (January to December along the bottom):

• Temperature is plotted as a line (read on one vertical axis, usually the right).
• Precipitation is plotted as bars (read on the other vertical axis, usually the left).

To identify a biome from a climatograph, ask two questions. How warm? — look at the temperature line: high and flat all year means tropical; a deep winter dip means a cold-winter biome. How wet, and when? — look at the bars: tall bars all year means rainforest; tiny bars all year means desert; tall bars in one season and short in another means a wet/dry climate like a savanna or grassland.

For example, a climatograph with a high, nearly flat temperature line and tall precipitation bars every month points to a tropical rainforest. One with a warm line but almost no bars all year points to a desert. A line that plunges below freezing in winter with only modest precipitation points to taiga or tundra. The graph lets you name a biome you have never seen.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Climate is the hidden hand behind the whole living map. The same two abiotic factors that sorted Florida's ecosystems in Lesson 30.03 — how warm and how wet — scale up to sort the entire planet into biomes. And this is exactly why the question of a changing climate, which the rest of this module takes up, is a biological question and not merely a weather one: shift the temperature and precipitation, and you shift the map of where life can live. Reading a climatograph is a small skill with a very large view.
