Reading 30.02
Living Together: How Species Help, Harm, and Compete
Anchor Reading · Predation, Competition, and the Three Symbioses
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
A community is a crowd of species living in the same place, and no species lives alone. Every one is caught in a web of relationships — eating and being eaten, competing for the same food, or partnered for life. This anchor reading for Lesson 30.02 sorts those relationships into a small, powerful set of categories. The trick to all of them is simple bookkeeping: for each species in a pair, ask whether the relationship helps it (+), harms it (−), or leaves it unaffected (0). Master that plus/minus habit and every interaction below becomes easy to name.

Predation (+ / −)

Predation is one organism (the predator) killing and eating another (the prey). The predator is helped (+); the prey is harmed (−). A Florida panther hunting a deer, a heron spearing a fish, an alligator taking a turtle — all predation.

Predation shapes both species over generations. It pushes prey toward better speed, camouflage, and alertness, and predators toward sharper senses and quicker strikes — a slow arms race that is natural selection (Module 6) at work. Herbivores eating plants are a form of predation too, sometimes called grazing.

Competition (− / −)

Competition happens when two organisms need the same limited resource — food, water, light, space, or a mate. Both are harmed (− / −), because each would do better without the other in the way. Two young oak trees reaching for the same patch of sunlight are competing; so are two hawks hunting the same field of mice.

Competition is the reason two species cannot share the exact same niche in the same place indefinitely (Lesson 30.01). The better-suited competitor gradually wins, and the other must shift its niche, move, or die out. Competition, quietly, is one of nature's great sorters.

Symbiosis: three ways to live in close partnership

Symbiosis means “living together” — a close, long-term relationship between two species. There are three kinds, and the plus/minus habit tells them apart at a glance:

Relationship Effect In words
Mutualism+ / +Both species benefit
Commensalism+ / 0One benefits; the other is unaffected
Parasitism+ / −The parasite benefits; the host is harmed

Mutualism (+ / +) — both partners gain. A bee visits a flower for nectar and, dusted with pollen, carries it to the next flower; the bee is fed and the plant is pollinated. In a coral reef, tiny algae called zooxanthellae live inside coral tissue: the algae get shelter and the coral gets sugars from the algae's photosynthesis — a partnership you will meet again in Lesson 30.03.

Commensalism (+ / 0) — one gains, the other neither wins nor loses. A barnacle riding on a whale's skin gets carried to fresh feeding waters; the whale, for the most part, is unaffected. The barnacle profits; the whale hardly notices.

Parasitism (+ / −) — one gains at the other's expense, but usually without killing it outright. A tick feeding on a deer's blood is fed (+) while the deer is weakened and exposed to disease (−). A parasite differs from a predator in that it typically lives on or in its host and harms it slowly, rather than killing and eating it at once.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Five relationships, one method: score each partner + , − , or 0, and the name follows. These interactions are the threads that hold a community together — and the reason a change to one species ripples out to many. When we walk into Florida's ecosystems next lesson, watch how often the health of a whole place turns on a single partnership, like the coral and its algae. Communities are not just collections of species; they are webs of these relationships.
