Lesson · Primary and Secondary Succession, and the One Word That Separates Them
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A volcano makes a new island of bare rock. A forest fire clears an old woodland to ash. Both will one day be green again — but one takes centuries and the other takes decades. Why?
Today you learn how a whole community rebuilds itself in a predictable order — ecological succession. You will tell apart primary succession (on bare rock, with no soil) and secondary succession (after a disturbance that leaves the soil), and see why the single word soil is the whole difference in speed.

🌋 PART 1 · PRIMARY SUCCESSION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A brand-new island of cooled lava rises from the sea — nothing but bare rock. What do you think could possibly grow there first? (No wrong answer — just picture it.)

Building soil from bare rock

Primary succession begins where there is no soil at all — bare rock, fresh lava, or new sand. The first arrivals are the pioneer species, above all lichens (a fungus-and-alga partnership) and then mosses. Their great job is to build soil: acids and dead remains crumble rock into grains and add organic matter, grain by grain. Only once soil exists can grasses, then shrubs, then trees follow. Because primary succession must make its own soil first, it is extremely slow — often centuries.

A real one, happening now: in 1963 an undersea eruption raised the new island of Surtsey off Iceland — sterile black rock with no soil, no seeds, no life. Protected and watched ever since, it has been colonized in just this order: microbes and molds, then mosses and lichens weathering the rock, then hardy plants and nesting birds as wind and sea carried in seeds. Surtsey is one of science's clearest live windows into primary succession.
🌪️ PART 2 · SECONDARY SUCCESSION

Rebuilding where the soil survived

Secondary succession follows a disturbance that clears a community but leaves the soil — a fire, a flood, or an abandoned field. Because the soil is already there, full of nutrients, roots, and buried seeds, recovery is much faster — years to decades, not centuries. After a fire, fast grasses and wildflowers return first, then shrubs and sun-loving young trees, and eventually a mature forest much like the one that burned. Same direction as primary succession — small fast pioneers toward large slow trees — but starting far ahead, on ready-made ground.

A wildfire burns through a forest. A few years later, grasses and young trees are already returning. What kind of succession is this, and why so fast?
On a newly cooled lava flow, lichens appear first and slowly build soil over many years. What kind of succession is this?
🌲 PART 3 · THE CLIMAX COMMUNITY

Where succession is heading

Both kinds of succession trend toward a relatively stable, mature stage called the climax community — one that renews itself and changes little until the next disturbance resets the clock. "Stable" is a rough, ongoing balance, not a permanent freeze: a mature forest holds until the next fire or storm, and then succession begins again.

The one word to remember: soil. If a place starts with bare rock and no soil, it is primary succession — slow, because it must build soil first. If soil remains after the disturbance, it is secondary succession — fast, because the ground is ready. Notice, too, the link to Module 8: the pioneer lichens and mosses that build soil are relatives of the plants you studied — the living things that literally create the ground the rest of the community stands on.
