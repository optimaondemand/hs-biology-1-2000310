Reading 33.01
How Populations Grow: The J-Curve and the S-Curve
Anchor Reading · Growth Factors, Exponential Growth, Logistic Growth
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
All year this course has climbed from atoms to cells to whole organisms. Now we climb one level higher, to the population — all the members of one species living in the same place at the same time. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 33.01. By the end you should be able to name the four factors that change a population's size, and tell apart the two classic shapes of population growth: the runaway J-shaped curve of exponential growth and the leveling S-shaped curve of logistic growth.

Four ways a population changes size

A population is not a fixed number. It rises and falls according to four factors. Two add members: births (new individuals born into the population) and immigration (individuals moving in from elsewhere). Two remove members: deaths and emigration (individuals moving out). When the additions outnumber the removals, the population grows; when removals win, it shrinks; when they balance, it holds steady.

A simple way to hold it: growth = (births + immigration) − (deaths + emigration). Keep the two "in" words (births, immigration) and the two "out" words (deaths, emigration) straight, and every population question that follows becomes easier.

Exponential growth: the J-shaped curve

Imagine a population with everything it could want — unlimited food, unlimited space, no predators, no disease. Under those ideal conditions a population grows by exponential growth: the more individuals there are, the more offspring they produce, so the population adds members faster and faster. Plotted over time, the line starts slow, then bends steeply upward into a shape like the letter J. The fastest rate a species could reproduce under perfect conditions is called its biotic potential.

You can see exponential growth in the real world for short bursts: bacteria in fresh nutrient broth, or a few rabbits released onto an island with no predators. But hold on to one truth from the start — no population can grow exponentially forever. The real world always runs out of something.

Logistic growth: the S-shaped curve

In nature, resources are limited. As a population grows, individuals begin to compete for food, water, space, and mates. Growth that starts out fast begins to slow down as the population gets crowded, until it roughly levels off. This more realistic pattern is logistic growth, and plotted over time it makes a shape like the letter S: a slow start, a steep middle, and a leveling-off at the top.

The level where the curve flattens is the largest population the environment can support over the long run — you will meet it by name, carrying capacity, in the next lesson. For now, notice the difference in the two shapes: the J-curve keeps climbing because nothing is holding it back; the S-curve levels off because something is.

Reading the two curves side by side

The two curves start out looking almost the same — both climb steeply at first. The difference shows up later. Ask yourself one question about any growth graph: does the line keep shooting up, or does it bend over and flatten?

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Populations are where biology becomes ecology — where the reproduction you studied in Module 5 and the variation of Module 6 play out among crowds of living things sharing a limited world. The J-curve and the S-curve are the alphabet of that story. In the next lesson you will learn why the S-curve levels off: the limiting factors and carrying capacity that keep every real population in check. And by the module's end, this same question — how a growing population meets its limits — will help you think clearly about our own species and the world we are responsible for.
