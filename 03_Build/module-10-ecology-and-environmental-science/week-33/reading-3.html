Reading 33.03
How a Community Rebuilds: Ecological Succession
Anchor Reading · Primary Succession, Secondary Succession, Climax Community
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
A single population changes over time; so does the whole community of species living together in one place. When a forest burns or a volcano makes new land, life does not return all at once — it arrives in a predictable order. This orderly rebuilding is called ecological succession, and it is the anchor text for Lesson 33.03. By the end you should be able to tell apart primary succession (starting on bare rock, with no soil) and secondary succession (following a disturbance, where soil remains), and explain why the second is so much faster.

Primary succession: life on bare rock

Primary succession begins where there is no soil at all — bare rock left by a retreating glacier, fresh lava, or newly exposed sand. The first colonizers are the pioneer species: tough, undemanding organisms that can live on rock, above all the lichens (a partnership of a fungus and an alga) and, a little later, mosses. Pioneer species do the slow, essential work of building soil: their acids and their dead remains crumble rock into grains and add organic matter, grain by grain, over many years.

Only once a thin soil has formed can small plants and grasses take root, then shrubs, then trees — each stage changing the ground enough to make the next stage possible. Because primary succession has to make its own soil first, it is extremely slow, often taking centuries.

A living laboratory: the island of Surtsey

In 1963, an undersea volcanic eruption off the south coast of Iceland pushed a brand-new island above the waves. It was named Surtsey, and it began as nothing but sterile black rock — no soil, no seeds, no life. Scientists protected it and have watched it ever since. Bacteria and molds came first, then mosses and lichens took hold, slowly weathering the rock; wind, birds, and the sea carried in seeds; and over the following decades, hardy plants and nesting birds established themselves. Surtsey is one of the clearest windows science has into primary succession — a place where life is colonizing bare rock in real time, exactly in the order this reading describes.

Secondary succession: rebuilding where soil remains

Secondary succession happens after a disturbance that clears away much of a community but leaves the soil behind — a forest fire, a flood, or a farm field left abandoned. Because the soil is already there, full of nutrients, roots, and buried seeds, recovery is much faster than primary succession. There is no need to spend centuries making soil from rock; life can rush back in years to decades.

After a fire, for instance, fast-growing grasses and wildflowers return first, then shrubs and sun-loving young trees, and eventually a mature forest much like the one that burned. The whole sequence runs the same direction as primary succession — from small, fast pioneers toward larger, slower, longer-lived species — but it starts far ahead, on ready-made ground.

Toward a climax community

Both kinds of succession trend toward a relatively stable, mature stage called the climax community — a community that keeps renewing itself and changes little unless a new disturbance strikes. It is "stable" only in a rough, ongoing sense: it will hold until the next fire, storm, or human change resets the clock.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Succession shows that ecosystems are not fixed scenery but living processes with a history and a direction. The single word that separates the two kinds — soil — is the whole difference between a recovery that takes decades and one that takes centuries. It also connects back to the plants of Module 8, whose pioneer relatives literally build the ground, and forward to the last lesson of the week, where we ask how human disturbance and a changing climate are resetting these communities faster than they can recover.
