Reading 33.04
Reading the Evidence: A Changing Climate
Anchor Reading · Three Independent Lines of Evidence · Module 10 CER Anchor
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
This is the reading your Module 10 CER essay is built on. It asks you to do what a scientist does: weigh the evidence. You will meet three independent lines of evidence that a changing climate points to — the temperature record, the atmospheric carbon dioxide record, and the shifting ranges and timing of living things — and see why the fact that three separate kinds of measurement converge on the same conclusion makes that conclusion strong. This is the anchor text for Lesson 33.04. Read it soberly and carefully; the goal is not alarm and not dismissal, but clear reasoning from evidence.

How to hold a large scientific question

A responsible thinker meets a big claim the way this course has taught all year: by asking what the evidence is and how good it is. On climate, the strong scientific consensus — shared by the world's major scientific bodies — is that Earth's climate is warming and that human activity, chiefly the burning of fossil fuels, is the main cause. That consensus rests not on any single study but on many independent measurements pointing the same way.

Being honest also means being precise about where uncertainty lives. The serious scientific debate is mostly about the rate and the magnitude — exactly how fast, exactly how much, and precisely where the effects will fall — not about whether warming is happening. Keeping that distinction straight is itself a mark of good science: confident where the evidence is strong, careful where it is still open.

Line 1 — The temperature record

For well over a century, thermometers at weather stations, on ships, and on ocean buoys around the world have recorded temperature. Averaged together, these instrumental measurements show that Earth's average surface temperature has risen, with the sharpest warming coming since the middle of the twentieth century. This is direct measurement, not modeling: many independent records, on land and sea, in both hemispheres, agreeing that the planet's average is warmer than it was.

Line 2 — The carbon dioxide record and its fingerprint

In 1958, the scientist Charles David Keeling began continuously measuring the amount of carbon dioxide (CO₂) in the atmosphere from an observatory high on Mauna Loa in Hawaii. His record — now called the Keeling Curve — shows atmospheric CO₂ climbing steadily, year after year, in a saw-toothed line that wiggles with the seasons but trends relentlessly upward. It is one of the most important continuous datasets in all of science.

But how do we know the added CO₂ comes from burning fossil fuels rather than from volcanoes or the ocean? The answer is a chemical fingerprint. Carbon comes in different isotopes (atoms of the same element with different masses). Carbon from ancient plants — which is what coal, oil, and gas are — carries a distinctive isotopic signature, and it contains no radioactive carbon-14 because it is far too old. As fossil-fuel carbon pours into the air, it shifts the atmosphere's isotope ratios in exactly the direction that "old plant carbon" predicts. That fingerprint ties the rising CO₂ directly to fossil-fuel burning — and it connects straight to the carbon cycle you studied last week: carbon locked underground for millions of years is being returned to the air far faster than the cycle removes it.

Line 3 — The living world is shifting

The third line of evidence comes from biology itself — from watching what living things do. Across many species, ecologists have documented ranges shifting poleward (toward the cooler poles) and upslope (to higher, cooler elevations), as organisms track the temperatures they are suited to. They have also documented changes in phenology — the timing of natural events. Spring is arriving earlier for many species: earlier flowering, earlier leaf-out, earlier insect emergence, and shifted timing of bird migration and breeding.

These biological signals are powerful precisely because they come from a completely different source than thermometers or gas analyzers. Plants and animals are not reading the temperature record; they are simply responding to their world — and their responses independently trace the same warming.

🔗 THE HEART OF THE ARGUMENT · CONVERGENCE OF INDEPENDENT EVIDENCE
Any one line of evidence could, in principle, be questioned on its own. But these three come from independent sources — physics (temperature), chemistry (CO₂ and its isotopes), and biology (species ranges and timing). When independent lines of evidence, gathered by different methods, all point to the same conclusion, that agreement is far harder to explain away than any single measurement. This is exactly the reasoning you used in Module 6 to argue for common descent, where the fossil record, comparative anatomy, and molecular sequences converged. Convergence of independent evidence is one of the strongest tools science has.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

This reading is the evidence file for your Module 10 CER, where you will build the argument yourself: a claim, the three independent lines of evidence, and the reasoning that ties them together — including why convergence matters. It is also where this year's long argument, from atoms to ecosystems, finally arrives at us. Understanding the evidence is the first responsibility of a good steward: you cannot care wisely for a world you refuse to see clearly. Read the evidence honestly, reason from it carefully, and let that reasoning — not alarm, and not dismissal — guide what you conclude.
