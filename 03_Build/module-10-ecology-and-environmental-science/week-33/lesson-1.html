Exponential vs. Logistic Growth
Lesson · Growth Factors, the J-Curve, and the S-Curve
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Put two bacteria in fresh broth and by morning there are billions. Put two rabbits in a meadow and you get a lot of rabbits — but never infinite rabbits. Why the difference?
Welcome to populations — the level of life above the single organism. Today you learn the four factors that change a population's size and the two classic shapes of growth: the runaway J-shaped curve of exponential growth and the leveling S-shaped curve of logistic growth. Get these two shapes into your eye and you can read almost any population graph on sight.

👥 PART 1 · WHAT CHANGES A POPULATION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A pond has 500 frogs this spring. Before reading on, what could make that number go up by next spring? (No wrong answer — just think it through.)

The four factors, two in and two out

A population is all the members of one species living in one place at one time. Its size changes by exactly four factors. Two add members: births and immigration (moving in). Two remove members: deaths and emigration (moving out). Hold it as a simple balance: growth = (births + immigration) − (deaths + emigration). When the "in" side wins, the population grows; when the "out" side wins, it shrinks.

Watch the mix-up: immigration means moving in; emigration means exiting. Keep the "in" pair (births, immigration) and the "out" pair (deaths, emigration) straight and every population problem gets easier.
📈 PART 2 · TWO SHAPES OF GROWTH

The J-curve and the S-curve

Exponential growth happens when resources are treated as unlimited — unlimited food, space, no predators, no disease. The more individuals there are, the faster the population adds new ones, so the graph bends ever more steeply upward into a shape like the letter J. The fastest a species could possibly reproduce under perfect conditions is its biotic potential. You see the J-curve in short bursts: bacteria in fresh broth, or a few animals loosed on a predator-free island.

But real resources run out. As a population grows and crowds, individuals compete, and growth slows until it roughly levels off. This realistic pattern is logistic growth, shaped like the letter S: slow start, steep middle, leveling top. The level where it flattens is the carrying capacity — the star of the next lesson.

Two graphs both start by rising steeply. One keeps shooting upward; the other bends over and flattens.
Which is exponential, which is logistic — and what makes the second one level off?
(Tap to flip.)
✅ THE ANSWER
The one that keeps shooting up is exponential (the J-curve); the one that levels off is logistic (the S-curve).
The S-curve flattens because resources are limited: as the population crowds, competition for food and space slows growth until births and deaths roughly balance at the environment's carrying capacity. The J-curve doesn't level off because it pretends nothing is ever in short supply — which is why no real population can follow it forever.

Say it in your notebook in your own words: the J-curve keeps climbing because nothing holds it back; the S-curve levels off because limited resources do.

⚠️ PART 3 · THE ONE THAT TRIPS PEOPLE UP

Exponential growth can't last

The single most common error on this topic is imagining that a population can just keep growing exponentially. It cannot. The real world always runs out of something — food, water, space, clean conditions — and when it does, the J-curve either bends into an S-curve or the population overshoots and crashes. Exponential growth is a short chapter, never the whole story.

Connect it back: the crowding, competition, and struggle that bend the J-curve into an S-curve are the very pressures that drove natural selection in Module 6. More offspring are always produced than the environment can support — and that surplus is exactly what selection acts on. Populations are where evolution and ecology meet.
