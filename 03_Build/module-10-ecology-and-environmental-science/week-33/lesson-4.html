🧠 PART 1 · HOW TO HOLD A BIG QUESTION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When someone makes a big scientific claim, what is the most useful first question a careful thinker can ask? (No wrong answer — just check in.)

Confident where the evidence is strong; careful where it is open

The world's major scientific bodies share a strong consensus: Earth's climate is warming, and human activity — chiefly burning fossil fuels — is the main cause. That consensus rests on many independent measurements, not one study. Being honest also means being precise about uncertainty: the serious scientific debate is mostly about the rate and magnitude (how fast, how much, exactly where), not about whether warming is happening. Good science is confident where evidence is strong and careful where it is still open.

📊 PART 2 · THREE INDEPENDENT LINES OF EVIDENCE

Physics, chemistry, and biology — three different witnesses

Each line below is measured by a completely different method, yet all three point the same way.

1. The temperature record (physics).
Over a century of thermometers — at weather stations, on ships, on ocean buoys — averaged together show Earth's surface has warmed, most sharply since the mid-1900s. This is direct measurement, agreeing across land, sea, and both hemispheres.
2. The CO₂ record and its fingerprint (chemistry).
In 1958, Charles David Keeling began continuously measuring atmospheric CO₂ at Mauna Loa. His Keeling Curve shows CO₂ rising steadily year after year. And the carbon-isotope fingerprint of the added gas matches ancient-plant carbon — coal, oil, and gas — tying the rise to fossil-fuel burning. This connects straight to last week's carbon cycle: carbon locked away for millions of years is returning to the air faster than the cycle removes it.
3. Biological shifts (biology).
Species' ranges are shifting poleward and upslope toward cooler conditions, and phenology — nature's timing — is shifting: earlier springs, earlier flowering and leaf-out, shifted migration and breeding. Living things are not reading thermometers; they are simply responding to their world, and independently tracing the same warming.

🌡️ CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH · HOW SCIENTISTS ASSEMBLE THE PICTURE
Recent work suggests just how much the modern climate picture depends on weaving many kinds of data together. Scientists are currently combining readings from satellites watching the whole planet, ocean buoys tracking sea temperatures, ice cores that preserve air bubbles from the deep past, and thousands of ground weather stations — feeding them into climate models that test how well our understanding reproduces what is actually observed. No single instrument sees everything; the strength comes from independent datasets that check one another. Researchers are careful to distinguish what they measure with high confidence (that warming is happening) from what they are still refining (precisely how fast, and exactly where the effects fall) — a working example of science holding certainty and uncertainty in the same honest hand.
🔗 PART 3 · WHY CONVERGENCE MATTERS

Three witnesses who never spoke to each other

Any one line, alone, could be questioned. But these three come from independent sources — physics, chemistry, and biology — gathered by different people using different methods. When independent lines all point to the same conclusion, that agreement is far harder to explain away than any single measurement. This is the very reasoning you used in Module 6 to argue for common descent, where fossils, anatomy, and DNA converged. Convergence of independent evidence is one of the strongest tools science has — and it is the backbone of the CER you are about to write.

🤖 AI AWARENESS · CAN A MODEL PREDICT WHERE A SPECIES WILL GO?
The opportunity is real: machine-learning models help ecologists project how species ranges may shift as climate changes, downscale broad climate projections to a local valley or coastline, and fill gaps where field data are sparse — letting researchers plan conservation across regions no team could survey by hand. But the pitfalls deserve equal weight. A model inherits the biases and gaps of its training data: if it learned mostly from well-studied places, it can mislead about the rest. Its output is a projection, not a measurement — a hypothesis about the future to test against what actually happens, not a fact. And a confident-looking map can hide deep uncertainty. Treat an AI ecological projection the way you treat any scientific claim: a useful lead you must verify against real observations, not an authority to obey. The three lines of evidence in this lesson are strong precisely because they are measured; a model's guess about tomorrow must still earn its trust.
