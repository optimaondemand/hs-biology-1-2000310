Carrying Capacity, Limiting Factors & Predator–Prey Cycles
Lesson · Why the S-Curve Levels Off
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday's S-curve leveled off — but we never said why. What actually stops a growing population, and does it stop at the same place forever?
Today you meet the answer: carrying capacity (K), the ceiling an environment can support, and the limiting factors that enforce it. You will sort those factors into density-dependent (stronger in a crowd) and density-independent (indifferent to the crowd), read a real growth graph, and watch two populations rise and fall together in the classic predator–prey cycle.

K is the level the S-curve settles at

The carrying capacity, symbol K, is the maximum population an environment can sustain over the long run given its food, water, and space. On a logistic (S-shaped) graph, K is the level where the curve flattens out. Below K the population tends to grow; above K it tends to fall back. Populations often overshoot K and then dip below before settling — and because environments change, K itself can shift up or down. Read the graph below, then answer the two questions under it.

🧩 PART 2 · TWO KINDS OF LIMITING FACTOR

Does the crowd make it worse?

A limiting factor is anything that keeps a population from growing without limit. To sort them, ask one question: does this factor hit harder when the population is crowded? Density-dependent factors get stronger with crowding — competition, predation, disease, and food shortage (a disease races through a crowd; food runs out faster with more mouths). Density-independent factors strike with the same force regardless of crowding — weather, natural disasters, and fire (a freeze doesn't count heads). A quick test: living with neighbors → density-dependent; the physical environment → density-independent.

🐉 PART 3 · PREDATOR AND PREY, IN RHYTHM

The lynx and the snowshoe hare

Predation is a density-dependent factor with a famous beat. When prey are plentiful, predators eat well and multiply; more predators then eat down the prey; with less food, predators decline; and the freed prey recover — starting the cycle over. The result is a repeating predator–prey cycle, the predator's peak lagging just behind the prey's. The textbook case is the Canada lynx and the snowshoe hare: long-term records show the hare booming and crashing roughly every ten years, with the lynx following a step behind.

The chain to remember: prey rise → predators rise (more food) → prey fall (eaten down) → predators fall (less food) → prey recover → repeat. Neither population controls itself alone; each is a limiting factor for the other. This is the two-population echo of the single-population balance from Lesson 33.01.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

Every real population is held in balance by its limits. Carrying capacity is the ceiling; limiting factors — density-dependent and density-independent — are what enforce it; and where two species are linked as predator and prey, their numbers dance together in a lasting rhythm. Keep the key caution: K is a moving target, not a fixed wall. Next we zoom out from one population to the whole community, and watch it rebuild itself through ecological succession.
