📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Yesterday's S-curve leveled off — but the reading never said why. This is the reading that answers it. This is the anchor text for Lesson 33.02. By the end you should be able to define carrying capacity, sort limiting factors into density-dependent and density-independent, and explain the rise-and-fall predator–prey cycle that links two populations together.

Carrying capacity: the level the S-curve settles at

The carrying capacity — written with the symbol K — is the maximum population size that a particular environment can sustain over the long run, given its food, water, space, and other resources. On a logistic (S-shaped) graph, K is the level where the curve flattens out. Below K, resources are plentiful and the population tends to grow; above K, the population has overshot what the environment can feed, and deaths outpace births until it falls back toward K.

Here is a subtlety worth keeping: K is not a fixed, permanent number. A good rainy year can raise a meadow's carrying capacity for rabbits; a drought or a fire can lower it. Carrying capacity describes an environment as it is now — and environments change.

Limiting factors: two kinds

Anything that keeps a population from growing without limit is a limiting factor. Ecologists sort limiting factors into two kinds by asking one question: does this factor hit harder when the population is crowded?

Density-dependent factors grow stronger as the population becomes denser (more crowded). Competition for food and space, predation, disease, and shortage of food all bite harder when individuals are packed together — a disease spreads faster through a crowd, and food runs out sooner when many mouths share it. These factors are the brakes that produce the S-curve's leveling-off.

Density-independent factors strike with the same force no matter how crowded the population is. A hard freeze, a hurricane or other natural disaster, a drought, or a fire will kill roughly the same fraction of a population whether it is sparse or dense — the weather does not count heads. A quick test: if a factor is basically about the physical environment (weather, disasters), it is density-independent; if it is about living with neighbors (competition, predators, disease), it is density-dependent.

Predator and prey: two populations that rise and fall together

Predation is a density-dependent factor with a famous rhythm. When prey are abundant, predators are well fed and their numbers climb. But more predators eat more prey, so the prey population falls. With less to eat, predators then starve and decline — which lets the prey recover, and the cycle begins again. The result is a repeating predator–prey cycle: two linked populations rising and falling out of step, the predator's peak lagging just behind the prey's.

The classic real-world example is the Canada lynx and the snowshoe hare of the northern forests. Long-term records show the hare population booming and crashing about every ten years, with the lynx population following the same rhythm a step behind — a living illustration of two populations bound together by who eats whom.

💡 A CAUTION WORTH REMEMBERING
It is tempting to picture carrying capacity as a hard ceiling a population slams into and stops at. Real populations rarely behave so neatly. They often overshoot K, then fall back below it, then recover — wobbling around carrying capacity rather than resting exactly on it. And because environments change, the value of K itself drifts up and down over time. Treat K as a moving average the environment can support, not a fixed wall.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Limiting factors are the reason the living world does not drown in any single species. Every population is held in balance by the resources it needs and the neighbors it lives among — a balance you first glimpsed in the natural selection of Module 6, where competition and predation shaped which individuals survived. Next you will watch whole communities change over time through ecological succession, and then turn these tools of population thinking toward the largest question of the module: a changing climate and our responsibility within it.
