📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A protein is built and folded — but who addresses it, ships it, and cleans up what wears out?
Today you meet the cell’s shipping department and cleanup crew: the Golgi apparatus (the post office), the lysosomes (recycling), the vacuoles (storage), and the peroxisomes (detox). Then you will see how they connect into one pathway — the endomembrane system.

📦 PART 1 · THE POST OFFICE AND THE CLEANUP CREW
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Imagine a question on the exam: “Which organelle packages and ships proteins?” Before you read on, which would you pick? (No wrong answer — just see what your instinct says.)

Four jobs after the build

The Golgi apparatus is a stack of flattened sacs that receives proteins and lipids from the ER and modifies, packages, and ships them — sorting each into a vesicle addressed to its destination. It is the cell’s post office. Around it work three more organelles: lysosomes, sacs of digestive enzymes that break down waste and worn-out parts (the recycling center); vacuoles, membrane storage spaces — plants have one large central vacuole that also helps support the cell; and peroxisomes, small enzyme sacs that break down fatty acids and detoxify substances, producing hydrogen peroxide and then destroying it on the spot.

🔧 PART 2 · THE ENDOMEMBRANE SYSTEM, ONE PATHWAY

A connected factory, not a list of parts

These organelles do not work alone. With the nuclear envelope and the ER they form the endomembrane system — a connected manufacturing-and-shipping pathway. Follow the route: nuclear envelope → ER → Golgi → vesicles → cell membrane or lysosomes. The DNA’s instructions leave the nucleus; the ER builds and folds the protein; the Golgi modifies, packages, and addresses it; and vesicles carry the finished product to the cell surface for export or to a lysosome for use. Each station hands off to the next, like conveyor belts in one factory.
