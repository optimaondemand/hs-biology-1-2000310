📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If the instructions for life are locked in the nucleus, how does a cell turn them into working parts?
A cell is a city, and today you visit the first three stations of its protein factory: the nucleus that stores the master instructions, the ribosomes that read them and build proteins, and the endoplasmic reticulum that folds and ships those proteins. Each part’s structure tells you its function — the idea that will guide every organelle this week.

🧬 PART 1 · THE NUCLEUS AND THE RIBOSOME
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A factory keeps its master blueprints locked in one room and sends out only photocopies to the workers. Why would a cell guard its DNA the same way? (No wrong answer — just think it through.)

The library and the builders

The nucleus is the cell’s control center because it holds the DNA — the same nucleic acid you met in Module 2, the molecule that carries the instructions for every protein. A double membrane called the nuclear envelope surrounds it, pierced by nuclear pores that let copies of instructions out while keeping the DNA in. Inside sits the nucleolus, where the parts of ribosomes are made. The ribosomes themselves are the cell’s protein builders: they link amino acids into chains following the DNA’s instructions. Free ribosomes float in the cytoplasm and build proteins for use inside the cell; bound ribosomes sit on a membrane network and build proteins for export or for membranes.

🔧 PART 2 · THE ENDOPLASMIC RETICULUM, ROUGH AND SMOOTH

Where structure announces the job

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a folded network of membranes reaching out from the nuclear envelope. Rough ER is studded with ribosomes — that is exactly why it is “rough” — and it folds and processes the proteins those ribosomes make, especially proteins headed for export. Smooth ER has no ribosomes on its surface; freed of protein work, it builds lipids and helps detoxify substances in the cell. The presence or absence of ribosomes on the surface tells you the job — structure announces function.
