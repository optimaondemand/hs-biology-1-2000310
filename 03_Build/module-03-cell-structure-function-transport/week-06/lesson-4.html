📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If every cell in your body holds the same DNA, why does a muscle cell look nothing like a nerve cell?
Two questions close the week. First, how do plant and animal cells differ, and why? Second, and deeper: how can one set of instructions build so many different kinds of cells? The answer — cell specialization through differentiation — reaches forward into Modules 5 and 9.

🌱 PART 1 · PLANT VS. ANIMAL CELLS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A plant cell has both a cell wall and a cell membrane; your cells have only the membrane. Before reading on, what is the difference in your own words? (No wrong answer — just check your thinking.)

Same toolkit, different builds

Plant and animal cells share most organelles, but a few parts belong to one and not the other. Plant cells have a rigid cell wall, a large central vacuole, chloroplasts, and plasmodesmata (channels between neighboring cells). Animal cells have centrioles and rely more on prominent lysosomes, and they have no wall. Keep one distinction razor-sharp: every cell has a flexible cell membrane that controls what crosses; only some cells also have a rigid cell wall outside that membrane. A membrane is a gate; a wall is a shell.

🧬 PART 2 · ONE GENOME, MANY CELLS

How specialization works

Nearly every cell in your body holds the same complete DNA — the same instructions. Yet a muscle cell, a nerve cell, and a red blood cell look and act completely differently. The reason: different cells express different genes, switching some instructions on and leaving others off. This becoming-specialized is called differentiation, and the result is cell specialization. Once again, structure fits function: a muscle cell is packed with mitochondria and contracting proteins for mechanical work; a nerve cell stretches into long fibers to carry signals far; a red blood cell even ejects its nucleus to hold more hemoglobin (the Module 2 protein). Same genome, different builds, each shaped for its task.
