📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
A factory runs on energy, and so does a cell. This reading meets the two organelles that handle energy — the mitochondria that release it and the chloroplasts that capture it — plus the cytoskeleton that gives the cell shape and lets things move inside it. The energy organelles point directly ahead to Module 4, where energy capture and release become the whole story. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 6.03.

Mitochondria: releasing energy

The mitochondrion is the site of cellular respiration — the process that breaks down food molecules to release usable energy in the form of ATP, the cell’s energy currency. Its structure serves that job beautifully. A mitochondrion has a double membrane, and the inner membrane is folded into ridges called cristae. Those folds pack far more working surface into a small space, and more surface means more energy-releasing reactions. Structure fits function once again.

A curious detail: mitochondria carry their own small loop of DNA, separate from the nucleus.

Chloroplasts: capturing energy

Found only in plants and algae, the chloroplast is the site of photosynthesis — capturing light energy and storing it in sugar. Inside, stacks of disk-shaped membranes called thylakoids (a stack is a granum; many are grana) hold the green pigment that traps sunlight, while the fluid around them, the stroma, hosts the reactions that build sugar. Like mitochondria, chloroplasts have a double membrane and their own DNA.

Why would two organelles inside your cells carry their own DNA? Recall the endosymbiotic theory from Lesson 5.03: long ago, free-living bacteria were taken inside larger cells and became permanent partners. Their leftover DNA and double membranes are the evidence — a deep tie between this unit and the story of how complex cells arose.

The cytoskeleton: shape, support, and movement

A cell is not held up by bone, yet it keeps its shape and moves materials from place to place. It does this with the cytoskeleton — a network of protein fibers running through the cytoplasm. It has three kinds of fibers:

Fiber Role
Microfilaments Thin fibers involved in shape and cell movement.
Intermediate filaments Rope-like fibers that give structural support and anchor organelles.
Microtubules Hollow tubes that act as tracks for transport and help move chromosomes during cell division.

Together they are part scaffold, part highway — holding the cell’s shape and moving its cargo.

✨ RESEARCH HAPPENING NOW · GROWING TINY ORGANS
If you understand how cells live and power themselves, a striking possibility opens up: could we grow them into miniature versions of organs in a dish? Scientists are currently exploring organoids — tiny, simplified three-dimensional clusters of cells, sometimes only a few millimeters wide, grown to mimic parts of a brain, gut, or kidney. Recent research suggests they may help model diseases and test candidate drugs without first using animals or people. Hold this honestly: organoids are still early and far simpler than a real organ — they lack full blood supply and many cell types — so findings are promising rather than settled. The principle beneath the work, though, is exactly this unit’s: a living cell, with all its organelles, is the smallest unit that can build something larger.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Mitochondria and chloroplasts are where the cell’s energy is released and captured — and that is precisely the doorway to Module 4, which spends three full weeks on how photosynthesis and cellular respiration actually work. You have now met the structures; next module you will watch them run. Hold onto one more thread: their own DNA and double membranes link them back to the endosymbiosis of Module 5 and forward to evolution in Module 6. The cell is the hinge of the whole course.
