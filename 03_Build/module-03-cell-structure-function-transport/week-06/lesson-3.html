⚡ PART 1 · THE ENERGY ORGANELLES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Mitochondria and chloroplasts each carry their own small loop of DNA, separate from the nucleus. Why might that be strange — and what could explain it? (No wrong answer — just reason it out.)

Releasing and capturing energy

The mitochondrion is the site of cellular respiration — it breaks down food to release usable energy as ATP. Its inner membrane folds into ridges called cristae, packing more working surface into a small space, and more surface means more energy-releasing reactions. The chloroplast, found only in plants and algae, is the site of photosynthesis: stacks of thylakoids (grana) trap sunlight, and the surrounding stroma builds sugar. Both have a double membrane and their own DNA — the evidence behind the endosymbiotic theory you met in Lesson 5.03, that these organelles descend from free-living bacteria taken inside a larger cell.

✨ FROM TODAY’S RESEARCH · GROWING TINY ORGANS
Once you understand how cells live and power themselves, a striking question follows: could we grow them into miniature versions of organs? Scientists are currently exploring organoids — tiny, simplified three-dimensional clusters of cells, sometimes only millimeters across, grown to mimic parts of a brain, gut, or kidney. Recent research suggests they may help model diseases and test candidate drugs. Hold this honestly: organoids are still early and far simpler than a real organ — they lack a full blood supply and many cell types — so the results are promising rather than settled. Treat the headlines the way you treat any new claim: with curiosity and care.
🧱 PART 2 · THE CYTOSKELETON

Scaffold and highway in one

A cell has no bones, yet it keeps its shape and moves materials around. The cytoskeleton — a network of protein fibers — does both. Microfilaments are thin fibers for shape and movement; intermediate filaments are rope-like fibers for support and anchoring; and microtubules are hollow tubes that serve as transport tracks and help move chromosomes during cell division. Together they are part scaffold, part highway.
