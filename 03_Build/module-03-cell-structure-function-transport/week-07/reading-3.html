Reading 7.03
Tonicity & the Red Blood Cell
Anchor Reading · Foundation for the Module 3 CER
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Now we put osmosis to work on a real cell. Drop a red blood cell into three different solutions and three different things happen — it swells, it shrivels, or it stays the same. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 7.03, and it is the foundation for your graded Module 3 CER. By the end you should be able to define hypotonic, hypertonic, and isotonic, predict how a cell behaves in each, and explain why intravenous (IV) fluids must match the blood.

Three words for three situations

Tonicity compares the solute concentration of a solution outside a cell to the concentration inside it. Because water moves by osmosis toward the higher solute concentration, tonicity tells you which way water will move — and therefore what will happen to the cell.

Outside solution Solute compared to cell Which way water moves
Hypotonic LOWER solute outside (more water outside) Water moves IN
Hypertonic HIGHER solute outside (less water outside) Water moves OUT
Isotonic EQUAL solute inside and out No net movement

A memory hook: hypo- = under (less solute outside); hyper- = over (more solute outside); iso- = equal. And always: water chases the solute.

The red blood cell in all three solutions

A red blood cell is an animal cell — it has a flexible membrane but no rigid cell wall to push back. So when water moves, the cell simply changes size and shape:

In a hypotonic solution (such as pure water), there is less solute outside than in, so water rushes in. The cell swells — and because it has no wall to resist, it can burst. The bursting of a red blood cell is called lysis (hemolysis).

In a hypertonic solution (such as very salty water), there is more solute outside, so water leaves the cell. It shrinks and puckers — this shriveling of a red blood cell is called crenation.

In an isotonic solution, solute is equal inside and out. Water still moves both ways, but equally, so there is no net change — the cell keeps its normal shape. This is the healthy, stable condition for a red blood cell.

Why a plant cell behaves differently

A plant cell has a tough cell wall outside its membrane — and this is exactly why the wall and the membrane are not the same thing (a point worth keeping straight). In a hypotonic solution a plant cell takes in water and presses firmly against its wall, becoming pleasantly turgid — this is what keeps a watered plant standing upright. The wall stops it from bursting. In a hypertonic solution the plant cell loses water and its membrane pulls away from the wall, a wilting condition called plasmolysis. Same osmosis, different outcome — because of one structural difference: the wall.

Why IV fluids must match your blood

This is not a textbook curiosity — it is a matter of life in any hospital. When fluid is given into a vein through an IV, it bathes your red blood cells directly. It must be isotonic to blood, which means roughly a 0.9% saline (salt) solution. Here is why the number matters:

If the IV fluid were too dilute (hypotonic — closer to pure water), water would rush into the red blood cells and they would swell and lyse. If it were too concentrated (hypertonic — too salty), water would leave the cells and they would shrivel (crenate). Either error damages the very cells that carry oxygen.

So the safe IV fluid is the one that matches the cells’ own interior — isotonic — so there is no net movement of water in or out, and the cells keep their shape. A single principle of osmosis, scaled up, becomes a rule that keeps patients alive.

✏️ THIS IS YOUR MODULE 3 CER
The graded writing assignment for this whole module is built directly on this reading. You will be asked to predict and justify how a red blood cell behaves in hypotonic, hypertonic, and isotonic solutions, and to use that reasoning to argue why an IV must be isotonic to blood. Read this page slowly and keep your notes — the claim, the evidence (which way water moves), and the reasoning (osmosis toward higher solute) are exactly what the CER will ask you to put in writing.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Tonicity is osmosis with the stakes made visible. The swelling, the shriveling, the steadiness — each is the membrane and the gradient from earlier this week, now playing out on a cell you can name. Hold the direction firmly (water chases the solute) and you can predict the fate of any cell in any solution. That predictive power is the heart of your CER, and a small triumph of perseverance: an invisible movement of water, reasoned out and put to use.
