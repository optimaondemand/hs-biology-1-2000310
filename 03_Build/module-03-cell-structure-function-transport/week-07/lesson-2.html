📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If a cell spends no energy, how does anything cross its membrane at all?
Nature has a free engine: particles in motion spread out from crowded places to empty ones. Today you will learn diffusion (particles moving down a gradient), osmosis (the diffusion of water), and the one direction that trips up nearly everyone — which way water actually moves.

2

3

🧱 PART 1 · PARTICLES SPREAD OUT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN

Diffusion, gradients, and equilibrium

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from higher to lower concentration — down the concentration gradient (a difference in concentration between two places). It needs no energy from the cell; the particles’ own random motion does the work. When particles are evenly spread and there is no longer net movement in one direction, the system has reached equilibrium — particles still move, but equally both ways. Across a membrane, small nonpolar molecules like oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse straight through the bilayer.

🔧 PART 2 · OSMOSIS — THE DIFFUSION OF WATER

Which way does water go?

Osmosis is diffusion with the spotlight on water: the movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane. Water can cross even when the dissolved solute cannot. The rule to lock in: water moves toward the side with MORE solute — the side with less free water — because that is the downhill direction for the water itself.

⚠️ CATCH THE MISTAKE · “WATER GOES WHERE THERE’S MORE WATER”
It is tempting to say water moves to where there is more water. That is backwards. Water moves toward the higher solute concentration — the saltier, more crowded side — because that side has less free water, and water flows toward where it is scarce. Keep the two gradients separate in your head: the solute is most concentrated on one side; the water is most concentrated on the other; water follows its own gradient, toward the solute.
