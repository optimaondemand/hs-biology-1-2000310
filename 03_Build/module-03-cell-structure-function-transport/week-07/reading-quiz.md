**1. (Reading 7.01 — fluid mosaic model)**
The cell membrane is described as a "fluid mosaic." What does the word *fluid* mean in this model?

- A) The membrane is made of liquid water
- B) The phospholipids and proteins drift sideways within the layer rather than being locked in place **✓**
- C) The membrane dissolves easily in water
- D) The membrane is permeable to every substance

*"Fluid" means the lipids and proteins move laterally through the membrane, like ships on a sea. "Mosaic" refers to the many proteins set among the lipids. The membrane is not made of water and is selectively, not freely, permeable.*

**2. (Reading 7.01 — bilayer self-assembly)**
Why do phospholipids arrange themselves into a bilayer with the tails pointing inward?

- A) The hydrophilic heads face the water and the hydrophobic tails hide from it **✓**
- B) The hydrophobic heads face the water and the tails hide from it
- C) The tails are positively charged and attract each other
- D) Cholesterol forces the phospholipids into two rows

*Each phospholipid is amphipathic: the hydrophilic phosphate head faces the watery surroundings on both sides, and the hydrophobic fatty-acid tails tuck inward, away from water. This self-assembly builds the bilayer of every cell membrane.*

**3. (Reading 7.02 — direction of osmosis)**
Two solutions are separated by a membrane that lets water pass but not the dissolved solute. Side A is 2% salt and Side B is 10% salt. In which direction will there be a net movement of water?

- A) From Side B toward Side A, because Side B has more water
- B) From Side A toward Side B, because Side B has the higher solute concentration **✓**
- C) There will be no net movement, because both sides contain water
- D) From Side B toward Side A, because water always moves toward lower solute

*Water moves by osmosis toward the side with the **higher solute** concentration (Side B), which is the side with less free water. The common error is thinking water moves toward "more water" — it moves toward more solute.*

**4. (Reading 7.02 — passive transport and energy)**
Diffusion and osmosis are both forms of passive transport. How much energy (ATP) must a cell spend to carry them out?

- A) None — the concentration gradient drives the movement **✓**
- B) One ATP for every particle that crosses
- C) A large amount, because crossing the membrane is difficult
- D) Energy is only needed for osmosis, not diffusion

*Passive transport requires no cellular energy because particles move down their concentration gradient, driven by their own random motion. Active transport, which moves substances against the gradient, is what requires ATP.*

**5. (Reading 7.03 — red blood cell in a hypotonic solution)**
A red blood cell is placed in a hypotonic solution (such as pure water). What will happen to it, and why?

- A) It will shrivel, because water moves out of the cell
- B) It will stay the same, because the solutions are balanced
- C) It will swell and may burst, because water moves into the cell **✓**
- D) It will swell, but its cell wall will prevent bursting

*In a hypotonic solution there is less solute outside than inside, so water moves into the cell by osmosis. Because a red blood cell has no cell wall, it swells and may burst (lyse). Animal cells have no wall — that answer choice is a trap.*

**6. (Reading 7.03 — IV-fluid reasoning)**
Why must intravenous (IV) fluids given to a patient be isotonic to blood (about 0.9% saline)?

- A) So that water rushes into the red blood cells and refills them
- B) So that there is no net movement of water into or out of the red blood cells **✓**
- C) Because salt kills bacteria in the bloodstream
- D) Because red blood cells need extra solute to function

*An isotonic fluid has the same solute concentration as the cells' interior, so there is no net water movement and the red blood cells keep their normal shape. A hypotonic (too dilute) fluid would make them lyse; a hypertonic (too concentrated) fluid would make them crenate.*

**7. (Reading 7.03 — cell wall vs. cell membrane)**
A plant cell and an animal cell are both placed in a hypotonic solution. The plant cell becomes firm (turgid) but does not burst, while the animal cell swells and may burst. What explains the difference?

- A) The plant cell has a rigid cell wall outside its membrane that resists bursting **✓**
- B) The plant cell has no membrane, so water cannot enter it
- C) The animal cell has a thicker membrane that traps more water
- D) Water moves into the animal cell but not into the plant cell

*Both cells take in water in a hypotonic solution, but the plant cell's rigid **cell wall** (outside the membrane) pushes back and prevents bursting, making the cell turgid. The animal cell, with only a membrane and no wall, can burst. The cell wall and cell membrane are not the same structure.*

**8. (Reading 7.04 — active transport and the Na⁺/K⁺ pump)**
The sodium-potassium pump moves 3 Na⁺ out of the cell and 2 K⁺ in, each cycle. This requires ATP. Why does this process need energy when diffusion does not?

- A) Because the ions are too large to fit through the membrane
- B) Because the pump moves the ions against their concentration gradients **✓**
- C) Because the membrane is impermeable to all ions
- D) Because the ions move down their gradients very quickly

*Active transport requires ATP because it moves substances **against** their concentration gradients — from where they are less concentrated to where they are more concentrated. Diffusion is free because it moves substances down their gradients.*

---

**9. (Reading 7.04 — passive vs. active / facilitated diffusion)**
Glucose crosses a cell membrane through a carrier protein, moving from where it is more concentrated to where it is less concentrated, and the cell spends no ATP. What type of transport is this?

- A) Active transport, because a protein is involved
- B) Facilitated diffusion, a form of passive transport **✓**
- C) Osmosis, because glucose dissolves in water
- D) Exocytosis, because the glucose leaves through a vesicle

*Facilitated diffusion uses a channel or carrier protein but is still **passive**: the substance moves down its gradient and no ATP is spent. Using a protein does not make transport active — moving against the gradient does.*
