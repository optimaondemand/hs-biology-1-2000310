📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If diffusion is free, why would a cell ever spend energy to move something across its membrane?
Because sometimes a cell must move a substance uphill, against its gradient — and that costs ATP. Today you will sort transport into passive and active: facilitated diffusion (downhill, with a protein door, no ATP), active transport (uphill, with ATP) and its famous sodium-potassium pump, and bulk transport for cargo too big for any door.

Facilitated diffusion vs. active transport

Some molecules — glucose, charged ions — cannot slip through the hydrophobic interior of the bilayer, so they cross through a channel or carrier protein shaped to admit them. Read “diffusion” carefully: facilitated diffusion is still passive. The molecule moves down its gradient and the cell spends no ATP; the protein is just a doorway. Active transport is the opposite: it moves a substance up its gradient, from scarce to crowded, and that requires ATP — the cell’s fuel (you’ll see how ATP is made in Module 4). The clean rule: down the gradient, no ATP; up the gradient, ATP required.

🔧 PART 2 · THE PUMP AND THE BIG STUFF

The sodium-potassium pump — and moving cargo in bulk

The most famous active-transport protein is the sodium-potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺ pump). Each cycle, powered by one ATP, it pushes three Na⁺ out and pulls two K⁺ in — both against their gradients, which is exactly why it must burn fuel. For cargo too large for any protein door — whole particles, big molecules — the cell uses bulk transport, wrapping the cargo in a membrane vesicle. Endocytosis brings material in (solid = phagocytosis, “cell eating”; liquid = pinocytosis, “cell drinking”); exocytosis ships material out by fusing a vesicle with the membrane.

🔗 LOOKING AHEAD · THE PUMP THAT FIRES A NERVE
By constantly moving sodium out and potassium in, the Na⁺/K⁺ pump builds an electrical difference across the membrane — the resting potential, a charged tension held ready. In Module 9, when you study the nervous system, this is exactly the setup a neuron uses: the resting potential built by this pump is what a nerve impulse releases and then resets. The transport you learn today becomes the basis of every thought and heartbeat later in the year.

Now sort the modes of transport.
