Passive Transport: Diffusion & Osmosis
Anchor Reading
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Some things cross the membrane for free — no energy spent by the cell. This is passive transport, and it runs on a simple rule of nature: particles spread out. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 7.02. By the end you should be able to define a concentration gradient, explain diffusion and osmosis, and — most important — say correctly which way water moves across a membrane.

Diffusion: particles spread out on their own

Open a bottle of perfume in one corner of a room and, given time, you will smell it across the room. No fan was needed. The scent molecules, in constant random motion, simply spread from where they are crowded toward where they are not. That is diffusion: the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration — that is, down the concentration gradient.

A concentration gradient is simply a difference in concentration between two places — a “downhill” for particles. Diffusion needs no energy from the cell, because it is powered by the particles’ own random motion. When the particles are evenly spread and there is no longer any net movement in one direction, the system has reached equilibrium. Particles still move, but just as many go each way, so the concentrations no longer change.

Across a membrane, small nonpolar molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse straight through the phospholipid bilayer, down their gradients — which is exactly how oxygen enters your cells and carbon dioxide leaves them.

Osmosis: the diffusion of water

Osmosis is just diffusion with the spotlight on one particular molecule — water. It is the movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane, from where water is more concentrated to where water is less concentrated. Water can cross the membrane (it slips through, and through channel proteins called aquaporins) even when a dissolved substance — the solute — cannot. So when there is more solute on one side, water moves to even things out.

Here is the sentence to fix in your mind: water moves toward the side with MORE solute (and therefore less free water). It can feel backwards at first, so say it carefully: water goes to the saltier, sugarier, more crowded side — because that is the side where water is scarcer.

⚠️ WATCH THIS · THE MOST COMMON OSMOSIS MISTAKE
Many students say “water moves to where there is more water.” That gets the direction backwards. Water moves toward the side with the higher SOLUTE concentration — the side with less free water — because that is the downhill direction for the water itself. Think of it from the water’s point of view: the crowded, salty side is where water is rarest, so that is where water flows. Keep both gradients straight: the solute is most concentrated on one side; the water is most concentrated on the other. Water follows its own gradient — toward the solute.

Why “passive” is the cell’s good fortune

Both diffusion and osmosis are forms of passive transport: the cell spends no energy to make them happen. The gradient does the work. This is a gift — a cell can move oxygen in, carbon dioxide out, and water where it is needed without burning a single unit of fuel, simply by letting nature run downhill.

But the same downhill rule has consequences a cell cannot ignore. If the water outside a cell is very different from the water inside, osmosis will move water in or out — sometimes dangerously so. That is the subject of the next reading, where a single red blood cell will swell, shrivel, or hold steady depending entirely on the solution around it.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Diffusion and osmosis are the simplest transport stories in biology — and among the most far-reaching. The same downhill movement of particles will explain how a leaf takes in carbon dioxide (Module 8), how nutrients cross your gut wall (Module 9), and how a paramecium holds its shape in pond water. Master the direction of the gradient now, especially for water, and the red-blood-cell puzzle in Reading 7.03 will already make sense.
