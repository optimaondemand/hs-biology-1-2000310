📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If a cell membrane is thinner than anything you can see, how can it decide what gets in and what stays out?
Every cell is wrapped in a membrane — a double layer of phospholipids, studded with proteins, and just fluid enough to work. Today you will see how the same amphipathic phospholipids from Module 2 build that wall on their own, what the phrase fluid mosaic really means, and how three kinds of membrane protein turn a wall into a smart, selectively permeable border.

From Module 2’s phospholipids to a living border

The cell membrane is built mainly from phospholipids — the amphipathic molecules you met in Module 2. Each has a hydrophilic phosphate head (water-loving) and two hydrophobic fatty-acid tails (water-fearing). Surrounded by water inside and out, they arrange themselves with no instruction: heads turn outward toward the water on both faces, tails tuck inward away from it. The result is a phospholipid bilayer — two sheets, tail to tail. This is form following function at its purest: the chemistry of the molecule builds the wall by itself.

🔧 PART 2 · FLUID, MOSAIC, AND SELECTIVE

Why “fluid mosaic” — and what the proteins do

Fluid: the lipids and proteins are not nailed down — they drift sideways like ships on a sea, and cholesterol tucked among the tails keeps the membrane from getting too loose when warm or too stiff when cold. Mosaic: the surface is a patchwork of many proteins set among the lipids. And here Module 2 returns: each protein works because it is folded into exactly the right shape. Channel proteins form a tunnel for specific particles; carrier (transport) proteins bind a molecule and change shape to ferry it across; receptor proteins have a binding site shaped to catch one signaling molecule. Together they make the membrane selectively permeable — it chooses what crosses.
