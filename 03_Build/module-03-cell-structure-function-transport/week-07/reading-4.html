Active Transport & Bulk Transport
Anchor Reading
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
So far, things have crossed the membrane the easy way — downhill, for free. But cells often need to move a substance uphill, against its gradient, and that costs energy. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 7.04. By the end you should be able to distinguish facilitated diffusion from active transport, explain the sodium-potassium pump, and describe bulk transport for things too big to fit through any protein.

Facilitated diffusion: passive, but with help

Some molecules need to cross the membrane but cannot slip through the hydrophobic interior on their own — glucose, for example, and charged ions are too large or too polar for the bilayer. For these, the membrane offers a helper: a channel or carrier protein shaped to let that specific molecule through. This is facilitated diffusion.

Read the word “diffusion” carefully: this is still passive transport. The molecule moves down its concentration gradient, and the cell spends no energy. The protein simply provides a doorway; the gradient still does the pushing. Facilitated diffusion is selective transport without a fuel bill.

Active transport: pushing uphill costs energy

Sometimes a cell must move a substance from where it is scarce to where it is already crowded — against the concentration gradient. Nature does not do this for free. Active transport moves a substance up its gradient, and to do so it must spend energy — the cell’s energy currency, ATP. (You will meet how ATP is made in Module 4; for now, treat it as the cell’s fuel.)

Here is the clean contrast worth memorizing: passive transport (diffusion, osmosis, facilitated diffusion) goes down the gradient and needs no ATP; active transport goes up the gradient and requires ATP. Direction tells you the cost.

The sodium-potassium pump

The most famous active-transport protein is the sodium-potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺ pump). With each cycle, powered by one molecule of ATP, it pushes three sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell and pulls two potassium ions (K⁺) in. Both movements are against the ions’ gradients — which is exactly why the pump must burn ATP to do it.

The pump is a carrier protein that does its job by changing shape each cycle — form following function once more — binding the ions on one side and releasing them on the other.

🔗 LOOKING AHEAD · THE PUMP THAT FIRES A NERVE
This humble pump is not just bookkeeping for ions. By constantly moving sodium out and potassium in, it builds an electrical difference across the membrane — the resting potential — a tiny charged tension held ready to fire. In Module 9, when you study the nervous system, this is exactly the setup a neuron uses: the resting potential built by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump is what a nerve impulse releases and resets. The transport idea you learn this week becomes the basis of every thought and heartbeat later in the year.

Bulk transport: moving the big stuff

Some materials are simply too large to pass through any channel or carrier — whole food particles, big molecules, even other cells. For these the membrane uses bulk transport, wrapping the cargo in a pocket of membrane called a vesicle. Because it moves large amounts and reshapes the membrane, bulk transport also requires energy.

Endocytosis brings material in: the membrane folds inward and pinches off a vesicle. When the cargo is solid (a cell “eating”), it is called phagocytosis; when it is liquid (a cell “drinking”), pinocytosis. Exocytosis is the reverse — a vesicle inside the cell fuses with the membrane and dumps its contents out, which is how cells export proteins and signals.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

You now have the full toolkit for how anything crosses a membrane: down the gradient for free (diffusion, osmosis, facilitated diffusion), up the gradient for a fee (active transport), or wrapped in a vesicle (bulk transport). This single membrane — built from Module 2’s phospholipids, studded with Module 2’s proteins — is the gatekeeper of every cell you will study from here on, from a leaf taking in minerals to the neuron that will carry a signal in Module 9. The wonder is that so much of life turns on which way a particle is allowed to move.
