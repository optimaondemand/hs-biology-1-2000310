Reading 5.02
Two Kinds of Cell, and Why Cells Stay Small
Anchor Reading
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Cell theory tells us every living thing is made of cells. But not all cells are built the same way. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 5.02. By the end you should be able to tell a prokaryotic cell from a eukaryotic one, and explain a surprising rule that governs all of them: why cells stay small. That rule comes down to a simple comparison — surface area versus volume.

Prokaryotes and eukaryotes

Every cell on Earth falls into one of two great groups, set apart by one feature: whether the cell keeps its DNA inside a membrane-bound nucleus.

A prokaryotic cell has no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles. Its DNA floats freely in a region called the nucleoid. Prokaryotes are generally small and relatively simple, and they include the two domains of single-celled life, Bacteria and Archaea. (The word literally means “before the kernel” — before the nucleus.) These were almost certainly the first cells to appear on Earth.

A eukaryotic cell has a true nucleus that wraps its DNA in a membrane, plus a set of membrane-bound organelles — little “organs” that each do a specialized job. Eukaryotic cells are generally larger and more complex. Animals, plants, fungi, and protists are all made of eukaryotic cells. (“Eukaryote” means “true kernel.”)

A common mix-up: the wall is not the membrane

Be careful with two structures that sound alike but are not. The cell membrane (also called the plasma membrane) is the thin, flexible phospholipid bilayer that surrounds every cell — it is the gatekeeper that controls what enters and leaves. The cell wall is a stiff outer layer that sits outside the membrane and gives shape and support.

Here is the key point students often get wrong: every cell has a membrane, but not every cell has a wall. Plant cells, fungi, and most bacteria have walls; animal cells do not. So a cell wall is never a safe way to tell a cell is alive or to tell prokaryote from eukaryote — both groups include cells with walls and the membrane is the universal feature.

Why cells stay small

A cell must constantly trade materials with its surroundings — food and oxygen in, waste out — and everything crosses through the membrane at the cell’s surface. So the membrane’s job depends on having enough surface for the size of the cell it must serve.

Here is the catch. As a cell grows, its volume (the amount of living material that needs feeding) grows much faster than its surface area (the membrane available to feed it). Double a cube’s width and its surface area goes up four times — but its volume goes up eight times. The inside outruns the outside.

Cube side Surface area Volume SA : V ratio
1 unit 6 1 6 : 1 (high)
2 units 24 8 3 : 1
3 units 54 27 2 : 1 (low)

When the SA:V ratio gets too low, the membrane can no longer move materials fast enough to keep the whole interior supplied, and the center starves. This is why cells stay small — and why a cell about to grow too large will instead divide into two, restoring a high SA:V ratio in each daughter cell. Form follows function: the surface must keep up with the volume it serves.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

The surface-area-to-volume rule is one of biology’s quiet master ideas. It explains the smallness of cells here, but it will return again and again: it is why your lungs and small intestine are folded into enormous surfaces, and why a leaf is thin and flat (Module 8). Whenever an exchange has to happen across a surface, life maximizes that surface. Hold onto this — it is the same logic at every scale of the body.
