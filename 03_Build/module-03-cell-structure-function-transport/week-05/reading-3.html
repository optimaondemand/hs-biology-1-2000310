Reading 5.03
The Cell That Swallowed a Cell
Anchor Reading
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Where did the complex eukaryotic cell come from? One of biology’s most astonishing answers is that two of its organelles were once free-living cells of their own. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 5.03. By the end you should be able to explain endosymbiotic theory, list the four lines of evidence that support it, and reason about how good that evidence really is.

An ancient partnership

The endosymbiotic theory proposes that two of the eukaryotic cell’s most important organelles — the mitochondrion (the site of cellular respiration) and the chloroplast (the site of photosynthesis) — began as independent prokaryotes. Long ago, a larger host cell engulfed a smaller bacterium but did not digest it. Instead, the two began to live together: the engulfed cell kept making energy, and the host gave it shelter. Over vast spans of time the inner cell became a permanent organelle. The word says it plainly: endo- (inside) + symbiosis (living together).

Mitochondria descend from an engulfed respiring bacterium; chloroplasts, found in plants and algae, descend from an engulfed photosynthetic bacterium. If this is true, then deep inside every plant and animal cell are the descendants of ancient bacteria, still doing the jobs they did billions of years ago.

Four lines of evidence

A bold claim needs strong evidence. Endosymbiotic theory rests on four observations, each pointing to the same conclusion — that these organelles were once cells:

Evidence Why it points to an ancient free-living cell
Their own circular DNA Mitochondria and chloroplasts carry their own small loop of DNA — separate from the nucleus, and shaped like a bacterium’s DNA, not the cell’s own.
Their own ribosomes They build their own proteins on ribosomes that resemble a bacterium’s ribosomes more than the cell’s own.
A double membrane Two membranes wrap each one — just what you’d expect if the host’s membrane folded around an engulfed cell that already had a membrane of its own.
They divide by binary fission They reproduce on their own by splitting in two — the same way bacteria do — rather than being built fresh by the cell.

Notice how this works as an argument: no single clue proves the theory, but four independent clues all pointing the same way make it very hard to explain any other way.

🏛️ A HUMAN STORY — REJECTED, THEN VINDICATED
When the American biologist Lynn Margulis proposed the modern endosymbiotic theory in 1967, the scientific establishment was not ready for it. The idea that organelles had once been independent bacteria struck many as wild, and her landmark paper was reportedly turned away by some fifteen journals before one finally published it. For years the theory sat at the edges of biology. But Margulis kept gathering evidence and kept making her case. Then the tools caught up: when scientists were able to sequence the DNA inside mitochondria and chloroplasts, they found it was unmistakably bacterial in character — just as she had predicted. The theory she had defended against the consensus is now in every biology textbook. Her story is this module’s clearest lesson in perseverance: an idea is not wrong because it is unwelcome, and a scientist who follows the evidence may have to wait, patiently, for the rest of the field to catch up.

Why this sets up Module 4

Hold onto these two organelles — you are about to spend three weeks inside them. In Module 4 (Cell Energy), the chloroplast is where photosynthesis captures sunlight into sugar, and the mitochondrion is where respiration releases that energy to power the cell. Endosymbiosis is not a side story: it explains why the cell’s two power plants look and behave like ancient bacteria, and it is the historical root of the energy chemistry you will study next.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Endosymbiotic theory is a model case of how science actually works: a startling claim, met with doubt, slowly winning out as independent lines of evidence converge. In your lesson you will practice doing exactly what scientists did — laying out the claim, weighing the evidence, and reasoning your way to a conclusion. That is the heart of claim, evidence, and reasoning, the writing skill at the center of this course.
