📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Could two of your cell’s organelles have once been free-living bacteria — and how would we ever know?
Today you meet a startling claim: the mitochondrion and the chloroplast began as independent prokaryotes that were engulfed and never digested. This is endosymbiotic theory. You will learn its four lines of evidence, meet the scientist who fought for it, and practice the move at the heart of this course — reasoning from evidence to a claim.

🦠 PART 1 · AN ANCIENT PARTNERSHIP
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Imagine someone told you that part of your own cells used to be free-living bacteria. What’s your honest first reaction? (No wrong answer.)

Endo- (inside) + symbiosis (living together)

The endosymbiotic theory proposes that the mitochondrion (the site of respiration) and the chloroplast (the site of photosynthesis) began as free-living prokaryotes. Long ago, a larger host cell engulfed a smaller bacterium but did not digest it; the two began to live together, and over time the inner cell became a permanent organelle. Mitochondria descend from an engulfed respiring bacterium; chloroplasts, in plants and algae, from an engulfed photosynthetic bacterium. If the theory is true, then ancient bacteria still live, transformed, inside every plant and animal cell — doing the same jobs they did billions of years ago.

🔬 PART 2 · FOUR LINES OF EVIDENCE

Four independent clues, one conclusion

A bold claim needs strong support. Endosymbiotic theory rests on four observations, each pointing the same way: these organelles have their own circular DNA (separate from the nucleus and bacterium-shaped); their own ribosomes (more bacterial than the cell’s own); a double membrane (just what you’d expect if the host’s membrane wrapped around an already-membraned cell); and the ability to divide by binary fission, the way bacteria do. No single clue is decisive — but four independent clues converging on the same answer make any other explanation very hard to defend.

🔬 A HUMAN STORY · REJECTED FIFTEEN TIMES, THEN VINDICATED
When the American biologist Lynn Margulis proposed the modern endosymbiotic theory in 1967, most of her field thought it was too strange to be true, and her landmark paper was reportedly rejected by about fifteen journals before one accepted it. For years the idea lingered at the margins of biology. But Margulis kept gathering evidence and kept making the case. Then the tools caught up: when scientists were finally able to sequence the DNA inside mitochondria and chloroplasts, it turned out to be unmistakably bacterial — exactly as she had predicted. The theory she defended against the consensus is now in every textbook. Hers is this module’s clearest lesson in perseverance: an idea is not wrong merely because it is unwelcome, and a scientist who follows the evidence may simply have to wait for everyone else to catch up.

✏️ PART 3 · CER PRACTICE (IN-LESSON, NOT GRADED)

Build the argument yourself

Scientists do not just collect facts — they build arguments from them. The pattern you will use all year is Claim → Evidence → Reasoning: state what you think is true, give the observations that support it, and explain why those observations lead to that claim. Practice it now on endosymbiosis. Write your answer in your notebook; this is rehearsal, not a graded essay.

🧩 CER PRACTICE PROMPT
“Mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free-living prokaryotes.” Does the evidence support this claim? Write a short CER paragraph (4–6 sentences).
Claim: State whether the evidence supports endosymbiotic theory.
Evidence: Name at least two of the four lines of evidence (circular DNA, own ribosomes, double membrane, binary fission).
Reasoning: Explain why each piece you chose points to an ancient free-living cell — what would a bacterium have that a normal organelle would not?
⚡ SELF-CHECK · HOW STRONG IS YOUR REASONING?
After writing, rate how clearly your paragraph explains why the evidence supports the claim (not just lists it).

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

Endosymbiosis is a model of how science really works: a startling claim, met with doubt, slowly winning out as independent evidence converges. The reasoning you practiced today — claim, evidence, why — is the same skill your graded module essay will demand, and the same patience that carried Margulis from rejection to vindication. That is the perseverance at this module’s heart.
