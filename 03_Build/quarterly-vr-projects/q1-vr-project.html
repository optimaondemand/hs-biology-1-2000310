🧩 WHAT THIS PROJECT IS
This is where the first nine weeks of the course come together in your hands. You have argued like a scientist (Module 1), you have learned why water’s bent shape and the four macromolecules make life chemically possible (Module 2), and you have studied the cell — its organelles, its membrane, and how things move across that membrane (Module 3). Now you will build a single, walkable eukaryotic cell in VR whose organelles are annotated all the way down to the molecular level, and you will record a short guided tour explaining how what the cell does follows from what it is made of.
The big question your project answers: “How does a cell’s structure — from its water and molecules up to its whole organelles — make its life possible?” This is the course’s first move up the microscopic→macroscopic spine: atoms → molecules → organelles → cell.
🧠 WHY THIS IS A VR PROJECT (NOT A POSTER)
A textbook cell diagram is flat and roughly one scale. A real cell is a crowded three-dimensional space, and its parts span an enormous range of scale — a water molecule is millions of times smaller than the whole cell. VR lets you do the one thing a diagram cannot: walk inside the cell at organelle scale, then zoom down to build the membrane out of individual phospholipids, and hold both scales in the same experience. You are not drawing a cell; you are constructing one you can stand inside and defend. That scale-shift is the subject.
🔨 WHAT YOU WILL BUILD — FOUR REQUIRED STATIONS
Working inside the provided Cell Biology Lab Location, you will assemble one annotated eukaryotic cell with these four stations. Every station is labeled with sticky notes that connect structure to function.
1
The molecular foundation. Place a water molecule model showing its bent shape and partial charges, and one example of each of the four macromolecules (carbohydrate, lipid, protein, nucleic acid). Sticky-note how water’s polarity makes it the cell’s solvent.
2
The membrane, built from molecules. Assemble a patch of the phospholipid bilayer from individual phospholipid IFX (heads out, tails in) and embed at least one transport protein. Sticky-note why the bilayer forms this way in water and how it makes the membrane selectively permeable.
3
The organelles, correctly placed. Build and label at least six organelles (nucleus, mitochondrion, ribosomes, rough & smooth ER, Golgi apparatus, and one more of your choice). Each sticky note must name the organelle and its job — structure and function together.
4
The transport demonstration. Set up a small osmosis station: a cell (or membrane patch) with sticky notes predicting what happens in hypotonic, hypertonic, and isotonic surroundings. Connect it to your Module 3 CER reasoning.

🎓 PORTFOLIO ARTIFACT & THE VIRTUE THIS PRACTICES · WONDER
You keep your spatial-recording tour and named snapshots as the first VR artifact in your Biology portfolio — the piece that shows you can build and defend a scientific model. And it is a fitting place for the module’s virtue, wonder: the same atoms found in the stars, arranged into water and macromolecules, assemble themselves into a living cell. Standing inside one you built yourself is a way of taking that seriously.
🎓 FACILITATOR NOTE
This project crystallizes nine weeks of weekly VR builds — students should be assembling, not inventing. Weeks 3–7 benefit from a short lab demonstrating how to clone and lock IFX and how to record a clean spatial tour. Common sticking points: students place organelles decoratively rather than by function (push them to justify each placement), and they skip the molecular scale (Station 1–2) because it is harder — hold the line on the bilayer-from-phospholipids requirement, since the scale-shift is the point of doing this in VR. The Week-9 gallery walk is the universal public-presentation mechanism; solo students walk the same assembled gallery and submit the same reflection.
