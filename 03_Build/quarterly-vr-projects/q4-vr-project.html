Florida Ecosystem Energy-Flow Walk
Assigned Week 28 · due end of Week 35 (before the Week-36 Final) · counts toward the Laboratory & Notebooks category (20%)
🌴 WHAT THIS PROJECT IS
This is the last VR project of the year, and it closes the course’s one long argument. This quarter you studied the human body as a system that maintains itself (Module 9) and the ecosystems organisms live inside (Module 10). Your capstone builds a walkable Florida ecosystem that shows energy flowing through it — and connects the organism to the environment by including a working human-homeostasis station. It is the top of the microscopic→macroscopic spine you started at a water molecule in Week 3.
The big question your walk answers: “How does energy flow and matter cycle through a Florida ecosystem — and how does the same logic of regulation that keeps a single body alive scale up to keep an ecosystem in balance?”
🧠 WHY THIS IS A VR PROJECT
Energy flow and matter cycling are movements through a place — energy climbing a trophic pyramid and mostly leaving as heat, carbon looping from air to producer to consumer and back. A walkable ecosystem lets a visitor physically follow those flows and feel the 10% rule as the pyramid narrows above them. VR also lets you set a human-scale homeostasis station inside the same landscape, so the “body as regulated system” and “ecosystem as regulated system” ideas sit side by side and can be compared. No flat diagram carries both scales and both flows at once the way a walk does.
🔨 WHAT YOU WILL BUILD — FOUR REQUIRED STATIONS
Working inside the provided Florida Ecosystem Location (Everglades, coral reef, or mangrove — your choice), compose these four stations, annotated with sticky notes and Whiteboard IFX.
1
The trophic energy pyramid. Build a food web for your chosen Florida ecosystem, then stack it into an energy pyramid: producers → primary → secondary → tertiary consumers. Sticky-note the 10% rule — why only about a tenth of the energy passes to each level, and where the rest goes (heat). Connect the base back to photosynthesis (Module 4): producers capture the energy the whole web runs on.
2
A biogeochemical cycle. Model the carbon cycle (or nitrogen or water cycle) with visible flows through your ecosystem — atmosphere, producers, consumers, decomposers, and back. Sticky-note at least one point where humans disrupt the cycle (fossil-fuel CO₂, eutrophication).
3
The human-homeostasis station. Build one negative-feedback loop (blood-glucose or body-temperature regulation): stimulus → receptor → control center → response → return to set point. Sticky-note how this “regulate around a set point” logic is the same idea that keeps your ecosystem’s populations in balance — the organism↔environment connection.
4
The stewardship station. Add one Florida conservation issue — an invasive species (python, lionfish), bioaccumulation up the web (DDT), or habitat loss. Sticky-note the evidence and one responsible human response. This is where the course’s closing virtue lives.
🌱 THE CAPSTONE MOVE — CLOSE THE SPINE
Somewhere in your walk, place a short “atoms → ecosystems” marker that names the journey: the water and carbon cycling through your ecosystem are the same atoms you built into a molecule in Q1; the energy in your pyramid was captured by photosynthesis; the species in your food web are related by the common descent you argued in Q2 and classified in Q3. Your tour should end by saying, in one or two sentences, how the whole year’s argument arrives here.

🎓 PORTFOLIO ARTIFACT & THE VIRTUE THIS PRACTICES · RESPONSIBILITY
Your capstone ecosystem walk is the culminating VR artifact of the year — the piece that shows you can hold the entire course, atoms to ecosystems, in one built place. The closing virtue is responsibility: stewardship, Carson’s land ethic, the common good. A walk that names a real Florida conservation issue and a responsible human response is not just a summary of ecology — it is the student taking a position on how we ought to live inside the household of life.
🎓 FACILITATOR NOTE
This is the capstone — the most common failure is a beautiful ecosystem with no flow: students place organisms but never show energy moving or the pyramid narrowing. Hold the line on the 10% rule and on visible cycle arrows. The homeostasis station is where the organism↔ecosystem analogy lives; a Week-28 lab revisiting negative feedback (glucose or temperature) helps students who conflate it with positive feedback. The “atoms→ecosystems” capstone marker matters: it is where the year’s spine becomes explicit and it feeds directly into the Week-36 Final review. The Week-35 gallery walk is the universal public moment — and a fitting close to four quarters of building. Solo students walk the same assembled gallery and submit the same reflection. Because the Final is Week 36, the project is due end of Week 35 with no build during exam week.
