📚 WHAT THIS WEEK IS
No new biology this week — just the chance to see the whole year as one picture.
At the midterm you walked the first half of the spine — from how we know to inherited information. Since then the argument has kept growing outward in scale: the heritable variation you studied became the raw material of evolution; evolution produced the ordered diversity of life; that diversity includes the plants that feed the living world and the human body that holds itself in balance; and all of it, finally, lives inside ecosystems of flowing energy and cycling matter. This page is the grand tour of the entire year — all ten modules and the bridges between them. Read it slowly, do the two check-ins, complete the Walk the Whole Spine review activity, then sit the final. The exam rewards students who see the connections, not just isolated facts.
🧬 THE SPINE OF THE WHOLE YEAR
M1 · How we know → M2 · Atoms & molecules → M3 · The cell → M4 · Energy → M5 · Information & heredity → M6 · Evolution → M7 · Diversity of life → M8 · Plants → M9 · The human body → M10 · Ecosystems
Each link makes the next one possible. Chemistry explains how cells can exist; cells explain how energy is captured and passed on; molecular genetics explains how life reproduces and varies; that variation drives evolution; evolution produces the diversity we classify; and every organism, from plant to person, lives inside an ecosystem where energy flows and matter cycles. Module 1 sits above the whole chain — the method you used to learn every link, and the CER reasoning behind every assessment, including this exam.

The Grand Tour — All Ten Modules
Each stop: the big idea, the must-know facts, and the bridge that carries you to the next module.
● SEMESTER 1 — ATOMS TO HEREDITY (reviewed in full at the midterm)
🔭 MODULE 1 · NATURE OF SCIENCE — the lens
Big idea: science earns trust through evidence, testing, and revision.

Must know: observation vs. inference; a fair test has one independent variable, a measured dependent variable, controlled variables, and a control group. A hypothesis is a testable proposed explanation; a theory is a broad, well-supported explanation (not “a guess that grew up”); a law describes what happens while a theory explains why. CER — Claim, Evidence, Reasoning — is how you argue from data.

→ The bridge: the lens for every unit that follows — and the CER framework is the tool behind all three final short-responses.
🧪 MODULE 2 · CHEMISTRY OF LIFE
Big idea: life is built from atoms and molecules, and structure determines function.

Must know: water’s bent, polar shape and hydrogen bonding give cohesion and surface tension, a high specific heat that moderates temperature, floating ice, and its power as the universal solvent. The four macromolecules — carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids — are built by dehydration synthesis and broken by hydrolysis. The deepest rule: a protein’s function comes from its folded shape (denaturation changes both).

→ The bridge: phospholipids and proteins become the membrane and enzymes of M3–M4; the shape→function rule returns for sickle cell (M5) and for antibodies (M9).
🧤 MODULE 3 · CELL STRUCTURE, FUNCTION & TRANSPORT
Big idea: the cell is life’s basic unit, and its membrane controls what enters and leaves.

Must know: the three tenets of cell theory; prokaryotic vs. eukaryotic; organelles by function; the fluid-mosaic membrane is a phospholipid bilayer studded with proteins. Diffusion and osmosis are passive (high→low); osmosis moves water. A cell swells in a hypotonic solution, shrivels in a hypertonic one, holds steady in an isotonic one (why IV fluid is 0.9% saline). Active transport (the Na⁺/K⁺ pump) spends ATP to move against the gradient.

→ The bridge: the chloroplast and mitochondrion are where M4’s energy reactions happen; the Na⁺/K⁺ pump returns as the neuron’s resting potential (M9); endosymbiosis is a line of evidence for evolution (M6).
⚡ MODULE 4 · CELL ENERGY
Big idea: cells capture, store, and release energy — energy flows, matter cycles.

Must know: ATP is the energy currency. Photosynthesis (chloroplast) uses light to build glucose: light-dependent reactions in the thylakoid make ATP/NADPH and release O₂ from splitting water; the Calvin cycle in the stroma builds sugar and needs no direct light. Cellular respiration (glycolysis → Krebs → electron transport chain) releases that energy; O₂ is the final electron acceptor. The two are not simple reverses — plants do both.

→ The bridge: the carbon atoms moving through these reactions travel the global carbon cycle (M10); this energy capture is what makes plants (M8) the foundation of ecosystems.
🧬 MODULE 5 · DNA, CELL DIVISION & GENETICS
Big idea: information is stored, copied, expressed — and inherited.

Must know: DNA is a double helix with A–T, C–G pairing; replication is semiconservative. Mitosis makes two identical cells; meiosis makes four unique gametes (crossing over + independent assortment = variation). The central dogma: DNA is transcribed to mRNA (nucleus) and translated to protein (ribosome), three-letter codons at a time. A single base substitution can change one amino acid, change a protein’s shape, and cause disease — sickle cell (GAG→GUG). Mendel’s ratios, Punnett squares, and pedigrees predict inheritance.

→ The bridge: the heritable variation that meiosis and mutation create is exactly the raw material natural selection acts on — the doorway into Semester 2.
● SEMESTER 2 — VARIATION TO THE LIVING PLANET
🦟 MODULE 6 · EVOLUTION AND NATURAL SELECTION
Big idea: species share common ancestors, and natural selection produces adaptation.

Must know: independent lines of evidence converge — the fossil record, comparative anatomy (homologous, analogous, vestigial), biogeography, and molecular sequence homology. Natural selection needs variation, heredity, and differential reproduction; fitness means reproductive success, not “strongest.” Other mechanisms: genetic drift (founder, bottleneck), gene flow, mutation as the source of variation. Antibiotic resistance is evolution observed in real time.

→ The bridge: common descent produces a nested pattern of relatedness — and that pattern is exactly what classification (M7) is built to capture.
🌳 MODULE 7 · CLASSIFICATION AND DIVERSITY OF LIFE
Big idea: we classify life to reflect its evolutionary history.

Must know: the Linnaean hierarchy (Domain → Species) and binomial nomenclature; dichotomous keys; cladograms built from shared derived characters and outgroups. The three-domain system — Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya — came from molecular sequence data, which revealed relationships that body shape alone had hidden. Surveying the domains and kingdoms (bacteria, protists, fungi, plants, animals) and the debate over whether viruses are alive.

→ The bridge: two of those surveyed groups get their own modules next — the plants (M8) and the animal body (M9) — and every group plays an ecological role (M10).
🌿 MODULE 8 · PLANT BIOLOGY
Big idea: plants are the photosynthetic foundation of terrestrial ecosystems.

Must know: three tissue systems (dermal, ground, vascular); roots, stems, and leaf anatomy tie structure to function. Xylem carries water upward by the cohesion-tension mechanism, driven by transpiration through stomata guarded by guard cells; phloem moves sugar by pressure-flow. Plants sense and respond without nerves — tropisms and the hormone auxin bend a shoot toward light.

→ The bridge: plant photosynthesis (back to M4) is the energy that enters nearly every food web (M10); tropisms and hormones parallel the body’s endocrine signaling (M9) — convergent solutions to the same problem.
🧠 MODULE 9 · HUMAN BODY SYSTEMS
Big idea: organ systems cooperate to maintain homeostasis.

Must know: levels of organization (cell → tissue → organ → system → organism); homeostasis held by negative feedback (temperature, glucose). Systems interlock: digestive enzymes (protein shape, M2) break down food; the heart and vessels transport it; the lungs exchange gases for respiration (M4); the neuron fires an action potential using the Na⁺/K⁺ pump (callback to M3), and drugs act at the synapse; the immune system uses antibodies (shape→function again) and memory, the basis of vaccines.

→ The bridge: a body that regulates itself is still one organism among many — a single node in the webs of energy and matter that make up an ecosystem (M10).
🌎 MODULE 10 · ECOLOGY AND ENVIRONMENTAL SCIENCE
Big idea: energy flows and matter cycles through ecosystems — and humans are part of them.

Must know: producers, consumers, decomposers; food chains and webs; energy pyramids and the 10% rule (energy flows one way, matter cycles). Biogeochemical cycles — carbon (links straight back to M4), nitrogen, water, phosphorus. Population growth (exponential vs. logistic), carrying capacity, and succession. Biodiversity, invasive species, conservation, and the three independent lines of evidence for anthropogenic climate change.

→ The bridge closes the loop: the carbon cycle is the cell’s chemistry (M4) written at planet scale, and the responsibility to steward it rests on understanding every link that came before.

📝 WHAT’S ON THE FINAL
The Week-36 Final is built in the same EOC format as your weekly quizzes, and it is cumulative — it draws from the whole year. Every multiple-choice question comes straight from a weekly quiz, so nothing on it is new.
70 multiple-choice
Spread across all ten modules, weighted by module size, and balanced to match the EOC domains (molecular/cellular, classification/evolution, ecology, and human anatomy). Re-do your weekly quizzes — especially from Semester 2, which wasn’t on the midterm — to study.
3 CER short-responses
One from each of the year’s three great domains: (1) molecular — trace a carbon atom through photosynthesis and respiration; (2) genetic/evolutionary — mutation → variation → common descent; (3) ecological — evidence for climate change, or energy flow and the 10% rule. Claim · Evidence · Reasoning — explain the mechanism.

If you can walk the whole spine in your own words — how we know → atoms and water → the cell and its membrane → capturing and releasing energy → copying and inheriting information → the variation evolution acts on → the ordered diversity it produces → the plants that feed the world → the body that holds itself in balance → the ecosystems that hold us all — then you understand biology, because you understand how each idea makes the next one possible.
