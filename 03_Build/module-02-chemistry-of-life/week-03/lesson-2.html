📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If a single hydrogen bond is so weak, how can it let an insect walk on water and lift a column of water to the top of a tree?
Yesterday you learned that water is polar. Today you will see what that polarity does. Polar water molecules reach out and grip one another with hydrogen bonds — each one feeble, but billions together are mighty. From those bonds come cohesion, adhesion, and surface tension: the reasons water beads, climbs, and holds a skin strong enough to carry a water strider.

📚 PART 1 · THE WEAK BOND THAT RUNS THE WORLD
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Picture an insect standing on the surface of a pond without sinking. What do you think holds it up? (No wrong answer — just check in with yourself.)

A weak grip, repeated a billion times

Because water is polar, the δ+ hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the δ− oxygen of a neighbor. That attraction is a hydrogen bond. Any single hydrogen bond is weak — far weaker than the covalent bonds inside the molecule — and it is constantly breaking and reforming as molecules jostle. But there are so many of them, all at once, that collectively they are remarkably strong. This is the secret behind everything in today’s lesson: not one mighty bond, but countless gentle ones, holding water together.

💧 PART 2 · COHESION, ADHESION, AND A SKIN OF WATER

Sticking to itself, sticking to others

Cohesion is water sticking to water — molecules holding one another by hydrogen bonds. At the surface, where molecules can only bond inward and sideways, this cohesion pulls the surface tight into a film: surface tension, the “skin” a water strider stands on. Cohesion also lets water travel as an unbroken column — the same property that helps a tree lift water from its roots all the way to its highest leaf, a story you will follow in detail in Module 8.

Adhesion is water sticking to other polar surfaces. It is why water climbs the wall of a thin tube or creeps up a paper towel — capillary action. In a tree, adhesion to the walls of narrow vessels works alongside cohesion to keep that water column from slipping back down. Cohesion and adhesion are partners: one binds water to itself, the other anchors it to its surroundings.

🔄 TAP TO FLIP
Cohesion
Water sticking to water through hydrogen bonds. Example: raindrops pull themselves into round beads on a windshield.
🔄 TAP TO FLIP
Adhesion
Water sticking to other polar surfaces. Example: water creeping upward into a dry paper towel dipped at its edge.
🔄 TAP TO FLIP
Surface tension
Cohesion at the air–water boundary, pulling the surface into a tight film. Example: a water strider walking across a pond without breaking through.
🔄 TAP TO FLIP
Capillary action
Water climbing a narrow space because adhesion (to the walls) and cohesion (to itself) work together. Example: water rising in a thin glass tube against gravity.
