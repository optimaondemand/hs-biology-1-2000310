Reading 3.02
Hydrogen Bonds: Cohesion, Adhesion & Surface Tension
Anchor Reading
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
This is the anchor reading for Lesson 3.02. In 3.01 you learned that water is bent and therefore polar. Today you will see what polar molecules do when there are billions of them together: they reach out and grab one another with a weak attraction called the hydrogen bond. From that one humble attraction come cohesion, adhesion, and surface tension — the reasons an insect can stand on a pond and water can climb to the top of a tall tree.

A weak attraction with enormous consequences

Recall that each water molecule has a slightly negative oxygen end (δ−) and slightly positive hydrogen ends (δ+). When two water molecules drift near each other, the δ+ hydrogen of one is attracted to the δ− oxygen of its neighbor. That attraction is a hydrogen bond — not a true covalent bond that shares electrons, but a gentle electrical pull between oppositely charged ends of separate molecules.

A single hydrogen bond is feeble — far weaker than the covalent bonds holding one molecule together. Each one is constantly breaking and reforming, billions of times a second, as the molecules jostle. But there are so many of them, all pulling at once, that collectively they are powerful. This is the secret of water: individually weak, collectively strong. Almost every special behavior of water traces back to this swarm of tiny, restless hydrogen bonds.

Notice the pattern from 3.01 repeating: the strong bonds within a molecule are covalent; the hydrogen bonds between molecules are weak. Both kinds matter, and this lesson is about what the weak ones accomplish.

Cohesion: water clinging to water

When water molecules cling to other water molecules through hydrogen bonds, we call it cohesion. Cohesion is why water forms rounded droplets instead of spreading into an invisible film, and why you can heap water slightly above the rim of a full glass before it spills — the molecules are holding hands and resisting separation.

Cohesion has a consequence that reaches all the way to the tops of trees. Inside a tree’s narrow vessels, water molecules are linked into one continuous, unbroken column. As water evaporates from the leaves at the top, it tugs upward on the molecule just below it, which tugs on the next, and the next — so the whole chain rises, pulled up by cohesion. We will return to this in Module 8, when we study how plants move water against gravity through transpiration. The thread starts here, in a hydrogen bond.

Surface tension: the skin on the pond

At the surface of a body of water, something special happens. A molecule deep in the water is pulled equally in all directions by its neighbors. But a molecule right at the top has no water above it — only air — so all its hydrogen-bond pulls come from the sides and below. This unbalanced pull draws the surface molecules tightly together into a kind of taut elastic film. We call it surface tension: cohesion concentrated at the air–water boundary, forming a “skin.”

Surface tension is strong enough that a water strider — a small, light insect — can stand and walk on the surface of a pond without sinking, its weight distributed across an unbroken skin of hydrogen-bonded water. It is also why a carefully placed paperclip can float on water even though steel is far denser than water. The water is not strong; the net of its hydrogen bonds is.

Adhesion: water clinging to other things

Water does not only stick to itself. Because it is polar, it is also attracted to other polar surfaces. When water clings to a different substance, we call it adhesion. Dip the corner of a paper towel into a puddle and watch the water climb upward into the dry fibers, against gravity: that is adhesion. The water molecules are gripping the polar surfaces inside the towel and pulling themselves along.

When adhesion and cohesion work together in a very thin tube, water can rise on its own — capillary action. Adhesion pulls the water up along the walls of the tube, and cohesion drags the rest of the column along behind it. The narrower the tube, the higher the water climbs. This same partnership of adhesion and cohesion is what lifts water through the microscopic plumbing of plants.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
An easy way to keep the two words straight: cohesion is water joined to its company — other water molecules. Adhesion is water added onto something else — a leaf, a towel, the glass wall of a tube. Cohesion holds the droplet together; adhesion lets it grab a surface and climb.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Cohesion, surface tension, and adhesion are not three separate marvels — they are three faces of the same weak hydrogen bond, which is itself just a consequence of water’s bent, polar shape. Hold onto the hydrogen bond, because it returns in a starring role: in Reading 3.04 and again in Module 5, hydrogen bonds will be what holds the two strands of your DNA together, pairing each base with its partner. The same gentle attraction that lets a water strider walk on a pond also keeps the instructions for building you from drifting apart. That is worth a moment of wonder.
