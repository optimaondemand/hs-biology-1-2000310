Acids, Bases & Buffers

Before you begin: finish Reading 3.04 and work through Lesson 3.04, including its practice classifying substances as acids or bases.
Lesson focus: reading the pH scale, telling acids from bases, and understanding how buffers keep life’s chemistry steady
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
This assignment has two parts. In Part A you place six common substances onto the pH scale and label each one acid or base. In Part B you answer in writing why human blood must stay near pH 7.4 and what a buffer actually does. The whole assignment should take about 20 minutes.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
If life’s molecules work only within a narrow pH range, how does the body keep its chemistry from swinging too far toward acid or base?
📝 STUDENT DIRECTIONS
1
Part A — Place on the pH scale (5 pts). For each of the six substances below, give an approximate pH value (0–14) and label it acid (pH below 7), base (pH above 7), or neutral (pH about 7). You may estimate the pH — what matters is putting them in the right order and on the correct side of neutral.
Substance Approx. pH Acid / base / neutral
Lemon juice  
Stomach acid

Human blood

2
Part B — Short answer (5 pts). In a short paragraph (four to six sentences), answer both questions: (a) Why must human blood stay close to pH 7.4 — what goes wrong if it drifts too far? (b) What does a buffer, such as the bicarbonate buffer system, actually do to keep blood pH steady? Submit a typed document or a clear photo of your completed scale and paragraph.
✅ SUCCESS CRITERIA
☐
All six substances are placed in a reasonable pH order and correctly labeled acid, base, or neutral.
☐
The answer explains why a narrow blood pH matters — for example, that proteins and enzymes fail outside their range.
☐
The answer correctly describes a buffer as resisting pH change by absorbing or releasing H⁺.
