**1. (Reading 3.01 — why water is polar)**
Why is a single water molecule polar?

- A) It contains three atoms, and any three-atom molecule is polar
- B) Its bent shape and uneven electron sharing leave one end slightly negative and the other slightly positive **✓**
- C) The hydrogen atoms pull electrons harder than the oxygen atom does
- D) Its atoms are held together by ionic bonds that transfer electrons completely

*Polarity requires both unequal electron sharing (oxygen pulls harder) and a bent shape so the charges do not cancel. A linear molecule with the same bonds would not be polar overall.*

**2. (Reading 3.01 — partial charges)**
In a water molecule, which atom carries a partial negative charge (δ−), and why?

- A) Hydrogen, because it is the smallest atom
- B) Hydrogen, because it has more protons than oxygen
- C) Oxygen, because it pulls the shared electrons harder than hydrogen does **✓**
- D) Neither atom, because the electrons are shared exactly equally

*Oxygen's electronegativity (3.44) is far higher than hydrogen's (2.20), so it draws the shared electrons closer and gains a partial negative charge; the hydrogens become partial positive.*

**3. (Reading 3.02 — cohesion / surface tension)**
A water strider can walk across the surface of a pond without sinking. This is possible mainly because of:

- A) Adhesion between the water and the strider's legs
- B) Cohesion among water molecules, which creates surface tension **✓**
- C) The high specific heat of the pond water
- D) Water's ability to dissolve the strider's weight

*Hydrogen bonds pull water molecules toward one another (cohesion); at the surface this produces a tense "skin" — surface tension — strong enough to support a light insect.*

**4. (Reading 3.02 — adhesion / capillary action)**
Water climbs upward inside a thin glass tube and soaks up a paper towel against gravity. This movement is best explained by:

- A) Cohesion, because water molecules attract one another
- B) Adhesion, because water molecules attract other polar surfaces **✓**
- C) High specific heat, because the water absorbs heat as it rises
- D) The water becoming less dense as it moves up

*Adhesion is the attraction between water and a different polar surface (glass or towel fibers). It pulls water up narrow spaces — capillary action.*

**5. (Reading 3.03 — moderating temperature)**
Which property of water allows it to moderate temperature changes in living organisms and their environments?

- A) Its low density as a solid
- B) Its ability to act as a solvent
- C) Its high specific heat **✓**
- D) Its bent molecular shape alone

*Hydrogen bonds must absorb a great deal of heat before water's temperature rises, so water resists rapid temperature swings — keeping cells, bodies, and coastal climates stable.*

**6. (Reading 3.03 — why ice floats)**
Unlike most substances, water is less dense as a solid than as a liquid, so ice floats. Why?

- A) Freezing removes hydrogen bonds, letting the molecules drift apart
- B) Hydrogen bonds lock the molecules into an open lattice that spreads them farther apart **✓**
- C) Ice traps air bubbles that make it lighter than water
- D) Cold water molecules weigh less than warm ones

*As water freezes, hydrogen bonds hold each molecule at a fixed distance in an open hexagonal lattice. This spacing makes solid water less dense than liquid, so ice floats and insulates the water below.*

**7. (Reading 3.03 — the universal solvent)**
Water dissolves salts and sugars more readily than most liquids. This is because water is:

- A) Nonpolar, so it slips between molecules easily
- B) Polar, so it surrounds charged and polar particles with hydration shells **✓**
- C) Acidic, so it breaks substances apart chemically
- D) Less dense than the substances it dissolves

*Because water is polar, its partial charges are attracted to ions and other polar molecules, surrounding each one in a hydration shell and pulling it into solution — earning water the name "universal solvent."*

**8. (Reading 3.04 — buffers)**
A buffer such as the bicarbonate system in human blood functions to:

- A) Always lower the pH of the blood
- B) Always add H⁺ to the blood
- C) Resist changes in pH, keeping the blood near pH 7.4 **✓**
- D) Remove all H⁺ from the blood so it becomes neutral

*A buffer absorbs excess H⁺ or releases H⁺ as needed, holding pH within a narrow range. This keeps blood near 7.4, where the body's proteins and enzymes work properly.*
