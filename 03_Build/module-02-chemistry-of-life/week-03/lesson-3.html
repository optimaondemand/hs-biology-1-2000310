📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why does the ocean warm slowly, ice float instead of sink, and salt vanish into a glass of water?
Three of water’s strangest habits — its high specific heat, its floating ice, and its power as the universal solvent — are not separate curiosities. Each one traces back to the same bent, polar molecule and its hydrogen bonds. Today you will connect each behavior to its cause, and to a consequence that keeps living things alive.

🔥 PART 1 · WATER HOLDS ITS TEMPER
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN

High specific heat — a built-in thermostat

Water has an unusually high specific heat (about 4.18 J/g·°C): it takes a lot of energy to raise its temperature even a little. Why? Because incoming heat must first go into breaking hydrogen bonds before it can speed the molecules up. This makes water a thermostat. Coastal climates stay mild, large bodies of water resist sudden swings, and the water inside your cells buffers you against the heat of the day. Water also has a high heat of vaporization: turning liquid to vapor carries away a great deal of heat, which is exactly why sweat cools you as it evaporates.

🧊 PART 2 · WHY ICE FLOATS, AND WHY THAT SAVES FISH

The solid that is lighter than the liquid

Almost every substance grows denser when it freezes — the solid sinks in its own liquid. Water is the great exception. As water freezes, its hydrogen bonds lock the molecules into an open, six-sided hexagonal lattice that holds them farther apart than they sit in the liquid. The result: ice is less dense than liquid water, so it floats. A frozen pond grows a lid of ice on top, and that lid insulates the liquid water below — which is why fish survive the winter instead of being frozen solid from the bottom up.

✨ FROM TODAY’S RESEARCH · FOLLOWING THE WATER
Because every form of life we know depends on liquid water, scientists searching for life beyond Earth treat liquid water as a leading marker of where to look — the band of distance from a star where a planet might be neither too hot nor too cold for it is called the habitable zone. Recent research is using powerful new telescopes, such as the James Webb Space Telescope, to study the atmospheres of distant exoplanets for signs of water vapor. It is worth being careful here: finding water would not be finding life. Water appears to be necessary for life as we know it, but it is not proof that life is present — and so far, none has been found. Scientists are currently exploring these questions, and the honest answer remains: we do not yet know.
🧪 PART 3 · THE UNIVERSAL SOLVENT

Why so much dissolves in water

Because water is polar, its δ+ and δ− ends are drawn to charged and polar particles. When salt enters water, the molecules surround each ion in a hydration shell — positive ends facing the negative ions, negative ends facing the positive ions — and pull the crystal apart. Water dissolves so many polar and ionic substances that it is called the universal solvent. Substances that mix with water this way are hydrophilic (“water-loving”); nonpolar substances that refuse to mix — oils and fats — are hydrophobic (“water-fearing”), a property you will meet again when lipids build a membrane in Lesson 4.02. Nearly all of life’s chemistry happens dissolved in water.
