🧬 PART 1 · THE MOLECULE THAT REMEMBERS

Nucleotides, the letters of life

Nucleic acids store and transmit genetic information. Their monomer is the nucleotide, built from three parts: a phosphate group, a five-carbon sugar, and a nitrogenous base. It is the base that carries meaning, and there are only a few of them — a tiny alphabet. DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) uses the sugar deoxyribose and the four bases A, T, C, G; it stores the cell’s master instructions. RNA (ribonucleic acid) uses the sugar ribose, swaps in the base U (uracil) where DNA uses T, and carries out the day-to-day work of building proteins. Just two molecules, a four-letter code — and every living thing is written in it.

🧝 PART 2 · THE DOUBLE HELIX AND ITS PAIRING RULE

Two strands, paired by hydrogen bonds

DNA is a double helix: two strands wound around each other and running in opposite directions — antiparallel. The strands are held together by complementary base pairing, and the rule is beautifully strict: A pairs with T, and C pairs with G. What holds each pair? The very same hydrogen bonds you studied between water molecules in Lesson 3.02 — individually weak, easy to unzip for copying, but collectively strong enough to guard the message. Because the pairing is fixed, either strand tells you the other: that is what makes DNA faithfully copyable. RNA, by contrast, is usually a single strand. Strands: DNA vs. RNA
🔄 TAP TO FLIP BACK

📜 PART 3 · HOW WE LEARNED THE SHAPE

A discovery, and a lesson in credit

The double helix was not obvious — it had to be worked out, and the story of how is as human as any in science.

💡 A MOMENT IN HISTORY · PHOTO 51 AND THE DOUBLE HELIX, 1953
At King’s College London, Rosalind Franklin used X-ray crystallography — firing X-rays through DNA fibers and reading the pattern they scattered — to measure the molecule’s dimensions with great care. Her clearest image, known as Photo 51, captured the signature of a helix. At Cambridge, James Watson and Francis Crick built the correct double-helix model in 1953, drawing on Franklin’s data — which her colleague Maurice Wilkins had shown Watson without her knowledge or consent. Watson, Crick, and Wilkins shared the 1962 Nobel Prize. Franklin had died in 1958, and because the prize is not awarded after death, she could not be named — and her central contribution went underacknowledged for decades. It is a story of brilliant inference and, at the same time, a hard lesson about the ethics of credit in science.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

With nucleic acids, the four families are complete — and the door to the rest of biology swings open. The hydrogen bonds that hold the base pairs are the same ones that held water together in Lesson 3.02; the code those bases spell out becomes the instructions for building proteins, whose folded shape (Lesson 4.03) does the work of life. All of that is the heart of Module 5, where you will follow the path from DNA to RNA to protein, and see how a single changed base can rewrite a life.
