📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
We close the module with the most famous molecule of all — the one that carries the instructions for building every protein you read about yesterday, and that passes those instructions from parent to child. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 4.04. By the end you should be able to name the monomer of a nucleic acid, describe the double helix and how its bases pair, and explain the key differences between DNA and RNA.

The molecule that remembers

Nucleic acids are the macromolecules that store and transmit genetic information — the cell’s library and its instruction manual. There are two: DNA and RNA. Both are polymers, and their monomer is the nucleotide.

A nucleotide has three parts joined together: a phosphate group, a five-carbon sugar, and a nitrogenous base. The sugar and phosphate of each nucleotide link to the next, forming a long backbone; the bases stick out from that backbone like rungs waiting for their match. It is the sequence of bases along the strand that carries the information — a chemical alphabet only a few letters long.

DNA: the double helix

In DNA the sugar is deoxyribose, and there are four bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), and guanine (G). DNA is built from two strands wound around each other into the famous double helix. The two strands run in opposite directions — they are antiparallel, like two lanes of traffic flowing past each other.

What holds the two strands together is one of the most elegant rules in biology: complementary base pairing. A always pairs with T, and C always pairs with G — and each pair is held by hydrogen bonds. Yes, the very same weak attraction you first met between water molecules in Reading 3.02 is what zips the two strands of your DNA together. Because the pairing is fixed, one strand always specifies the other: if you know the sequence of one strand, you can write its partner. That simple fact is what makes DNA copyable — the heart of inheritance.

DNA stores the cell’s master instructions, kept safe and rarely altered — the reference copy in the library.

RNA: the working copy

If DNA is the reference book that never leaves the library, RNA is the photocopy you carry to the workbench. Its sugar is ribose; it uses the bases A, C, G, but in place of thymine it uses uracil (U); and it is usually single-stranded. RNA’s job is to help carry out protein synthesis — turning the DNA instructions into the proteins of Reading 4.03.

Feature DNA RNA
Sugar Deoxyribose Ribose
Bases A, T, C, G A, U, C, G (uracil replaces thymine)
Strands Double (helix) Usually single
Job Stores the master instructions Helps carry out protein synthesis

Remember the one-letter swap: DNA has T, RNA has U. It is a favorite point to be tested on.

📚 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF SCIENCE
The shape of DNA was worked out in 1953, and the story is one of both brilliant inference and a hard lesson in the ethics of credit. At King’s College London, Rosalind Franklin used X-ray crystallography to photograph DNA; her famous image, known as “Photo 51,” captured the precise dimensions of the helix. At Cambridge, James Watson and Francis Crick built the correct double-helix model — relying on Franklin’s data, which her colleague Maurice Wilkins had shown to Watson without her knowledge or consent. In 1962, Watson, Crick, and Wilkins shared the Nobel Prize. Franklin had died in 1958, and the prize is not awarded posthumously — but for decades afterward her central contribution went underacknowledged. The double helix is a triumph of reasoning from evidence; it is also a reminder that science is done by people, and that honesty about whose work made a discovery possible is part of doing it well.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

You have now met all four macromolecules — and ended on the one that ties the others together. The base sequence in DNA is a code, and that code is read out to build proteins, whose shape (Reading 4.03) decides what they do. This is the doorway to Module 5, where we follow the path DNA → RNA → protein and watch how the instructions are copied and used. Step back and see the whole module at once: from a bent water molecule to a self-assembling membrane to a folded enzyme to a copyable helix — the same handful of atoms, the same atoms as the stars, arranged step by step into something that can grow, work, and remember. That is the wonder this module has been building toward.
