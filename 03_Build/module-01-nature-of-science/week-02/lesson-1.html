📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When someone says “it’s just a theory,” are they right — or are they confusing two very different words?
Today you’ll pin down three words that science uses with great precision: hypothesis, theory, and law. Then you’ll meet two scientists the experts told to sit down — and who turned out to be right.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define hypothesis, theory, and law and explain how they differ.
2
I will be able to explain why a scientific theory does not “become” a law, and why “just a theory” misunderstands the word.
3
I will be able to classify scientific statements as hypotheses, theories, or laws.

📚 PART 1 · THREE WORDS, THREE JOBS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When you hear “evolution is just a theory,” what do you think the word “theory” is being used to mean?

Tap to reveal each term

Three statements, three different jobs. Notice that a law describes a pattern while a theory explains the cause — they answer different questions, so neither becomes the other.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · HYPOTHESIS / THEORY / LAW
HYPOTHESIS — tap to reveal
A specific, testable, falsifiable proposed explanation — tentative, and exactly what an experiment is built to test. Example: “This bacterium grows faster at 37 °C than at 20 °C.”
THEORY — tap to reveal
A broad, well-substantiated explanation built from many tested hypotheses and supported by large, independent bodies of evidence. It answers why. Example: the germ theory of disease; cell theory; evolution by natural selection.
LAW — tap to reveal
A statement — often mathematical — that describes a consistent pattern in nature. It tells you what happens, not why. Example: Mendel’s law of segregation; the laws of thermodynamics.
🩸 PART 2 · RIGHT, AND TOLD TO SIT DOWN
💡 A MOMENT IN HISTORY · WEGENER (1912) & MARSHALL AND WARREN (1984)
Alfred Wegener proposed that continents drift — matching coastlines, identical fossils across oceans, matching rock layers. Geologists rejected him for fifty years because he had no mechanism. He was right; seafloor spreading later supplied the missing force, and drift became part of plate tectonics. Decades on, Barry Marshall and Robin Warren argued that a bacterium, not stress, caused stomach ulcers. Almost no one believed bacteria could survive stomach acid — so in 1984 Marshall drank a culture of it, gave himself the predicted inflammation, and cured it with antibiotics. Nobel Prize, 2005. In both cases the consensus changed the honest way: by evidence the old view could not explain away.

This is why theories stay open to revision — not because they’re weak, but because that open door is the only way a Wegener or a Marshall can ever be heard. Holding a strong theory firmly and leaving it open to better evidence is the honesty this module is about.

💪 THE HABIT THIS BUILDS

From now on, when you meet the word “theory,” pause and ask which meaning is intended. Treating a well-tested theory with the respect it has earned — while keeping the door open to better evidence — is exactly the honest habit of mind this course is training. Next, you’ll see how a theory earns that trust: through peer review, reproducibility, and consensus.
