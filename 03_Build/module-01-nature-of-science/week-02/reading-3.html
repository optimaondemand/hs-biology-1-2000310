Models, and How to Spot Pseudoscience
Anchor Reading
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Scientists almost never study the world directly — they study models of it. This reading explains what a scientific model is, why every model is deliberately incomplete, and then turns that idea into a practical tool: how to tell real science from pseudoscience — claims that look scientific but don’t play by science’s rules. You’ll use this the next time a dramatic health headline lands in your feed.

What a model is — and why it’s never the whole truth

A model is a simplified representation of something too large, too small, too slow, or too complex to grasp directly. Models come in many forms: a labeled diagram of a cell, a plastic model of DNA, a food web drawn on a whiteboard, an equation for population growth, or a computer simulation of Earth’s climate. Each one strips away detail on purpose so that the part you care about becomes clear.

Because models simplify, every model is “wrong” in some way — it leaves something out. A subway map distorts real distances, but it is exactly what you need to ride the train. The honest scientist’s question is never “Is this model true?” but “Is this model useful for the question I’m asking, and where will it break down?”

You will build and use models all year — including in VR, where you’ll construct cells, molecules, and ecosystems with your own hands. Each time, ask what the model captures well and what it leaves out. Knowing a model’s limits is part of using it honestly.

Science vs. pseudoscience

The philosopher Karl Popper argued that the mark of a genuinely scientific claim is falsifiability: a real scientific idea sticks its neck out and says what evidence would prove it wrong. A claim that can explain away any possible result isn’t strong — it’s empty. Pseudoscience wears the costume of science (technical words, confident experts, “studies”) but quietly breaks the rules. Here is a working checklist:

Ask… Science Pseudoscience
Could it be proven wrong? Makes testable, falsifiable predictions. Vague enough to explain any outcome.
What’s the evidence? Controlled studies, reproduced by others. Personal stories (“testimonials”) and cherry-picked cases.
Does it change with evidence? Revises claims when data demand it. Never abandons the claim; blames the doubters.
Why should I believe it? Open methods anyone can check. “Trust me” / ancient secret / lone genius nobody can question.

Be careful: a claim being new or unpopular does not make it pseudoscience — Wegener and Marshall were both. The test is never popularity. It is whether the claim can be tested, has survived testing, and would yield to better evidence.

🔗 TRY THIS · A REAL HEADLINE TEST
In Lesson 2.03 you’ll put a real health headline through this checklist. To prepare, pick any “miracle cure” or “one weird trick” health headline you’ve seen and ask the four questions above. A genuinely useful free guide for this is the U.S. National Library of Medicine’s MedlinePlus “Evaluating Health Information” page (medlineplus.gov/evaluatinghealthinformation.html). Notice how its advice — check the source, look for evidence, beware of claims that sound too good — is the same logic you just learned, applied to everyday life.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

You will be sold things your whole life using the language of science. The skill that protects you is not memorizing which claims are false — it’s knowing the questions that separate a careful claim from an empty one. A model honestly used, and a claim honestly tested, share the same virtue: they tell you where they could be wrong.
