📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How can a claim sound completely scientific and still be worthless — and how would you tell?
First you’ll see why scientists study models rather than the world directly, and why every good model is honest about what it leaves out. Then you’ll turn that idea into a practical filter for telling science from pseudoscience — and try it on a real headline.

2

📚 PART 1 · EVERY MODEL LEAVES SOMETHING OUT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A subway map shows stations as evenly spaced dots on straight colored lines. Is the map “wrong”?

A model is a simplified stand-in for something too big, small, slow, or complex to study directly — a cell diagram, a DNA kit, a food web, a climate simulation. Because it simplifies, every model leaves something out. The honest question is never “Is it true?” but “Is it useful for my question, and where does it break down?” You’ll build many models this year, including in VR — each time, name what it captures and what it omits.

🩸 PART 2 · THE FOUR QUESTIONS

Philosopher Karl Popper said a genuinely scientific claim sticks its neck out: it tells you what evidence would prove it wrong. Tap each test to see how science and pseudoscience answer it differently.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · SCIENCE vs. PSEUDOSCIENCE
Could it be proven wrong? — tap to reveal
Science: makes testable, falsifiable predictions. Pseudoscience: stays vague enough to explain any outcome, so nothing could ever disprove it.
What’s the evidence? — tap to reveal
Science: controlled studies reproduced by others. Pseudoscience: personal testimonials and cherry-picked cases that ignore the misses.
Does it change with evidence? — tap to reveal
Science: revises claims when data demand it. Pseudoscience: never abandons the claim — it blames the doubters instead.
Why should I believe it? — tap to reveal
Science: open methods anyone can check. Pseudoscience: “trust me” — an ancient secret or a lone genius nobody is allowed to question.

Careful: being new or unpopular doesn’t make a claim pseudoscience — remember Wegener and Marshall. The test is whether it can be tested and would yield to better evidence, never whether it’s popular.

🧪 PART 3 · RUN THE FILTER

Apply the four questions. Which claims behave like science, and which like pseudoscience?

⚡ TRY IT · SCIENCE OR PSEUDOSCIENCE?
Tap your answer for each.
1. “This bracelet heals through an energy field that science cannot detect or measure.”
2. “Patients given this antibiotic clear the infection faster than those given a placebo in a controlled trial.”
3. “If the remedy didn’t work for you, it’s because you didn’t believe in it strongly enough.”
4. “Our peer-reviewed, reproducible study supports this conclusion, which we’d revise if stronger evidence emerged.”
🔗 WEB INTERACTIVE · TEST A REAL HEADLINE
Open the U.S. National Library of Medicine’s free guide, MedlinePlus: Evaluating Health Information — medlineplus.gov/evaluatinghealthinformation.html. Find one “miracle cure” health headline online and run it through the four questions above plus MedlinePlus’s checklist (Who runs the site? What’s the evidence? Is it selling something? When was it updated?). Bring your verdict to Assignment 2.03.
