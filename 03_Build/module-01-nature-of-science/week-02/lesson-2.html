📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If even brilliant scientists make mistakes, why should we trust what science as a whole tells us?
Because science is built to catch mistakes. Today you’ll meet the three-part system that does it — peer review, reproducibility, and consensus — and look honestly at where the system has been straining.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to describe how peer review, reproducibility, and consensus make scientific knowledge trustworthy.
2
I will be able to explain why a single unrepeated study is treated with caution.
3
I will be able to explain why scientific self-correction — even the “replication crisis” — is a sign of strength, not failure.

📚 PART 1 · THREE SAFEGUARDS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A headline says “New study finds chocolate boosts memory.” What’s your first instinct?

Tap to reveal each safeguard

No one scientist decides what is true. These three safeguards work together — and notice that consensus is the result of the other two, not a vote.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · THE THREE SAFEGUARDS
PEER REVIEW — tap to reveal
Before publication, independent experts hunt for flaws: weak controls, conclusions the data don’t support, overlooked alternatives. Not perfect — but by the time you read a result, critics have already tried to break it.
REPRODUCIBILITY — tap to reveal
A real result is one independent labs can produce again from the published methods. This is why methods are reported in detail — so others can check the work by redoing it. One result is a question; a reproduced result is knowledge.
CONSENSUS — tap to reveal
Not a popularity vote — the convergence that remains after years of independent researchers trying and failing to knock an explanation down. It is the weight of evidence, and it changes when better evidence arrives.
🩸 PART 2 · WHEN SCIENCE CATCHES ITSELF
✨ CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH · THE “REPLICATION CRISIS”
Recent research suggests the self-checking system has been straining. Over the last decade, teams trying to reproduce many published studies — especially in psychology and parts of biomedicine — found that a worrying share didn’t hold up. Scientists are currently exploring why: pressure to publish surprising results, small samples, and selective reporting all seem to contribute. The hopeful part is also the honest one: we know about this because scientists went looking for it. Reforms now being tested — registering a study’s plan in advance, sharing raw data, rewarding replication — are self-correction working in public.

A field that admits its own weak results, and builds tools to fix them, is doing exactly what makes science trustworthy. Compare that with a claim that can never be wrong, by anyone, ever — that’s the topic of your next lesson.

🧪 PART 3 · HOW MUCH SHOULD I TRUST THIS?

For each situation, decide whether the result should be trusted more or treated with caution — and why.

💪 THE HABIT THIS BUILDS

When you meet a scientific claim, ask the three questions: peer-reviewed? reproduced? consistent with the wider evidence? This is not cynicism — it’s the disciplined trust that lets you take science seriously without being fooled. Next, you’ll sharpen the same skill into a tool for telling science from pseudoscience.
