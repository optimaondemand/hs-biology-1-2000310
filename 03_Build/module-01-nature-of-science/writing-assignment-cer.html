Module 1 CER Writing Assignment
Put a Flawed Study on Trial
Claim · Evidence · Reasoning · 12 pts · counts toward the CER writing category
📖 WHAT THIS IS
This is your first full CER essay — the kind of analytical writing you will do once per module all year. It pulls together everything in Module 1: observation vs. inference, experimental design, the difference between a strong and a weak argument, and the CER structure from Reading 1.04. It is assigned at the start of Week 1 and due at the end of Week 2. Your teacher will read and score it personally — this one is not auto-graded.
🎯 YOUR TASK
You are given a flawed experimental study. Identify the claim it makes, evaluate the quality of its evidence, name the reasoning error — and then rewrite a conclusion the evidence can actually support.
📜 THE STUDY TO ANALYZE
— from a (fictional) product newsletter —
“Brain-Boost Gummies raise test scores — the proof is in! A teacher gave her first-period Honors Biology class one Brain-Boost gummy each morning for two weeks before their unit test. Her third-period regular Biology class got no gummies. On the unit test, the Honors class averaged 88% while the regular class averaged 74%. ‘The gummies clearly work,’ the teacher concluded. ‘The class that took them scored 14 points higher. Parents should buy Brain-Boost for any student who wants better grades.’ The company notes that the gummies contain vitamin B12 ‘known to support brain function,’ and that several students said they ‘felt more focused.’”
As you read, think back to Week 1: Was there a fair comparison? Did more than one thing differ between the groups? Is this evidence, or a testimonial?
📝 HOW TO STRUCTURE YOUR ESSAY (FOLLOW THESE FOUR PARTS)
Write in paragraphs, not a list. The sentence starters are scaffolding to get you going — make the words your own.
1
State the study’s claim. In your own words, what is the study claiming? Starter: “The study claims that…”
2
Evaluate the evidence. What evidence is offered, and how strong is it? Point to specific problems: was there a fair, controlled comparison? How many variables differed between the two classes? Are “felt more focused” and B12 “known to support brain function” real evidence or something weaker? Starter: “The evidence is weak because…”
3
Name the reasoning error. Identify the central flaw in the logic — the leap the study makes that the evidence does not support. Name it (for example, a confounded comparison, or treating a correlation as a cause) and explain why it breaks the argument. Starter: “The main reasoning error is…, because…”
4
Rewrite a defensible conclusion — and fix the design. Write a conclusion the evidence can actually support, then describe one change that would make a fair test of the gummies (think: one variable, equivalent groups, a real control). Starter: “A conclusion the evidence supports is… To test this fairly, you would…”
🧭 THE VIRTUE THIS PRACTICES · HONESTY
This module’s virtue is honesty — following the evidence even when a tidy story tempts you elsewhere. The gummy study isn’t lying; it is doing something subtler and more common: letting a hopeful conclusion run ahead of what the evidence can carry. Learning to catch that gap — in others’ arguments and your own — is the heart of thinking like a scientist.
✅ SUCCESS CRITERIA
☐
The study’s claim is accurately stated in your own words.
☐
The evidence is evaluated with specific weaknesses named (not just “it’s bad”).
☐
The central reasoning error is named and explained.
☐
A defensible conclusion and a fairer experimental design are proposed; the essay reads as one connected argument (~400–700 words).
📊 CER SCORING RUBRIC (3 PTS × 4 CRITERIA = 12 PTS)
Criterion Strong (3) Developing (2) Beginning (1)
Claim Specific and accurate; states the study’s claim and your defensible rewrite clearly. Somewhat vague or partly inaccurate. Missing, or just restates the prompt.
Evidence Names multiple specific weaknesses (confounded groups, no control, testimonial, no replication) and explains each. Names one or two weaknesses with partial explanation. Generic (“the evidence is weak”) or unexplained.
Reasoning Correctly names the central error (confounding / correlation≠causation) and explains why it breaks the argument; proposes a fair redesign. Identifies a flaw but explanation or redesign has gaps. Restates evidence without naming the logical error.
Integration Reads as one coherent argument; parts connect; within the word range. Parts present but loosely connected. Disconnected; listed without flow.
Total possible: 12 pts
