**1. (Reading 1.01 — observation vs. inference)**
A student writes in her notebook: "The water in the beaker is cloudy." Which best describes this statement?

- A) An inference, because she is explaining what happened
- B) An observation, because it reports something sensed directly **✓**
- C) A hypothesis, because it could be tested
- D) A conclusion, because it ends the investigation

*Targets the observation/inference confusion. A reports cloudiness directly with the eyes — an observation.*

---

**2. (Reading 1.01 — qualitative vs. quantitative)**
Which of the following is an example of **quantitative** data?

- A) The leaf is bright green and smooth
- B) The frog jumped away when touched
- C) The plant grew 3.4 cm in one week **✓**
- D) The solution smells like vinegar

*Quantitative data are numerical measurements with units; the others are qualitative descriptions.*

---

**3. (Reading 1.02 — independent variable)**
A scientist tests how the temperature of water affects how quickly sugar dissolves in it. What is the **independent variable**?

- A) The amount of sugar that dissolves
- B) The temperature of the water **✓**
- C) The type of sugar used
- D) The size of the beaker

*The independent variable is the factor deliberately changed — here, water temperature. The amount dissolved is the dependent variable.*

---

**4. (Reading 1.02 — controlled experiment / confounding)**
A student grows one plant in sunlight with fertilizer and another plant in a dark closet with no fertilizer. After two weeks, the sunny plant is taller. Why can the student **not** conclude that fertilizer caused the difference?

- A) Two weeks is not enough time for any plant to grow
- B) Plants do not respond to fertilizer
- C) Two variables changed at once, so the experiment is confounded **✓**
- D) The student should have measured mass instead of height

*Light and fertilizer both differ between the plants, so the cause cannot be isolated — a confounded design.*

---

**5. (Reading 1.02 — why scientists repeat tests)**
A researcher gets a surprising result from a single experiment. According to how science works, what is the most appropriate next step?

- A) Publish the result immediately as a proven fact
- B) Repeat the experiment and have others replicate it **✓**
- C) Discard the result because it was surprising
- D) Change the hypothesis to match the result

*Replication guards against flukes and error; a single result is rarely sufficient.*

---

**6. (Reading 1.03 — falsifiability)**
Which statement is a **testable (falsifiable) hypothesis**?

- A) An undetectable force that no instrument can measure helps plants grow
- B) Blue light makes basil plants grow taller than red light does **✓**
- C) Sunsets are more beautiful in autumn
- D) All living things are interesting

*Only B predicts a specific, measurable outcome that an experiment could prove wrong. The others cannot be tested.*

---

**7. (Reading 1.03 — hypothesis vs. prediction)**
"If warmer water increases a goldfish's breathing rate, then a goldfish in 28 °C water will show more gill movements per minute than one in 18 °C water." The underlined part — "a goldfish in 28 °C water will show more gill movements per minute" — is best described as the:

- A) Claim about beauty
- B) Independent variable
- C) Prediction (the expected result) **✓**
- D) Control group

*The "then…" clause states the specific expected result if the hypothesis is true — a prediction, distinct from the explanatory hypothesis.*

**8. (Reading 1.04 — Claim, Evidence, Reasoning)**
In a Claim–Evidence–Reasoning argument, what is the role of the **reasoning**?

- A) To state the question being investigated
- B) To list the materials used in the experiment
- C) To present the raw data collected
- D) To explain *why* the evidence supports the claim **✓**

*Reasoning is the logical bridge connecting evidence to claim, usually by naming the underlying scientific principle.*
