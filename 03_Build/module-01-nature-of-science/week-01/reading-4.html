📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
This is the anchor reading for Lesson 1.04, and it introduces the backbone of the entire course: CER — Claim, Evidence, Reasoning. Every major piece of writing you do this year will be built this way. The reading closes with how to keep a scientific notebook and how to work safely — the practical habits of a working scientist.

A doctor who counted the dead

In the 1840s, in a Vienna maternity hospital, the physician Ignaz Semmelweis noticed something terrible. In one ward, run by doctors, far more new mothers were dying of childbed fever than in a second ward run by midwives. The difference was large and consistent. Semmelweis did not simply assert a cause; he built an argument from what he could measure.

He noticed that the doctors, unlike the midwives, often came straight from dissecting corpses to delivering babies. He proposed that some invisible “cadaverous material” was being carried on their hands. When he ordered doctors to wash their hands in a chlorinated-lime solution, the death rate in their ward fell dramatically — from roughly one in six to a small fraction of that.

Semmelweis had a claim (handwashing prevents these deaths), he had evidence (the death rate fell sharply once handwashing began), and he had reasoning (something invisible was being transferred from corpses to patients). Tragically, his colleagues rejected him — partly because he could not yet explain what the invisible thing was; germ theory was still decades away. He was right, and it cost him dearly to be right too early. His story is the perfect introduction to how a scientific argument is built — and to how hard it can be to get one accepted.

Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

Scientists do not just report results; they make arguments. The structure of a scientific argument is called CER, and it has three parts:

Part What it is Semmelweis
Claim A clear, specific, arguable answer to the question — something that could be wrong. Handwashing prevents childbed-fever deaths.
Evidence Specific data or observations that support the claim — not just “studies show.” Deaths in the doctors’ ward fell sharply after handwashing began.
Reasoning The logic linking evidence to claim — why the evidence supports it. An invisible substance carried on hands was causing the infections; removing it removed the cause.

In the classical tradition, this is simply the shape of any good argument: the claim is your thesis, the evidence is your proof, and the reasoning is the logic that ties them together. The strongest CER arguments go one step further and answer the best objection someone could raise — the counterargument.

The scientific notebook

A scientist’s notebook is the honest record of what was actually done and seen — dated, specific, and never tidied up after the fact. Record observations as they happen, in ink or saved with a timestamp, including the results that don’t fit your expectations. Those surprising results are often where discovery hides. Throughout this course you will keep a notebook (a physical notebook or a Word document in your OneDrive), and your lab work will be built on it.

The cardinal rule is honesty: you write down what happened, not what you wish had happened. A result that disappoints you is still data.

⚠️ SAFETY FIRST
Whenever you do hands-on lab work this year, basic safety is non-negotiable: wear eye protection when handling liquids, heat, or anything that can splash; tie back long hair near flames; never taste or directly sniff unknown substances; know where to find water and an adult; and clean up and wash your hands when you finish. Most home-lab accidents come from skipping one of these — not from anything exotic.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

CER is not just a school format — it is how scientists, lawyers, and careful thinkers everywhere build a case that can persuade a skeptical reader. Semmelweis had the claim, the evidence, and the reasoning, and was still rejected because the world was not ready. Learning to make the argument well, and to hold it honestly, is what this module — and this whole year — is training you to do.
