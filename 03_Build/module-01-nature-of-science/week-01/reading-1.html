What Counts as Science?
Anchor Reading
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
This is the anchor reading for Lesson 1.01. Read it slowly, pencil in hand. It asks a question you have never been formally asked, even though you have answered it every day of your life: how do you know what you know? By the end you should be able to tell the difference between an observation and an inference, between qualitative and quantitative data, and between a question science can answer and one it cannot.

A quarrel about how to know

For most of recorded history, the way to settle a question about the natural world was to ask an authority. If you wanted to know how many teeth a horse had, you did not open a horse’s mouth — you consulted Aristotle, who had written the number down centuries earlier. Knowledge was something you inherited, not something you went and checked.

In the year 1620, an English philosopher named Francis Bacon published a book arguing that this had things exactly backwards. The way to understand nature, Bacon insisted, was not to reason down from the opinions of famous men but to build up from careful, patient observation of the world itself. Look first; conclude second. It sounds obvious to us now. It was a genuinely radical claim then — a quiet revolt against the idea that truth comes from above rather than from evidence.

Bacon did not invent this idea alone. Six hundred years earlier, the scholar Ibn al-Haytham, working in Cairo, had already insisted that a claim about light or vision had to be tested against what the eye and the instrument actually showed. What we now call science is the long flowering of that one stubborn commitment: when the world and the authority disagree, the world wins.

What science actually is

Science is not a body of facts. It is a method — a disciplined way of asking questions about the natural world and answering them with evidence. The facts in your textbook are the products of that method, but the method itself is the thing worth learning, because the method is what lets you decide, on your own, whether a new claim is trustworthy.

A claim is scientific when it can, at least in principle, be tested against evidence — when there is some observation that could show it to be wrong. “Plants grow faster in red light than in blue light” is a scientific claim: you can grow plants under each color and measure. “Red is the most beautiful color” is not a scientific claim — not because beauty doesn’t matter, but because no measurement could settle it. Science is powerful precisely because it is modest: it confines itself to questions that evidence can decide, and it leaves the rest to other kinds of human thinking.

Biology is the branch of this method aimed at the living world — at what living things are, how they work, how they came to be, and how they are related. It is, more than any other science, the study of us.

Observation and inference are not the same thing

The first habit of a scientist is to separate what you see from what you conclude. An observation is information you gather directly with your senses or an instrument. An inference is an explanation or conclusion you build on top of an observation. The two feel like one act, but they are not, and confusing them is the source of an enormous amount of bad reasoning.

Suppose you walk outside and the ground is wet. The ground is wet is an observation. It rained last night is an inference — a reasonable one, but not the only one. A sprinkler, a burst pipe, or heavy morning fog would leave the same wet ground. The observation is solid; the inference is a hypothesis you would have to check.

Observation (what you sense) Inference (what you conclude)
The leaves of the plant on the windowsill have turned yellow. The plant is not getting enough light.
The pond water under the microscope is full of moving green specks. The specks are living organisms.
A patient’s temperature reads 39.4 °C. The patient has an infection.

In each row, the left column could be checked by anyone with the same senses or instrument. The right column is a story we tell to explain the left — and the story could be wrong.

Two kinds of data

The observations a scientist records are called data, and they come in two forms. Qualitative data describe qualities — color, shape, texture, smell, behavior — in words: “the solution turned cloudy and blue.” Quantitative data are measured amounts expressed as numbers with units: “the temperature rose 4.2 °C in ten minutes.”

Neither kind is better; good biology uses both. A field biologist describing the courtship dance of a bird needs careful qualitative description; a biologist measuring how fast a population grows needs numbers. What matters is that the data be honest — recorded as they actually were, not as you hoped they would be.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
A fast test for telling observation from inference: ask, “Could two careful people with the same equipment disagree about this?” If no — it is an observation. If yes — you have slipped into inference, and you owe it some evidence.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

You will spend this year studying cells, genes, ecosystems, and your own body. But every one of those facts was once a hard-won inference, built carefully on top of observations by people who refused to confuse the two. Learning to keep observation and inference apart — and to be honest about which one you are doing — is the single most useful intellectual habit this course can give you. It is also the first form of the virtue this module is built around: honesty, the willingness to follow the evidence even when it points somewhere you did not expect.
