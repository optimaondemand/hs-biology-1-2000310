From Hunch to Hypothesis
Anchor Reading
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
This is the anchor reading for Lesson 1.03. You now know how to build a fair test. But a test has to be a test of something. This reading is about that something: the hypothesis — how a vague hunch becomes a sharp, testable explanation, and why the best hypotheses are the ones that could be proven wrong.

A hunch is not yet a hypothesis

Every investigation starts with a hunch — a feeling that something might be going on. “I bet my plant is dying because of the new spot I put it in.” A hunch is a fine starting point, but it is too loose to test. A hypothesis is a hunch that has been sharpened into a proposed, testable explanation for an observation. It does two things a hunch does not: it proposes a specific cause, and it can be checked against evidence.

A useful way to write one is the “If…then…because” form. If the cause I propose is real, then here is what I should observe when I test it, because here is the reasoning that links them. For example: If the yellow leaves are caused by too little light, then a matching plant moved to a sunny window should green up over two weeks, because leaves need light to make the green pigment they use to capture energy.

Hypothesis vs. prediction

These two words are often confused, and the difference is worth getting right. A hypothesis is the proposed explanation — it answers why. A prediction is the specific, expected result if the hypothesis is true — it answers what will happen when I test. The hypothesis is the idea; the prediction is the idea’s testable fingerprint.

Hypothesis (the proposed explanation) Prediction (the expected result)
The yellow leaves are caused by too little light. A plant moved to a sunny window will produce new green leaves within two weeks.
Warmer water speeds up how fast this fish breathes. In 28 °C water the fish’s gill movements per minute will be higher than in 18 °C water.

The best hypotheses can be proven wrong

Here is something that surprises most people: a good scientific hypothesis must be falsifiable — there has to be some possible result that would show it to be false. This is not a weakness; it is the whole point. “Warmer water speeds up this fish’s breathing” is falsifiable: if you raise the temperature and the breathing rate doesn’t change, the hypothesis is in trouble. By contrast, “an invisible, undetectable force is helping the plant” cannot be tested by any measurement, so it is not a scientific hypothesis at all.

A hypothesis that survives an honest attempt to prove it wrong is far more trustworthy than one that was never really put at risk. Science makes progress not by collecting confirmations but by sticking its neck out and seeing if the world chops it off.

Where results go wrong: error

Even a well-designed test produces imperfect data, and an honest scientist accounts for it. Random error is the scatter that comes from small, uncontrollable fluctuations — a slightly unsteady hand on a ruler, tiny differences between seeds. You reduce it by measuring carefully and repeating. Systematic error is a consistent bias in one direction — a scale that always reads 2 grams heavy, a thermometer that runs cold. It won’t shrink with repetition; you have to find and fix its source.

This is why a single experiment, however clean, is rarely the end of the story. Results must be replicated — by you, and then by others — before the scientific community treats them as reliable.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Before you call a statement a hypothesis, ask: “What result would prove this wrong?” If you can answer, you have a testable hypothesis. If nothing could ever count against it, it is a belief or an opinion — not something science can test.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

“What would prove this wrong?” is one of the most powerful questions a human being can ask — of a scientific claim, a news story, an advertisement, or even an answer from an AI. The willingness to state, in advance, what would change your mind is the heart of honest thinking, and it is the virtue this whole module is built around.
