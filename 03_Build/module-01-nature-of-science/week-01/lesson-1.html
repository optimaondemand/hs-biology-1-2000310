📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How do you know what you know — and how would you tell if you were wrong?
Before we study a single cell or gene, we have to agree on what makes a claim about nature trustworthy. Today you will learn to separate an observation from an inference, tell qualitative data from quantitative, and recognize which questions science can actually answer. These are the tools you will use for the rest of the year.

📚 PART 1 · A STARTING HONEST GUESS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN

Science is a method, not a pile of facts

It is tempting to think of science as the stuff in the textbook — the mitochondria, the food webs, the Punnett squares. But those are the products. Science itself is a method: a disciplined way of asking a question about the natural world and answering it with evidence rather than authority. A claim earns the word “scientific” when there is some possible observation that could show it to be wrong. That is why “this plant grows faster in red light” is a scientific claim and “red is the most beautiful color” is not — the first can be tested, the second cannot. Science is powerful because it is modest about its own reach.

🔬 PART 2 · OBSERVATION VS. INFERENCE

The most useful distinction in all of science

An observation is information you gather directly with your senses or an instrument. An inference is the explanation or conclusion you build on top of that observation. They feel like one act, but they are two, and good scientists keep them apart on purpose. The ground is wet is an observation. It rained last night is an inference — reasonable, but a sprinkler or heavy fog would leave the same wet ground. The observation is solid; the inference is a hypothesis you would still have to check.

💡 A MOMENT IN HISTORY · THE REVOLT AGAINST AUTHORITY
For centuries, the way to answer a question about nature was to consult an authority — usually Aristotle, writing centuries earlier. In 1620 the English philosopher Francis Bacon argued this was backwards: real knowledge is built up from patient observation, not handed down from famous names. Six hundred years before Bacon, the scholar Ibn al-Haytham in Cairo had already insisted that claims about light and vision be tested against what the instrument actually showed. Both were making the same stubborn point that defines science to this day: when the world and the authority disagree, the world wins. It cost real courage to say so.

📊 PART 3 · TWO KINDS OF DATA

Qualitative and quantitative

The observations a scientist records are called data. Qualitative data describe qualities in words — color, texture, smell, behavior: “the solution turned cloudy and blue.” Quantitative data are measured amounts, written as numbers with units: “the temperature rose 4.2 °C in ten minutes.” Neither is better — strong biology uses both — but you should always know which one you are recording.

⚠️ AI REALITY CHECK · EVALUATING A CLAIM
An AI assistant can summarize a study, define a term, or draft a tidy explanation in seconds — a genuinely useful way to start investigating a question. It cannot tell you whether the claim is actually true. AI tools sometimes state confident-sounding conclusions with no evidence behind them, and even invent sources. Treat an AI’s answer the way an early scientist learned to treat a new microscope: a tool worth using, but one whose output you verify against the evidence yourself. The judgment of whether a claim holds up is still your job — this year is where you build it.

💪 THE HABIT THIS BUILDS

Every fact you will learn this year — about cells, genes, ecosystems, your own body — was once a careful inference built on top of honest observations. The scientists who built those facts refused to confuse the two, even when an authority told them otherwise. Keeping observation and inference apart, and being honest about which you are doing, is the first and most durable habit this course will give you.
