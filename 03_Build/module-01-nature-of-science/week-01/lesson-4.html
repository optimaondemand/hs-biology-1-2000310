📚 PART 1 · THE SHAPE OF AN ARGUMENT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of the last time you tried to convince someone of something true. What did you lean on most? (No wrong answer.)

Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

A scientific argument has three parts. Tap each card to reveal what it means — and notice that the third one, reasoning, is the part people most often leave out.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · THE THREE PARTS OF CER
CLAIM — tap to reveal
A clear, specific, arguable answer to the question — one that could be wrong. Not a question or a vague generality. Example: “Handwashing prevents childbed-fever deaths.”
EVIDENCE — tap to reveal
Specific data or observations that support the claim — with the actual numbers or facts, not “studies show.” Example: “Deaths in the doctors’ ward dropped sharply once handwashing began.”
REASONING — tap to reveal
The logic that explains why the evidence supports the claim — the scientific principle connecting them. Example: “An invisible substance on hands was causing the infections, so removing it removed the cause.” The strongest reasoning also answers the best counterargument.
🩸 PART 2 · RIGHT, AND REJECTED
💡 A MOMENT IN HISTORY · IGNAZ SEMMELWEIS, VIENNA, 1847
Semmelweis built a complete CER argument: a claim (handwashing prevents these deaths), evidence (the ward’s death rate fell sharply after he required chlorinated-lime handwashing), and reasoning (an invisible substance was being carried from corpses to patients). He was right. Yet his colleagues rejected him for years — partly because he could not yet say what the invisible substance was; germ theory was still decades away. A correct argument can still be refused when the world isn’t ready. The lesson cuts both ways: hold your evidence honestly, and stay humble about how hard acceptance can be.

Notice what carried Semmelweis’s case: not the claim alone, and not the data alone, but the reasoning that linked falling death rates to a transferable cause. Evidence without reasoning is just a pile of facts; reasoning is what turns facts into an argument.

🧪 PART 3 · NOTEBOOK & SAFETY

Set up like a scientist

Your scientific notebook is the honest, dated record of what you actually did and saw — including results that surprised you. Title the first page, date your entries, and never erase a result because it disappointed you. Before you do any hands-on work, run the safety check below.

⚡ TRY IT · SAFE OR UNSAFE?
For each lab habit, decide whether it is safe practice or a problem to fix.
1. Putting on eye protection before mixing two liquids.
2. Tasting a clear unknown liquid to figure out what it is.
3. Writing down a result in your notebook even though it’s not what you expected.
4. Leaning over a candle flame with long, untied hair.

💪 THE HABIT THIS BUILDS

CER is the spine of this course and of clear thinking generally. From now on, when you make a claim — in biology or anywhere — ask yourself: What is my evidence, and what is the reasoning that connects it to my claim? Next week you’ll use this to tell a scientific theory from a mere guess.
