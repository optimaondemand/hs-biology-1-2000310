📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What would it take to prove your idea wrong — and why is that the most important question you can ask?
Today you turn a vague hunch into a sharp hypothesis, tell a hypothesis apart from a prediction, and learn the surprising rule that the best scientific ideas are the ones that could be shown false. You’ll also see why this is the single best test for any claim — including one an AI gives you.

📚 PART 1 · FROM HUNCH TO HYPOTHESIS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When you have a strong hunch about something, how does it feel to imagine it being wrong? (No wrong answer.)

Sharpen the hunch

A hypothesis is a hunch sharpened into a proposed, testable explanation. The “If…then…because” form makes it sharp: If the cause I propose is real, then here is the specific result I should see when I test it, because here is the reasoning that links them. The “then” part is your prediction — the expected result — while the explanation itself is the hypothesis. The hypothesis says why; the prediction says what will happen when I test.

⚖️ PART 2 · COULD IT BE PROVEN WRONG?

Falsifiability is a feature, not a bug

A scientific hypothesis must be falsifiable — there must be some possible observation that would show it to be false. That is what lets the world weigh in. A claim that no result could ever contradict isn’t a strong hypothesis; it’s simply outside the reach of testing. Use the scale below to judge how testable each statement is.

⚡ PLACE IT · HOW TESTABLE?
For each statement, decide how well it could be tested — and possibly proven wrong — with an experiment.
1. “Salt water kills bean seedlings faster than fresh water does.”
2. “An undetectable life-force, measurable by no device, makes the seeds sprout.”
3. “Plants prefer to be talked to.”
⚠️ AI REALITY CHECK · SCIENCE’S TEST APPLIES TO AI TOO
When an AI tool hands you a confident-sounding answer, treat it exactly like a hypothesis: ask “what evidence would show this is wrong, and where can I check it?” An AI can give you a claim to investigate. It cannot do the checking for you — it sometimes states falsehoods with total confidence and even invents sources. The same question that tests a scientific idea — what would prove this wrong? — is your best defense against believing an AI too quickly.
📐 PART 3 · WHEN RESULTS GO WRONG

Two kinds of error

Even a clean test produces imperfect numbers. Random error is scatter from small, uncontrollable wobbles — a slightly unsteady ruler, tiny seed-to-seed differences; you shrink it by measuring carefully and repeating. Systematic error is a consistent bias — a scale that always reads 2 g heavy; repeating won’t fix it, you must find its source. Because of error, no single experiment settles a question: results must be replicated, by you and by others, before they can be trusted.

💪 THE HABIT THIS BUILDS

“What would prove this wrong?” is the question that separates a thinker from a believer. State it in advance — about your own ideas most of all — and you have built the core habit of honest science.
