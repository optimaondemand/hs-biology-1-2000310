Reading 1.02
Designing a Fair Test
Anchor Reading
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
This is the anchor reading for Lesson 1.02. In Reading 1.01 you learned to separate observation from inference. Now you will learn how scientists turn a question into a fair test — an experiment built so that, when the results come in, you can actually trust what they seem to say.

Twelve sick sailors

In 1747, a Scottish naval surgeon named James Lind faced a killer. Scurvy — a disease we now know comes from a lack of vitamin C — was killing more sailors than enemy cannon. Lind had a hunch that something in the diet was responsible, but a hunch is not knowledge. So he did something that was still rare at the time: he ran a controlled test.

Aboard HMS Salisbury, Lind took twelve sailors who all had scurvy and were at roughly the same stage of illness. He kept them in the same part of the ship, on the same basic diet, and split them into six pairs. To each pair he gave one different treatment: cider, vinegar, seawater, a spiced paste, dilute sulfuric acid — and to one pair, oranges and lemons. The pair given citrus recovered so quickly that one was fit for duty within days.

Notice why Lind’s test was convincing. Because he had held nearly everything else the same — same ship, same diet, same stage of illness — the one thing that differed between the groups was the treatment. When the citrus pair recovered, the treatment was the most reasonable explanation. That design — change one thing, hold the rest steady — is the heart of a fair test.

The parts of an experiment

A controlled experiment has a small set of named parts. Learn them now; you will use them every time you read or design an experiment for the rest of the course.

Part What it is In Lind’s test
Independent variable The one thing the scientist deliberately changes. The treatment each pair received.
Dependent variable What the scientist measures — the outcome that may depend on the change. How much each sailor’s scurvy improved.
Controlled variables (constants) Everything deliberately held the same so it can’t muddy the result. Same ship, base diet, stage of illness.
Control group A group that does not get the treatment, giving a baseline to compare against. The pairs given no real remedy (e.g., seawater).

Why “one thing at a time” matters

Imagine Lind had given the citrus pair oranges and moved them to a warmer, drier cabin and fed them fresh meat. If they recovered, what cured them? You could not say. When two or more things change at once, the experiment is confounded — the variables are tangled, and the result cannot point to a single cause. A fair test changes exactly one independent variable and holds the rest constant, so that any change in the dependent variable has only one reasonable explanation.

This is also why a single result is rarely enough. Scientists replicate — they repeat the test, and others repeat it too — because one experiment can always be a fluke. Trustworthy knowledge is built from results that hold up when checked again.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
A quick way to find the variables in any experiment: the independent variable finishes the sentence “I changed the…” and the dependent variable finishes “…and I measured the…” Everything else you kept the same is a controlled variable.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

This module’s major writing assignment asks you to evaluate a flawed experiment — to find the error in someone else’s design and explain why it makes the conclusion untrustworthy. Everything in this reading is the toolkit for that task. Being able to spot a confounded experiment is also a life skill: it is exactly what you need to judge a headline that claims a food, a supplement, or a habit “causes” some effect.
