Reading 12.03
When the Code Changes
Anchor Reading · Mutations and Their Effects
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Copying DNA is astonishingly faithful — but not perfect. Once in a while a letter is changed, added, or lost. A change in the DNA sequence is a mutation. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 12.03. By the end you should be able to tell a point mutation (substitution) from a frameshift (insertion or deletion), and explain why a single substitution can be silent, missense, or nonsense — and why a frameshift is usually far worse.

Point mutations: one letter changes

A point mutation, or substitution, swaps one base for another — a single letter of the message is changed. Because the code is read in three-letter codons, changing one letter changes (at most) one codon. Whether that matters depends entirely on what the new codon says. There are three possibilities.

• Silent — the new codon still codes for the same amino acid. Because several codons can mean the same amino acid, the protein is unchanged. Example: UUU and UUC both code for phenylalanine, so UUU→UUC changes nothing.
• Missense — the new codon codes for a different amino acid. One amino acid in the protein is swapped for another, which may or may not change how the protein folds and works.
• Nonsense — the new codon becomes a stop codon. The ribosome quits early, producing a short, usually broken protein.

Frameshift: the whole message scrambles

A frameshift mutation happens when one or more bases are inserted or deleted, and the number added or removed is not a multiple of three. Remember that the ribosome reads in fixed three-letter steps. Add or remove a letter and every codon after that point shifts — the reading frame slides over, and the rest of the message is garbled into nonsense codons.

Think of a sentence read three letters at a time: THE BIG CAT ATE. Delete the first letter and the same letters now read HEB IGC ATA TE — every word after the cut is wrong. That is why a frameshift is usually far more damaging than a single substitution: a substitution misspells one word, while a frameshift garbles the whole rest of the sentence.

Key contrast: a substitution changes (at most) one codon; a frameshift changes every codon downstream of the change.

Not all mutations are bad — and not all matter

It is tempting to think every mutation is a disaster, but most are not. A silent mutation changes nothing. Many missense mutations swap one similar amino acid for another and barely affect the protein. A few are harmful, like the one you will study tomorrow. And — importantly for later in the year — a rare mutation is helpful, giving an organism an advantage. Mutations are the original source of the variation that natural selection acts on (Module 6). Faithful copying preserves what works; the occasional error is the raw material of change.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

You can now name three outcomes of a single swapped letter — silent, missense, nonsense — and explain why losing or adding a letter is worse still. Tomorrow you will follow one specific missense mutation all the way from a single DNA letter to a human disease, and see how one wrong amino acid bends a protein out of shape and, with it, a person’s health. Keep these three words ready: silent, missense, nonsense.
