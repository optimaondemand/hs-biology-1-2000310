📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Copying is faithful — but not perfect. What happens when one letter of the message is wrong?
A change in the DNA sequence is a mutation. Today you will tell a point mutation (substitution) from a frameshift (insertion/deletion), sort substitutions into silent, missense, and nonsense, and see why a frameshift usually does far more damage than a single swap.

🔍 PART 1 · ONE LETTER CHANGES: THREE OUTCOMES

Silent, missense, nonsense

A point mutation (substitution) swaps one base for another, changing at most one codon. What happens depends on what the new codon says. Silent: the new codon still codes the same amino acid (e.g. UUU and UUC both code phenylalanine, so UUU→UUC changes nothing). Missense: the new codon codes a different amino acid. Nonsense: the new codon becomes a stop, ending the protein early.

🧰 PART 2 · FRAMESHIFT: THE WHOLE MESSAGE SCRAMBLES

Add or lose a letter and the frame slides

A frameshift happens when bases are inserted or deleted — and the number is not a multiple of three. Since the ribosome reads in fixed three-letter steps, every codon after the change is shifted and garbled. Read three at a time: THE BIG CAT. Delete the first letter and you get HEB IGC AT — every word after the cut is wrong. That is why a frameshift is usually far worse than a substitution.

🌱 PART 3 · NOT ALL MUTATIONS ARE BAD

The raw material of change

Most mutations are silent or have little effect; some are harmful; and a rare one is helpful, giving an organism an advantage. Mutations are the original source of the variation that natural selection acts on — a forward link to Module 6. Faithful copying preserves what works; the occasional error is where new possibilities come from. Tomorrow you follow one specific missense mutation all the way to a human disease.
