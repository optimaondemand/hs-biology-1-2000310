🩸 PART 1 · THE FIVE-LINK TRACE

One base change, followed all the way out

1. DNA. One base in the beta-globin gene is substituted — a single letter changed.
2. mRNA codon. The codon now reads GAG → GUG.
3. Amino acid. GAG = glutamic acid (Glu); GUG = valine (Val). The 6th amino acid of the chain changes from Glu to Val.
4. Protein shape. Glu is hydrophilic (charged); Val is hydrophobic. The swap gives hemoglobin a sticky patch.
5. Phenotype. When oxygen is low, the altered hemoglobin molecules clump into stiff rods, bending the cell into a rigid sickle. Sickled cells clog capillaries and break down — pain crises and anemia.

Because GUG codes a different amino acid than GAG, this is a missense mutation. One amino acid out of many — yet it changes everything downstream.

🎲 PART 2 · CHECK THE KEY LINK

The link students most often blur is which amino acid the change produces. Flip the card to lock it in.

🔄 TAP TO FLIP
In sickle-cell disease the mRNA codon changes GAG → GUG. Which amino-acid swap does that cause, and what type of mutation is it?
🔄 ANSWER
Glutamic acid (Glu) → valine (Val). GAG codes Glu; GUG codes Val. Because the new codon codes a different amino acid, it is a missense mutation. Glu is hydrophilic and Val is hydrophobic — that difference is what makes hemoglobin clump. Tap again to flip back.
🧬 PART 3 · FORM FOLLOWS FUNCTION, ALL THE WAY DOWN

Back in Module 2 you learned that a protein works because of its precise folded shape. Sickle-cell disease is that idea made vivid: change one amino acid → change the shape → change the function → change the organism. The disease is recessive (two altered copies needed); people with one copy — sickle-cell trait — are largely healthy and even have some resistance to malaria, a twist you will revisit in Module 6.

🏛️ A HUMAN STORY · THE FIRST “MOLECULAR DISEASE”
In 1949 the chemist Linus Pauling and his colleagues showed that the hemoglobin of people with sickle-cell disease behaved differently in an electric field from normal hemoglobin. Pauling called it a “molecular disease” — the radical idea that an illness could be pinned to a defect in a single kind of molecule. A few years later, in the mid-1950s, Vernon Ingram nailed it down to a single amino-acid change in the beta-globin chain. It was the first time anyone had traced a human disease all the way to one wrong building block in one protein — the exact trace you are walking today.
🚀 CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH · CAN WE FIX A SINGLE LETTER?
If a single base causes the disease, could we correct that single base? Scientists are currently exploring newer CRISPR tools — called base editors and prime editors — that aim to change individual DNA letters without cutting both strands. Recent research is exploring whether such precise edits could one day correct single-base mutations like the one behind sickle-cell disease. Encouragingly, the first CRISPR-based therapies for sickle-cell disease have recently reached patients. These approaches are new and still being studied, not a routine cure — and we will look at how CRISPR actually works in Week 14. For now, hold the bigger idea: understanding the mechanism, letter by letter, is what makes even imagining a repair possible.
