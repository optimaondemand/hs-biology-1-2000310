**1. (Reading 12.01 — the central dogma)**
Which sequence correctly shows the flow of genetic information described by the central dogma?

- A) protein → RNA → DNA
- B) DNA → RNA → protein **✓**
- C) RNA → DNA → protein
- D) DNA → protein → RNA

*The central dogma states that information flows from DNA to RNA (transcription) and then from RNA to protein (translation). DNA is the master copy, RNA is the working copy, and the protein is the final product that does the work.*

**2. (Reading 12.01 — transcription and RNA)**
During transcription, RNA polymerase reads a DNA template and builds an mRNA strand. A base **A** on the DNA template is copied into which base on the mRNA?

- A) Thymine (T)
- B) Guanine (G)
- C) Uracil (U) **✓**
- D) Cytosine (C)

*RNA has no thymine; it uses uracil (U) in its place. So wherever the DNA template reads A, the new mRNA strand gets a U. The other pairings are unchanged (T→A, G→C, C→G). RNA is also single-stranded and uses the sugar ribose.*

**3. (Reading 12.01 — RNA roles)**
A cell makes several kinds of RNA. Which one brings amino acids to the ribosome and has an anticodon that pairs with an mRNA codon?

- A) Messenger RNA (mRNA)
- B) Transfer RNA (tRNA) **✓**
- C) Ribosomal RNA (rRNA)
- D) DNA polymerase

*Transfer RNA (tRNA) is the delivery molecule: it carries a specific amino acid and has a three-base anticodon that base-pairs with the matching mRNA codon. mRNA carries the message; rRNA joins with proteins to build the ribosome itself.*

**4. (Reading 12.02 — the start codon)**
A ribosome begins translating an mRNA. Which codon signals where translation should START?

- A) UAA
- B) UGA
- C) AUG **✓**
- D) UAG

*Translation begins at the start codon AUG, which also codes for the amino acid methionine and sets the reading frame. UAA, UAG, and UGA are the three stop codons — they end translation, not begin it.*

**5. (Reading 12.02 — transcription vs. translation location)**
A student claims that transcription and translation happen in the same place in a human cell. Why is this incorrect?

- A) They both happen in the nucleus
- B) Transcription happens in the nucleus, but translation happens at the ribosome in the cytoplasm **✓**
- C) They both happen at the ribosome
- D) Transcription happens in the mitochondria, translation in the nucleus

*Transcription occurs in the nucleus, where the DNA is, and produces mRNA. The mRNA then travels out to a ribosome in the cytoplasm, where translation builds the protein. The two steps occur in two different places, with mRNA as the courier between them.*

**6. (Reading 12.02 — the universal genetic code)**
The codon AUG codes for the same start signal in a bacterium, a maple tree, and a human. What does this near-universal genetic code provide evidence for?

- A) That all living things share a common ancestor **✓**
- B) That bacteria and humans are unrelated
- C) That the code is different in every species
- D) That proteins are made from DNA directly, with no RNA

*Because nearly all organisms use the same codon dictionary, the simplest explanation is that they inherited it from a shared common ancestor. This universality also has a practical payoff: a human gene placed in a bacterium is read correctly, which is why bacteria can be engineered to make human proteins.*

**7. (Reading 12.03 — silent mutation)**
A substitution changes a codon from UUU to UUC. Both codons code for the amino acid phenylalanine. What kind of mutation is this?

- A) A frameshift mutation
- B) A nonsense mutation
- C) A missense mutation
- D) A silent mutation **✓**

*Because the new codon still codes for the same amino acid (phenylalanine), the protein is unchanged — this is a silent mutation. Several codons can specify the same amino acid, so some single-base changes have no effect at all.*

---

**8. (Reading 12.03 — identifying missense vs. nonsense)**
A point mutation changes a codon so that it now codes for a STOP signal, ending the protein early. What type of substitution is this?

- A) A silent mutation
- B) A nonsense mutation **✓**
- C) A missense mutation
- D) A frameshift mutation

*When a substitution turns an amino-acid codon into a stop codon, the ribosome quits early and produces a short, usually nonfunctional protein — this is a nonsense mutation. A missense mutation instead changes the codon to a different amino acid; a silent mutation changes nothing.*

---

**9. (Reading 12.03 — why a frameshift is worse)**
Why is a frameshift mutation (a single base inserted or deleted) usually more damaging than a single substitution?

- A) It changes only one amino acid in the protein
- B) It shifts the reading frame, so every codon after the change is garbled **✓**
- C) It always turns the first codon into a stop
- D) It removes the entire gene from the chromosome

*The ribosome reads in fixed three-letter steps. Adding or deleting a base (not in a multiple of three) shifts the reading frame, so every codon downstream is misread. A substitution changes at most one codon, but a frameshift scrambles the rest of the message.*

**10. (Reading 12.04 — the sickle-cell trace)**
In sickle-cell disease, a single base change makes the beta-globin mRNA codon read GUG instead of GAG. GAG codes for glutamic acid and GUG codes for valine. What kind of mutation is this, and what is its immediate effect on the protein?

- A) A silent mutation; the hemoglobin is unchanged
- B) A nonsense mutation; the hemoglobin chain ends early
- C) A missense mutation; one amino acid changes from glutamic acid to valine **✓**
- D) A frameshift mutation; every amino acid after the change is wrong

*Because the new codon (GUG) codes for a different amino acid (valine) than the original (GAG, glutamic acid), this is a missense mutation. The single Glu→Val swap replaces a hydrophilic amino acid with a hydrophobic one, which makes hemoglobin molecules clump when oxygen is low, distorting red blood cells into a sickle shape.*
