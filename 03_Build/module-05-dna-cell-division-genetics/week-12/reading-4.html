Anchor Reading · Tracing Sickle-Cell Disease from Base to Phenotype
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
This is the reading the whole week has been building toward. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 12.04, and the conceptual anchor for the Module 5 CER essay. By the end you should be able to trace a single mutation through every step: DNA → mRNA codon → amino acid → protein shape → phenotype, using the real and famous example of sickle-cell disease.

The protein at the center: hemoglobin

Hemoglobin is the protein that fills your red blood cells and carries oxygen from your lungs to every tissue. Part of it is built from a gene called beta-globin. As with every protein, hemoglobin works because of its precise folded shape (the lesson of Module 2). Healthy hemoglobin stays dissolved and smooth, and red blood cells keep their soft, round, doughnut-like form — flexible enough to squeeze through the narrowest capillaries. Now watch what one wrong letter does to all of that.

The trace, step by step

1. DNA. A single base in the beta-globin gene is substituted — one letter changed.
2. mRNA codon. The change makes the mRNA codon read GAG → GUG.
3. Amino acid. GAG codes for glutamic acid (Glu); GUG codes for valine (Val). So the 6th amino acid of the beta-globin chain changes from glutamic acid to valine.
4. Protein shape. Glutamic acid is hydrophilic (water-loving, charged); valine is hydrophobic (water-fearing). Swapping one for the other gives the protein a sticky patch.
5. Phenotype. When oxygen is low, these altered hemoglobin molecules stick together and form long stiff rods that bend the red blood cell into a rigid sickle shape.

Because GUG codes for a different amino acid than GAG, this is a missense mutation — exactly the kind you met in the last reading. Just one of the protein’s many amino acids is swapped, yet that single swap is enough to change everything downstream.

Sickled cells are stiff and pointed. They snag and clog the smallest blood vessels (causing pain crises and tissue damage) and they break apart faster than the body can replace them (causing anemia). One letter in one gene, traced all the way out to a person’s health.

Inheritance, and a twist

Sickle-cell disease is inherited in a recessive pattern: a person must inherit the altered gene from both parents to develop the disease. People with only one copy — called sickle-cell trait — are largely healthy. And here is the twist that connects to evolution: carriers have some resistance to malaria. In regions where malaria is common, carrying one copy can be an advantage, which helps explain why the gene persists. You will return to this idea in Module 6.

Above all, hold onto the through-line of this module: a protein’s function depends on its precise folded shape. Change one amino acid and you change the shape; change the shape and you change the function; change the function and you change the organism. Form follows function, all the way down to a single letter.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

This single trace — DNA to mRNA to amino acid to shape to phenotype — is the spine of your Module 5 CER essay. When you write it, you will not just name sickle-cell disease; you will explain the mechanism, link by link, showing how one changed base becomes one changed life. Lesson 12.04 will walk you through building exactly that argument.
