One Letter, One Disease: Tracing a Mutation

📖 WHAT THIS IS
This is the writing that pulls Module 5 together. Across this module you learned how genetic information is stored (the double helix), copied (replication and cell division), and expressed (transcription and translation). Now you will show that you can follow that whole chain in a single, real example: sickle-cell disease, which begins with one wrong letter in the DNA. Your task is to trace that one change all the way from a single base in a gene to the shape of a protein to a person’s health — explaining the mechanism at every step, not just the names. It is assigned at the start of Week 11 and due at the end of Week 14, so the entire module’s story can inform your argument. Your teacher will read and score this one personally — it is not auto-graded.
🧬 THIS IS A SEMI-STRUCTURED CER
You are given the molecular facts of the case below, and the question to answer. You decide how to organize them into a single coherent argument — what your claim is, which facts count as evidence, and how the mechanism links each step to the next. The facts are the same ones a geneticist would use; the reasoning that joins them into an explanation is yours to write.
🎯 YOUR TASK
Trace a single base substitution in the beta-globin gene from DNA → mRNA codon → amino acid → protein shape → disease phenotype, explaining the mechanism at each step — and use that trace to argue how one change in a single DNA “letter” can produce an inherited disease.
🧬 THE CASE: THE MOLECULAR FACTS YOU ARE GIVEN
Hemoglobin is the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. One of its building blocks is the beta-globin chain, built by translating the beta-globin gene. In a person with sickle-cell disease, the gene differs from the usual version at a single base. The table below follows that one change down the central-dogma chain.
Step in the chain Usual beta-globin Sickle-cell version
mRNA codon (6th codon)GAGGUG
Amino acid placed (position 6)glutamic acid (Glu)valine (Val)
Property of that amino acidhydrophilic (water-loving), chargedhydrophobic (water-fearing)
Effect on the proteinstays dissolved, normal shapemolecules stick together when O₂ is low
Effect on the red blood cellround, flexible discstiff, curved “sickle” shape
Effect on the person (phenotype)healthy oxygen transportblocked vessels, pain, anemia
Note on inheritance: the sickle-cell allele is recessive. A person with two copies has the disease; a person with one copy (sickle-cell trait) is generally healthy and carries some resistance to malaria. You may use this in your Integration.
📝 BUILD YOUR ARGUMENT IN FOUR MOVES
Write in connected paragraphs, not a list. You decide the wording — these are the moves a strong CER makes, not a fill-in-the-blank.
1
Claim. In one or two sentences, state how a single base substitution in the beta-globin gene leads to sickle-cell disease. Make it specific — not “a mutation causes disease,” but a claim that names a single base change producing a changed protein and, through it, a changed phenotype.
2
Evidence. Walk the chain using the specific facts from the case: the codon change GAG → GUG, the amino-acid change glutamic acid → valine at position 6, the change in that amino acid’s chemical property, and the resulting changes to the protein, the red blood cell, and the person. Name each step — don’t skip from “mutation” straight to “disease.”
3
Reasoning — the mechanism. This is the heart of the essay. Explain why each step forces the next. Why does changing one DNA base change one mRNA codon (base pairing and the genetic code)? Why does a different codon place a different amino acid (translation reads codons three letters at a time)? Why does swapping a water-loving amino acid for a water-fearing one change the protein’s shape and make hemoglobin molecules clump — and why does that, in turn, deform the cell and block vessels? Tie this back to Module 2’s rule that a protein’s function comes from its folded shape. Identify the mutation as a missense mutation, and explain how you know. As a check on your reasoning, address one “what if”: why does a silent mutation (one that changes a base but not the amino acid) usually cause no disease at all?
4
Integration. Close by connecting the trace to the module’s bigger story. This single mutation is heritable — it is copied faithfully in replication and passed to the next generation through meiosis (the sickle allele is recessive, so two copies are needed for the disease). That is the double edge of faithful copying: the same precision that lets life pass on a working genome also passes on its errors. You might also note that the universality of the genetic code — the same GUG meaning “valine” in every organism — is exactly what makes the biotechnology of Week 14 (and even gene-editing repair of this very mutation) possible.
🧡 THE VIRTUE THIS PRACTICES · RESPONSIBILITY
This module’s virtue is responsibility — the theme of faithful copying and what we inherit and pass on. A cell copies three billion DNA letters with astonishing care, because a single wrong letter can echo all the way up to a human life, as this case shows. Your CER honors that care by being equally exact: following the chain one verified step at a time, naming the mechanism that links each step to the next, and refusing to skip from cause to consequence without showing the work in between. Precision is a form of responsibility.
✅ SUCCESS CRITERIA
☐
A specific, arguable claim links a single base substitution to the sickle-cell phenotype.
☐
Evidence names every step of the chain — codon GAG→GUG, glutamic acid→valine, the property change, and the effects on protein, cell, and person.
☐
Reasoning explains why each step forces the next (base pairing, the genetic code, shape → function), names the mutation as missense, and contrasts it with a silent mutation.
☐
The essay reads as one connected argument and ties the trace to heredity / faithful copying (~400–700 words).
📊 CER SCORING RUBRIC (3 PTS × 4 CRITERIA = 12 PTS)
Criterion Strong (3) Developing (2) Beginning (1)
Claim Specific and arguable; links a single base change to the sickle-cell phenotype. Somewhat vague or only partly captures the cause-to-phenotype link. Missing, or just restates the prompt.
Evidence Names every step of the chain with the specific codon and amino-acid changes; no gaps. Some steps cited, but general or one or two links skipped. Few facts used, or facts described inaccurately.
Reasoning Explains why each step forces the next (code & shape→function), names it missense, and contrasts a silent mutation. Connects the steps but with gaps in the mechanism, or no silent-mutation contrast. States the steps without explaining the cause.
Integration Reads as one coherent argument and ties the trace to heredity / faithful copying; within the word range. Components present but loosely connected. Listed independently without connection.
