🧬 PART 1 · A MOLECULE THAT CARRIES A MESSAGE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Almost every cell in your body holds the same complete set of DNA — the same instructions. So why does a skin cell look and act so differently from a nerve cell or a muscle cell? (No wrong answer — just check in.)

The nucleotide and the double helix

DNA is a long chain of repeating units called nucleotides. Every nucleotide has exactly three parts: a sugar, a phosphate, and one nitrogen base. The sugars and phosphates link into two long sugar-phosphate backbones — the two rails of a twisted ladder, the double helix. The rungs are pairs of bases reaching in from each side. And the two strands run in opposite directions: they are antiparallel. Only the bases change from unit to unit, so the order of bases is where the message lives.

🧱 PART 2 · THE BASE-PAIRING RULE

A pairs with T; C pairs with G

The four bases are adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), and guanine (G). They do not pair at random. A always pairs with T (held by two hydrogen bonds) and C always pairs with G (held by three). Those hydrogen bonds are the same kind of weak attraction you first met in Module 2 — individually weak, but in their millions, strong enough to hold the helix together while still letting it “unzip” when needed.

A  ⇔  T  (2 H-bonds)  ·   C  ⇔  G  (3 H-bonds)
Bridge to Module 2: this is hydrogen bonding at work — the same idea that gave water its special properties now spells out the genetic code.

✅ THE COMPLEMENT
It reads T – C – G – A – T.
Pair each base by the rule: A→T, G→C, C→G, T→A, A→T. Because the rule is fixed, one strand always determines the other — there is no guesswork.
🔗 PART 3 · WHY THE SHAPE MATTERS

Each strand spells out its partner

Here is the payoff of the structure. Because A always pairs with T and C with G, each strand contains all the information needed to rebuild the other. Pull the two strands apart, and each becomes a template for a perfect new partner. The double helix is not just a beautiful shape — it is a self-describing copy machine. This is exactly the door into your next reading, where you will see how the cell actually does the copying.

🏛️ A HUMAN STORY · 1953, AND THE ETHICS OF CREDIT — REVISITED
You met this story in Module 2, before you knew what the structure did; revisit it now that you do. In April 1953, James Watson and Francis Crick published their model of the DNA double helix in the journal Nature. Their model rested critically on the X-ray diffraction work of Rosalind Franklin — especially the famous “Photo 51” — which they saw through her colleague Maurice Wilkins. Franklin’s contribution was badly under-credited in her lifetime. She died in 1958; the Nobel Prize for the discovery was awarded in 1962 to Watson, Crick, and Wilkins, and Nobel Prizes are not given posthumously, so Franklin could not be honored. This module’s virtue is responsibility — the faithful copying of DNA, yes, but also the faithful, honest assignment of credit. Good science depends on both kinds of honesty.
