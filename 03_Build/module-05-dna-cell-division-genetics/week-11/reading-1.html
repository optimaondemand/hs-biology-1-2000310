Reading 11.01
The Shape of the Code
Anchor Reading · DNA Structure and Base Pairing
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Inside almost every one of your cells is a thread that, stretched out, would reach about two meters — and your body holds enough of it to wrap around the Earth many times over. On that thread is written the complete instructions for building and running you. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 11.01. By the end you should be able to describe the shape of DNA — the famous double helix — name the parts of a nucleotide, and explain the base-pairing rule that lets one strand spell out the other.

A molecule that carries a message

Back in Module 2 you met the four families of large molecules that life is built from, and among them the nucleic acids — the information molecules. DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is the chief of these. It does something no other molecule in the cell does: it stores a message in a chemical alphabet, and it can be copied so that the message is passed on, faithfully, from one cell to its daughters and from one generation to the next. That faithful copying is the thread of this whole module — the quiet responsibility of passing on, intact, what we have inherited.

To understand how a molecule can carry a message, we have to look at its shape. As with the proteins and enzymes you have already studied, in DNA structure is the key to function.

The building block: a nucleotide

DNA is a polymer — a long chain made of repeating units. The unit is called a nucleotide, and each nucleotide has exactly three parts: a sugar (deoxyribose), a phosphate group, and a nitrogen base. The sugar and phosphate are the same in every nucleotide; they link up to form the structural spine of the molecule. Only the base changes from one nucleotide to the next — and so it is the bases that carry the message.

There are four bases, and you should know them by their first letters: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), and guanine (G). A strand of DNA is simply a long sequence of these four letters — ...A–T–G–G–C–A–T... — in a particular order. That order is the information.

Two strands, twisted: the double helix

DNA is not a single strand but two, wound around each other into the elegant spiral we call a double helix — a twisted ladder. The two outer rails of the ladder are the sugar-phosphate backbones: alternating sugar, phosphate, sugar, phosphate, running the whole length of the molecule. The rungs of the ladder are pairs of bases, one reaching in from each strand, joined in the middle by weak hydrogen bonds.

The two strands run in opposite directions — they are antiparallel. Picture two lanes of a road running side by side but with traffic flowing opposite ways. This detail will matter a great deal next lesson, when the molecule is copied.

The base-pairing rule

Here is the heart of the matter. The bases do not pair just any way they please. A always pairs with T, and C always pairs with G. This is the rule of complementary base pairing, and it holds because of the hydrogen bonds you first met in Module 2: an A–T pair is held by two hydrogen bonds, and a C–G pair by three. The shapes and bonding patterns simply fit — A with T, C with G — like two halves of a zipper.

A  ⇔  T   (2 hydrogen bonds)  ·   C  ⇔  G   (3 hydrogen bonds)
Because the pairing is fixed, one strand always spells out the other. If you know one side of the ladder, you know the other.

Why complementarity is everything

Look at what the rule gives us. If one strand reads A–T–G–C, then the strand across from it must read T–A–C–G — there is no other possibility. Each strand contains all the information needed to rebuild its partner. This is not a curiosity; it is the single most important consequence of DNA’s structure, because it tells us exactly how the molecule can be copied. When the two strands separate, each can serve as a template to build a new partner. The structure all but explains the copying — which is why, when the double helix was first proposed, its discoverers noted, with great restraint, that the pairing “immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism.” That mechanism is your next reading.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

A double helix; two antiparallel sugar-phosphate backbones; rungs of paired bases — A with T, C with G. Hold that picture, because everything else in this module hangs on it. The way DNA is copied, the way one cell becomes two, the way traits pass from parent to child, and the way a single wrong letter can cause disease — all of it follows from the shape of the code and the simple, faithful rule that A pairs with T and C pairs with G.
