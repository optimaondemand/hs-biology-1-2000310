Lesson · The Cell Cycle and Mitosis
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You began as one cell. How does one cell become two — each with a complete, correct copy of the DNA?
Now that the DNA is copied, the cell must divide it fairly. Today you will walk the cell cycle (G1, S, G2, then M) and the four phases of mitosis — Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase — followed by cytokinesis, ending in two genetically identical daughter cells.

🧬 PART 1 · THE CELL CYCLE

Grow, copy, prepare, divide

A cell’s life runs in an orderly cell cycle. Most of it is interphase: G1 (the cell grows and works), S (the cell replicates its DNA — exactly the copying from Lesson 11.02), and G2 (more growth and preparation). Only then comes M phase — mitosis, dividing the nucleus — followed by cytokinesis, splitting the cytoplasm into two cells. The logic is strict: copy the DNA in S before dividing it in M. Copy first, then split.

🧱 PART 2 · MITOSIS, PHASE BY PHASE (PMAT)

Four phases, in order

Mitosis pulls the two copies of the DNA apart so each daughter gets a full set. The memory aid is PMAT:

P — Prophase: chromosomes condense and become visible as joined sister chromatids; the nuclear envelope breaks down.
M — Metaphase: chromosomes line up single-file at the cell’s middle (the metaphase plate).
A — Anaphase: sister chromatids separate and are pulled to opposite poles.
T — Telophase: nuclear envelopes re-form around the two sets of chromosomes.

Then cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm, and one cell has become two.

Sort each description into its phase. (A memory trick: Anaphase = chromatids move Apart.)

⚡ SELF-CHECK · WHICH PHASE OF MITOSIS?
For each event, tap the phase of mitosis where it happens. You’ll get instant feedback.

2. Chromosomes line up single-file across the equator of the cell.
3. Sister chromatids separate and are pulled to opposite poles of the cell.
4. New nuclear envelopes re-form around the chromosomes at each pole.
🧡 PART 3 · TWO IDENTICAL CELLS — AND WHEN IT GOES WRONG

For growth and repair

Because each daughter received one copy of every chromosome, mitosis ends in two diploid, genetically identical cells. This is the division of growth, healing, and replacement — new cells just like the old. The cell cycle is also one of the most tightly governed processes in biology: when its controls fail and cells divide without restraint, the result is cancer, the subject you will return to later in this module.

⚡ RESEARCH IN PROGRESS · WATCHING DIVISION IN LIVING TISSUE
Scientists today are exploring ways to watch the cell cycle unfold in living tissue. Using fluorescent tags that glow at different cycle stages, recent research lets researchers see, in real time, exactly when cells pause, divide, or fail to stop — work that may sharpen how doctors understand tumors, which often arise when the cycle’s “stop” signals are ignored. These approaches are still being refined, but they suggest that the orderly cycle you are studying may one day be read like a clock inside a patient’s own cells.
