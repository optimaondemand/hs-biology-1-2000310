**1. (Reading 11.01 — base-pairing rule)**
In a molecule of DNA, which bases pair together across the two strands?

- A) A pairs with C, and T pairs with G
- B) A pairs with G, and T pairs with C
- C) A pairs with T, and C pairs with G **✓**
- D) Any base can pair with any other base

*The base-pairing rule is fixed: adenine (A) always pairs with thymine (T), and cytosine (C) always pairs with guanine (G). An A–T pair is held by two hydrogen bonds and a C–G pair by three. Because the pairing is fixed, one strand always determines the sequence of the other.*

**2. (Reading 11.01 — parts of a nucleotide)**
A nucleotide, the repeating unit of DNA, is made of three parts. What are they?

- A) A sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogen base **✓**
- B) Two sugars and one phosphate
- C) An amino acid, a sugar, and a base
- D) A fatty acid, a phosphate, and a base

*Every DNA nucleotide is built from a sugar, a phosphate group, and one nitrogen base (A, T, C, or G). The sugars and phosphates link into the backbone, which is the same in every nucleotide; only the base changes, so the order of bases is what carries the information. Amino acids and fatty acids belong to proteins and lipids, not DNA.*

**3. (Reading 11.01 — antiparallel double helix)**
The DNA double helix is often compared to a twisted ladder. What forms the two "rails" of that ladder, and how are the two strands arranged?

- A) The bases form the rails, and the strands run in the same direction
- B) The sugar-phosphate backbones form the rails, and the strands run in opposite (antiparallel) directions **✓**
- C) Hydrogen bonds form the rails, and the strands are identical copies
- D) The phosphate groups form the rungs, and the strands are unpaired

*The two outer rails are the sugar-phosphate backbones; the rungs are the paired bases joined by hydrogen bonds. The two strands run in opposite directions, a feature called antiparallel — a detail that matters during replication.*

**4. (Reading 11.02 — semiconservative replication)**
DNA replication is described as "semiconservative." What does this mean about each new double helix that is produced?

- A) Each new molecule is made of two brand-new strands
- B) Each new molecule keeps one original strand and gains one new strand **✓**
- C) Half of the bases are copied and half are left blank
- D) Only one of the two new molecules contains any original DNA

*Semiconservative means each daughter double helix conserves one original (old) strand and pairs it with one newly built strand. Keeping the original strand as a template is part of why replication is so faithful. Meselson and Stahl confirmed this pattern experimentally in 1958.*

**5. (Reading 11.02 — replication enzymes)**
At the replication fork, which enzyme separates the two DNA strands by breaking the hydrogen bonds between paired bases?

- A) DNA polymerase
- B) Ligase
- C) Helicase **✓**
- D) Phosphate

*Helicase is the "unzipper": it breaks the hydrogen bonds holding the base pairs together and separates the strands. DNA polymerase then adds complementary nucleotides to each template strand, and ligase seals the joints between newly made segments.*

**6. (Reading 11.03 — order of the cell cycle)**
A cell must do several things in a strict order before it divides. Which sequence is correct?

- A) The cell divides (M), then copies its DNA (S), then grows (G1)
- B) The cell grows (G1), copies its DNA (S), prepares (G2), then divides (M) **✓**
- C) The cell copies its DNA (S), divides (M), then grows (G1)
- D) The cell prepares (G2), divides (M), then copies its DNA (S)

*The cell cycle runs G1 (growth) → S (DNA is replicated) → G2 (growth and preparation) → M (mitosis and cytokinesis). The logic is strict: the DNA must be copied in S before it can be divided in M. Copy first, then split.*

**7. (Reading 11.03 — a phase of mitosis)**
During which phase of mitosis do the sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles of the cell?

- A) Prophase
- B) Metaphase
- C) Anaphase **✓**
- D) Telophase

*In Anaphase, the sister chromatids are pulled apart to opposite poles (a useful memory aid: Anaphase = Apart). In Prophase the chromosomes condense and the nuclear envelope breaks down; in Metaphase they line up at the equator; in Telophase new nuclear envelopes re-form.*

**8. (Reading 11.03 — product of mitosis)**
A skin cell divides by mitosis to heal a wound. What are the two daughter cells like compared with the original?

- A) Two genetically identical diploid cells **✓**
- B) Four genetically unique haploid cells
- C) Two cells with half the original number of chromosomes
- D) Four cells identical to the parent

*Mitosis produces two daughter cells that are diploid and genetically identical to each other and to the original — exactly what growth, repair, and replacement require. Producing four cells, or halving the chromosome number, describes meiosis, not mitosis.*

**9. (Reading 11.04 — product of meiosis; targets a common misconception)**
A student claims that meiosis, like mitosis, produces cells that are all genetically identical. Why is this claim incorrect?

- A) Meiosis produces four cells that are genetically UNIQUE, because of crossing over and independent assortment **✓**
- B) Meiosis produces two identical cells, just like mitosis
- C) Meiosis produces identical cells, but more slowly than mitosis
- D) Meiosis does not change the DNA at all, so the cells are clones

*Meiosis produces four genetically unique haploid cells, not identical ones. Two mechanisms create the variation: crossing over (homologous chromosomes swap segments) and independent assortment (homologous pairs orient randomly). This variety is exactly why gametes — and the offspring they form — differ.*

**10. (Reading 11.04 — mitosis vs. meiosis; targets a common misconception)**
Which statement correctly contrasts mitosis and meiosis?

- A) Both produce four haploid cells
- B) Both produce two identical diploid cells
- C) Mitosis makes two identical diploid cells; meiosis makes four unique haploid cells **✓**
- D) Mitosis makes gametes; meiosis makes body cells for growth

*Mitosis involves one division and yields two genetically identical diploid cells (for growth and repair); meiosis involves two divisions and yields four genetically unique haploid cells (the gametes). Treating the two as producing the same kind of cells is one of the most common errors in biology.*
