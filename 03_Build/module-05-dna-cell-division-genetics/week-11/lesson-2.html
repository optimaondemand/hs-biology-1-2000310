📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Before a cell divides, it must hand each daughter a complete, correct copy of all its DNA. How does it copy six billion letters — and almost never get one wrong?
Yesterday’s base-pairing rule is the secret. Today you will see how the cell unzips the helix and builds a new strand against each old one — a process called semiconservative replication — and meet the three enzymes that do the work: helicase, DNA polymerase, and ligase.

🧬 PART 1 · KEEP ONE, BUILD ONE

Semiconservative replication

When DNA is copied, the two strands are pulled apart, and each old strand is used as a template to build a new complementary partner. The result: two double helices, each made of one old strand and one new strand. We call this semiconservative — half of each new molecule is conserved from the original. Keeping one original strand as the reference is part of why copying is so accurate. (Meselson and Stahl confirmed this pattern experimentally in 1958.)

🔨 PART 2 · THE CREW AT THE FORK

Three enzymes, three jobs

Copying happens at a Y-shaped replication fork. Helicase unzips the helix, breaking the hydrogen bonds between paired bases. DNA polymerase then moves along each exposed template and adds complementary nucleotides one by one (A across from T, C across from G). Finally ligase seals the joints between newly built segments into one continuous strand. Because the strands are antiparallel, one new strand (the leading strand) is built smoothly and the other (the lagging strand) in short pieces that ligase stitches together — you only need the big picture, not the fine detail.

Match each enzyme to its job.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

Replication is faithful copying in action: helicase unzips, polymerase reads each old strand and lays down its complement, ligase seals the seams, and each new helix keeps one old strand and one new. This is the first and most basic act of responsibility — making an honest, complete copy before anything can be passed on. Next: how the cell takes its two copies and splits in two.
