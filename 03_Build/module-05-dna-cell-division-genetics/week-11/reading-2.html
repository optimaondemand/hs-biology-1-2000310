Reading 11.02
Copying the Code
Anchor Reading · DNA Replication
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Every time one of your cells divides, it must first hand each daughter cell a complete, correct copy of all your DNA — about six billion bases, copied with astonishing accuracy. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 11.02. By the end you should be able to explain what it means that replication is semiconservative, and name the three enzymes that do the work: helicase, DNA polymerase, and ligase.

The structure hands you the answer

Recall the base-pairing rule from Reading 11.01: A pairs with T, C pairs with G, always. That single rule is what makes copying possible. Because each strand spells out its partner exactly, all the cell has to do is pull the two strands apart and then build a fresh, complementary strand against each old one. When it is finished, where there was one double helix there are now two — and each is identical to the original. The structure of DNA practically describes its own method of copying.

Semiconservative: keep one, build one

Notice the clever economy of this. Each new double helix keeps one old strand and pairs it with one brand-new strand. We call this semiconservative replication — semi- meaning “half,” because half of each daughter molecule (one whole strand) is conserved from the parent. The old strand is never thrown away; it is the trustworthy template against which the new strand is checked, base by base.

Scientists confirmed this elegant prediction by careful experiment — Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl demonstrated the semiconservative pattern in 1958 — ruling out the alternatives. Keeping one original strand is part of why copying is so faithful: there is always an authoritative reference to copy from.

The crew of enzymes at the fork

Copying happens at a Y-shaped junction called the replication fork, where the helix is being unzipped. Three enzymes — protein machines, recall, whose jobs come from their shapes — do most of the work:

Helicase — unzips the double helix, breaking the hydrogen bonds between paired bases and separating the two strands.
DNA polymerase — moves along each exposed template strand and adds complementary nucleotides one at a time (A across from T, C across from G), building the new strand.
Ligase — seals up the joints, joining the newly made segments into one continuous strand.

There is one wrinkle worth knowing lightly. Because the two strands are antiparallel (Reading 11.01) and polymerase can build in only one direction, one new strand — the leading strand — is made smoothly in one continuous run, while the other — the lagging strand — is made in short pieces that ligase later stitches together. You do not need the fine detail; just remember that the antiparallel shape forces the two new strands to be built a little differently, and that ligase is the enzyme that finishes the seams.

Faithful, but not perfect

DNA polymerase is remarkably accurate, and it even proofreads its own work, catching and fixing most of its rare mistakes. But no copying is perfect. Occasionally a wrong base is left in place — a mutation. Most mutations are harmless, some are repaired, and a few change the message in ways that matter. You will follow exactly how a single changed letter can ripple outward later in this module; for now, hold the picture of a careful but imperfect scribe, copying six billion letters and getting almost every one right.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Replication is the most basic act of biological responsibility: before a cell can pass anything on, it must first make an honest, complete copy. Helicase unzips, polymerase reads each old strand and lays down its complement, ligase seals the seams, and the result is two double helices where there was one — each keeping one old strand and one new. Hold this, because next you will watch the cell take those two copies and split itself in two.
