Lesson · Meiosis and Genetic Variation
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Brothers and sisters share the same parents — so why is no two of them alike? What kind of cell division makes difference instead of copying?
Mitosis copies faithfully. Meiosis does the opposite: through two divisions, it produces four genetically unique haploid cells — the gametes. Today you will learn how crossing over and independent assortment reshuffle the deck, and you will sharpen the single most important contrast in this module: mitosis versus meiosis.

🧬 PART 1 · A DIVISION THAT HALVES AND SHUFFLES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
If gametes (sperm and egg) were made by ordinary mitosis — keeping the full diploid set — what problem would show up in the very next generation? (No wrong answer — just check in.)

Two divisions, four unique cells

Most body cells are diploid (two sets of chromosomes). Meiosis copies the DNA once, then divides twice (meiosis I and II), ending with four haploid cells — one set each. These are the gametes. When a sperm and egg join, the two halves combine to restore the normal diploid number. And crucially, the four cells are not identical — they are each genetically unique.

🃏 PART 2 · TWO WAYS THE DECK GETS SHUFFLED

Crossing over and independent assortment

Crossing over: early in meiosis I, matching (homologous) chromosomes line up and swap segments, so a chromosome ends up carrying a fresh mix of the mother’s and father’s gene versions. Independent assortment: when the homologous pairs are separated, each pair orients randomly and independently — a coin flip repeated for every pair. Together these produce an enormous number of possible gametes, which is exactly why siblings differ. Each child is a different shuffle of the same deck.

🔗 A BRIDGE FORWARD · TO EVOLUTION

The variation meiosis creates is not idle decoration. In Module 6 you will study natural selection — and selection can only act if individuals differ. Meiosis, through crossing over and independent assortment, is one of the great engines that generates that difference. The shuffling of the deck in every generation is precisely the raw material evolution works on. This is one of the threads that ties the whole course together.

Mitosis copies (two identical cells); meiosis reshuffles (four unique haploid cells). Both serve the module’s theme of responsibility — one passing on an exact copy, the other passing on a fresh combination so life can vary, adapt, and continue.
