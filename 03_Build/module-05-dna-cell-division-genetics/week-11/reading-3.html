Reading 11.03
One Cell Becomes Two
Anchor Reading · The Cell Cycle and Mitosis
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
You began as a single cell. The trillions of cells in your body today all trace back to that one, through round after round of careful division — and you replace millions of cells every minute as you read this. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 11.03. By the end you should be able to walk through the cell cycle (G1, S, G2, and M phase) and name the four phases of mitosis in order — Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase — ending in two genetically identical cells.

The cell cycle: a cell’s orderly life

A cell does not divide on a whim. It moves through an orderly sequence called the cell cycle. Most of that cycle is spent in interphase — the long working stretch between divisions — which has three parts:

G1 (first gap) — the cell grows and carries out its normal work.
S (synthesis) — the cell replicates its DNA (exactly the copying you studied in Reading 11.02), so it now has two complete copies.
G2 (second gap) — the cell grows more and prepares the machinery it needs to divide.

Only after all three is the cell ready for M phase — mitosis, the division of the nucleus — followed by cytokinesis, the splitting of the cytoplasm into two whole cells. Notice the logic: the DNA must be copied (S) before it can be divided (M). Copy first, then split.

Mitosis: four phases, in order

Mitosis is the process that pulls the two copies of the DNA apart so that each daughter cell receives a complete set. Biologists divide it into four phases. A common memory aid is PMAT.

P — Prophase: the copied DNA coils up into compact chromosomes, now visible as two joined sister chromatids; the nuclear envelope breaks down.
M — Metaphase: the chromosomes line up single-file across the middle of the cell (the equator, or metaphase plate).
A — Anaphase: the sister chromatids are pulled apart to opposite ends (poles) of the cell.
T — Telophase: a new nuclear envelope re-forms around each set of chromosomes at the two poles.

Then cytokinesis pinches the cytoplasm in two, and where there was one cell there are now two. Because each new cell got one copy of every chromosome, the two daughters are genetically identical to each other and to the original.

Two identical cells — for growth and repair

Mitosis is the cell division of growth, repair, and replacement. When you grow taller, heal a cut, or replace worn-out skin and blood cells, mitosis is at work, producing more cells just like the ones already there. (Some organisms even reproduce this way, making whole new individuals by mitosis alone.) The product is always the same: two diploid, genetically identical daughter cells. Hold that result, because next lesson you will meet a very different kind of division — one whose whole purpose is to make daughter cells that are not identical.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

The cell cycle is faithful copying made visible: grow (G1), copy the DNA (S), prepare (G2), then divide the nucleus through PMAT and split the cytoplasm (cytokinesis). The result — two identical cells — is the cell honoring its responsibility to pass on a complete and correct set of instructions. When this orderly process loses its controls and cells divide without restraint, the result is cancer; the cell cycle is among the most carefully governed processes in all of biology.
