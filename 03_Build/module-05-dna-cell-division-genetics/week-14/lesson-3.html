Lesson · PCR and Its Applications
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A single molecule of DNA is too little to study. How do we make a billion copies of just the part we want?
Today you meet PCR — the polymerase chain reaction — which borrows the cell’s own copying machinery (Week 11) and runs it in a tube. You will learn its three repeating steps (denature, anneal, extend) and where PCR shows up in real life, from forensics to a COVID-19 test.

🌡️ PART 1 · THREE STEPS, DRIVEN BY TEMPERATURE

Denature, anneal, extend

A PCR machine simply cycles the temperature, and each temperature drives one step. Denature (~95°C): heat pulls the double helix into two single strands. Anneal (~50–60°C): cooling lets short primers bind to the edges of the target region. Extend (~72°C): a heat-stable polymerase (Taq) builds a new complementary strand from each primer. Then it repeats — and because every copy becomes a template, the target doubles each cycle, reaching billions of copies in ~20–35 cycles. Match each step to its temperature.

🔎 PART 2 · WHAT THE COPIES ARE FOR

Amplify a tiny sample and the invisible becomes readable. DNA fingerprinting copies stretches that vary from person to person, producing band patterns (read on a gel from Lesson 14.02) used in forensics and paternity cases. PCR also detects the DNA of disease-causing organisms; the COVID-19 PCR test amplifies a piece of the virus’s genetic material so even a small infection becomes detectable. 💡 TAP TO REVEAL
PCR copies DNA in a tube. Which two ideas from Week 11’s natural DNA replication does it reuse?
✅ THE ANSWER
(1) Complementary base-pairing — A pairs with T, G with C, so each single strand is a template for a faithful new strand. (2) DNA polymerase — the enzyme that reads a template and builds the new strand. PCR adds only heat-cycling and primers to aim and repeat the cell’s own copying. Nothing here is new chemistry — it is replication, sped up and targeted.
📜 PART 3 · A HUMAN STORY
🏛️ A HUMAN STORY · AN IDEA ON A MOUNTAIN ROAD
Some of the most important ideas in science arrive in the most ordinary moments. In 1983, the chemist Kary Mullis was driving Highway 128 through the California redwoods at night when the logic of PCR fell into place in his mind: separate the strands, let primers bind to the edges of the stretch you want, let a polymerase copy it — then do it again, and again, each round doubling the copies. It was an idea built entirely from biology students already knew, recombined into something new. PCR went on to transform medicine, forensics, and research, and Mullis shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1993. It is a reminder that creativity in science is often not a new fact but a new arrangement of familiar ones.
