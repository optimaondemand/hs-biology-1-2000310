Reading 14.03
Copying DNA by the Billions
Anchor Reading · PCR and Its Applications
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
A single molecule of DNA — from a drop of blood, a cheek swab, a virus — is far too little to study. We need millions of copies. The cell already knows how to copy DNA (Week 11); biotechnology learned to do it in a tube, on demand, at blinding speed. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 14.03. By the end you should be able to describe the three repeating steps of PCR (the polymerase chain reaction) and name what it is used for.

Borrowing the cell’s own copier

PCR stands for the polymerase chain reaction. Its whole genius is to take the machinery the cell already uses to replicate DNA — the enzyme DNA polymerase and the rule of complementary base-pairing (A–T, G–C) from Week 11 — and run it over and over in a test tube to copy one chosen stretch of DNA. Because each new copy can itself be copied in the next round, the number of copies doubles every cycle. After about 20 to 35 cycles, a single target stretch has become billions of copies — enough to see, sort, and study.

To aim the copying at just the region you want, PCR uses short pieces of DNA called primers that match the borders of the target. The polymerase only extends from where a primer has bound, so only the chosen region is amplified.

Three steps, repeated by temperature

A PCR machine simply raises and lowers the temperature, and each temperature drives one step. One cycle has three:

1. Denature (~95°C). High heat pulls the double helix apart into two single strands — just as melting separates the two strands, exposing the letters to be copied.
2. Anneal (~50–60°C). The mixture is cooled so the short primers can bind (anneal) to their matching sequences at the edges of the target region.
3. Extend (~72°C). A heat-stable DNA polymerase (the famous one is called Taq) starts at each primer and builds a new complementary strand, copying the target.

Then the cycle repeats. A “heat-stable” polymerase is essential, because an ordinary one would be destroyed by the 95°C denaturing step (remember from Module 4 that high heat denatures most proteins). Taq, drawn from a heat-loving microbe, survives — so the same enzyme keeps working through every cycle.

What billions of copies are good for

Once a tiny sample is amplified, it can be analyzed. DNA fingerprinting uses PCR to copy stretches of DNA that vary from person to person; the band patterns (read on a gel from Lesson 14.02) help identify individuals in forensics and settle questions of paternity. PCR also powers medical testing: it can detect the DNA of a disease-causing organism in a patient, and the COVID-19 PCR test works by amplifying a piece of the virus’s genetic material so even a small infection becomes detectable.

In every case the principle is the same: copy faithfully, copy enough, and the invisible becomes readable. It is the cell’s own talent for faithful copying — this module’s thread of responsibility — turned into a tool.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

PCR is one of the most important tools in all of modern biology, and you already understand the science under it: base-pairing and a copying enzyme, the same ideas you met in Week 11. Nothing here is magic — it is the cell’s replication logic, sped up and aimed. That is the pattern of biotechnology: take what life already does faithfully, and put it to work.
