Lesson · Cell-Cycle Checkpoints and Cancer
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A cell copies itself trillions of times. What keeps the copying from running away?
This week opens Module 5’s final stretch and folds in biotechnology. Today we look at what happens when the cell’s faithful copying loses its governor. You will meet the three checkpoints that police the cell cycle, the genes that run them, and the central idea that cancer is a genetic disease — a failure of control caused by mutations in the genes that govern division.

🛡️ PART 1 · THE CELL CYCLE HAS INSPECTIONS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A factory that copies a delicate part billions of times needs inspection stations, not just a fast assembly line. What would you guess the cell inspects before it lets division continue? (No wrong answer — just check in.)

Three checkpoints

The cell cycle does not roll forward unchecked. At three checkpoints it pauses to inspect. The G1/S checkpoint asks whether the DNA is undamaged and the cell is ready to commit to copying. The G2/M checkpoint asks whether the DNA was fully and correctly replicated. The spindle (M) checkpoint asks whether every chromosome is properly attached to the spindle, so each daughter cell will receive a complete set. Pass the inspection and the cycle continues; fail it, and the cycle halts for repair — or the cell undergoes apoptosis, an orderly self-destruction, rather than pass on damage. This is the cell’s responsibility to its descendants, enforced.

🧬 PART 2 · CANCER IS A GENETIC DISEASE

Brakes, accelerators, and what breaks

The checkpoints are run by proteins, and proteins come from genes. Tumor-suppressor genes are the brakes — the famous p53, the “guardian of the genome,” can halt a damaged cell or trigger apoptosis. Proto-oncogenes are the accelerator, pushing division when growth is needed. When mutations (Week 12) damage these genes — disabling a brake like p53, or jamming the accelerator into an over-active oncogene — control fails. The cell ignores its checkpoints, refuses apoptosis, and divides uncontrollably into a tumor; some cells break free and spread (metastasis). This is why we say cancer is a genetic disease: it is caused by mutations in the genes that govern the cell cycle. The mutations may be inherited (born with one faulty copy) or acquired from carcinogens, UV light, smoking, or random copying errors.

🔬 PART 3 · SCIENCE ON THE EDGE OF THE FIELD

If cancer is a disease of specific mutated genes, then reading those genes should guide treatment — and that is exactly where the field is moving.

🏹 CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH · READING A TUMOR’S GENOME
Recent research increasingly treats cancer as a problem to be read. Scientists are now able to sequence the DNA of a patient’s tumor to find the specific “driver” mutations behind it, and researchers are exploring ways to tailor treatment to those mutations rather than relying only on broad therapies that strike every dividing cell. A different and active line of work, immunotherapy, explores how to enlist a patient’s own immune system against cancer cells. These approaches are promising and rapidly developing — but they are not magic cures, they do not work for every cancer or every patient, and much remains uncertain. Notice the connection forward: immunotherapy depends on the controlled cell division of immune cells you will study in Module 9.

Faithful copying is governed copying. Checkpoints inspect; tumor-suppressor and proto-oncogene proteins balance brake and accelerator; and when the genes that run them are mutated, control breaks down into cancer.
