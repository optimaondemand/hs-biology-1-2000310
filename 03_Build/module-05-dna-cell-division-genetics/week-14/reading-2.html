Reading 14.02
Cutting and Pasting DNA
Anchor Reading · Recombinant DNA and Gel Electrophoresis
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
If the genetic code is universal — if A pairs with T and G with C in a bacterium exactly as in a human (Week 12) — then a gene from one organism, moved into another, should still be readable. That single idea launched biotechnology. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 14.02. By the end you should be able to explain how scientists cut, combine, and paste DNA to make recombinant DNA, and how gel electrophoresis sorts DNA fragments by size.

Molecular scissors: restriction enzymes

To work with a gene you first have to cut it out cleanly. The tools for this are restriction enzymes — proteins that recognize a specific short sequence of DNA letters and cut the double helix wherever that sequence appears. One famous example, EcoRI, finds the sequence GAATTC and cuts it. Many restriction enzymes cut the two strands in a slightly staggered way, leaving short single-stranded overhangs called sticky ends.

Why “sticky”? Because any two pieces of DNA cut by the same enzyme have complementary overhangs — and complementary single strands pair up (A–T, G–C). So a human gene and a bacterial DNA cut with the same enzyme will snap together at their matching sticky ends. The base-pairing rule you learned in Week 11 is what makes the cutting and pasting possible.

A vehicle and a glue: plasmids and ligase

A gene by itself cannot get into a bacterium and be used. It needs a vector — a carrier. The usual carrier is a plasmid: a small, circular piece of DNA that bacteria naturally carry apart from their main chromosome. Scientists cut open a plasmid with the same restriction enzyme used on the gene, so the plasmid and the gene have matching sticky ends.

When the gene’s sticky ends pair with the plasmid’s, the join is still just resting in place. An enzyme called DNA ligase — the same kind of “molecular glue” the cell uses to seal DNA during replication — seals the backbone permanently. The result is a single circular molecule carrying DNA from two sources: recombinant DNA. Slip this recombinant plasmid into living bacteria (a step called transformation), and as the bacteria multiply they read the foreign gene and make its protein — turning a flask of bacteria into a tiny protein factory.

Sorting the pieces: gel electrophoresis

Once you have cut DNA into fragments, you need a way to see and separate them. Gel electrophoresis does exactly that. Samples of DNA are loaded into wells at one end of a slab of gel, and an electric current is switched on across it. DNA’s sugar-phosphate backbone is negatively charged, so every fragment is pulled toward the positive electrode at the far end.

But they do not all travel at the same speed. The gel is a dense mesh, and smaller fragments slip through it more easily and travel farther, while larger fragments are held back and stay closer to the wells. After the run, the fragments have sorted themselves into bands by length. This is the key idea, and it is easy to get backward, so say it carefully: gel electrophoresis separates DNA by size, not by charge (every fragment carries the same kind of negative charge), and smaller fragments travel farther, not larger ones.

Negatively charged DNA → pulled toward the + electrode
Separation is by size: small fragments travel farther, large fragments stay near the wells.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Cut, combine, paste, and sort — these four moves are the grammar of every later technique in this week. Recombinant DNA gave us bacteria that make human medicines; gel electrophoresis lets us read the results of every cut and copy. Both work only because the genetic code is shared across all of life — the same deep unity you will meet again in evolution (Module 6). What we cut from one genome, another can faithfully read.
