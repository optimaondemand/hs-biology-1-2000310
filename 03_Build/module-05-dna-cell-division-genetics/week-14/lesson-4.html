💊 PART 1 · A TRIUMPH OF CUTTING AND PASTING

Recombinant human insulin

For decades, insulin for people with diabetes was extracted from the pancreases of pigs and cattle — slightly different from human insulin and limited in supply. Using exactly the cut-combine-paste-transform process from Lesson 14.02, scientists inserted the human insulin gene into bacteria, which then read the universal code and manufactured genuine human insulin. The first such product reached patients in the early 1980s. Today the hormone is brewed in vats of engineered cells. It is the clearest proof that recombinant DNA is not a curiosity but a medicine. (Note: the original discovery of insulin in the 1920s is a separate story you will meet in Module 9 — here we mean only the bacterial production of human insulin.)

✂️ PART 2 · CRISPR-CAS9, A PROGRAMMABLE EDITOR

Guide, find, cut, repair

Unlike a restriction enzyme that cuts only at one fixed sequence, CRISPR-Cas9 can be aimed. A short guide RNA is written to match the exact DNA sequence you want to target (by base-pairing). The Cas9 protein carries the guide along the genome until it finds the matching sequence, and there Cas9 cuts the DNA. The cell’s repair machinery then either disables the gene or, with a supplied template, rewrites it. Change the guide RNA and you change the target. Match each part of the system to its job.

🏛️ A HUMAN STORY · BORROWED FROM BACTERIA
CRISPR was not invented in a vacuum — it was borrowed from bacteria, which use a CRISPR system as a kind of immune memory, recognizing and cutting the DNA of viruses that attacked them before. In 2012, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier published how this natural defense could be turned into a programmable tool for editing any genome — aim the guide RNA, and Cas9 cuts where you choose. For that work they shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. It is a striking case of the connection this course keeps making: a trick that life evolved for one purpose (a bacterium’s defense, Module 9’s territory) becomes, in human hands, a tool for another.
⚖️ PART 3 · JUST BECAUSE WE CAN — SHOULD WE?

Editing the body (somatic) cells of one patient affects only that person. Editing the germline — eggs, sperm, or embryos — changes every cell of a future person and is passed to their descendants. The germline-editing debate is genuinely unsettled, and it is one we all share.

🤖 AI AWARENESS · MACHINE LEARNING IN GENE EDITING
Designing a CRISPR edit means choosing a guide RNA that hits the right target and not the wrong ones. Scientists are increasingly using machine-learning models to predict which guide RNAs will work well and to flag likely off-target cut sites — places the guide might accidentally match. The opportunity is real: such tools can speed up design and help researchers avoid dangerous edits. But here is the pitfall, and it matters: these models can be confidently wrong. They are trained on limited data and can carry its biases, so a prediction that looks certain may simply be mistaken. An AI’s suggested guide is a hypothesis to test in the lab, never an authority to trust on its own — every prediction still needs wet-lab confirmation. Treat an AI output the way this whole course teaches you to treat any claim: ask what the evidence is, and check it.
