**1. (Reading 14.01 — cell-cycle checkpoints)**
At the G2/M checkpoint, what does a cell verify before it proceeds toward division?

- A) That its DNA was fully and correctly replicated **✓**
- B) That it has absorbed enough sunlight
- C) That its cell wall is fully built
- D) That all its proteins have been digested

*The G2/M checkpoint inspects whether DNA replication (the S phase) finished completely and without error before the cell commits to mitosis. The G1/S checkpoint instead checks for DNA damage and readiness to copy, and the spindle checkpoint checks chromosome attachment.*

**2. (Reading 14.01 — cancer as a genetic disease)**
Why is cancer described as a genetic disease at the level of the cell?

- A) It is always inherited from a parent
- B) It is caused by mutations in the genes that control the cell cycle **✓**
- C) It is caused by bacteria invading the cell
- D) It only occurs in cells that lack DNA

*Cancer is genetic at the cell level because it arises from mutations in genes that govern division — disabling tumor-suppressor "brakes" or over-activating proto-oncogene "accelerators." This is true whether the mutations are inherited or acquired during life, so "always inherited" is incorrect.*

**3. (Reading 14.01 — inherited vs. acquired mutations)**
A person develops a mutation in a skin cell after years of heavy sun exposure, leading to skin cancer. This mutation is best described as:

- A) Inherited, because all cancer comes from parents
- B) Acquired, because it arose during the person's life from an environmental cause **✓**
- C) Inherited, because skin is present at birth
- D) Neither inherited nor acquired

*UV radiation in sunlight is a carcinogen that can damage DNA during a person's lifetime. A mutation caused this way is acquired, not inherited. Inherited mutations are ones a person is born with, such as a faulty tumor-suppressor copy passed down from a parent.*

**4. (Reading 14.02 — recombinant DNA)**
A scientist cuts a human gene and a bacterial plasmid with the same restriction enzyme and joins them into a single molecule. What has the scientist made?

- A) A protein
- B) A ribosome
- C) Recombinant DNA **✓**
- D) An mRNA strand

*Combining DNA from two sources — here a human gene and a bacterial plasmid — into one molecule produces recombinant DNA. Using the same restriction enzyme gives both pieces matching sticky ends so they pair up, and DNA ligase then seals them together.*

**5. (Reading 14.02 — what gel electrophoresis separates by)**
A student says, "Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments by their electric charge." Why is this incorrect?

- A) DNA fragments have no charge at all
- B) The fragments are separated by size, not charge, since every fragment carries the same kind of negative charge **✓**
- C) The fragments are separated by color
- D) Charge has no effect because the gel blocks the current

*Every DNA fragment has a negatively charged backbone, so charge alone cannot tell them apart — they all move toward the positive electrode. What differs is size: the gel mesh slows larger fragments and lets smaller ones through, so the separation is by size.*

**6. (Reading 14.02 — which fragments travel farther)**
After a gel electrophoresis run, which DNA fragments are found farthest from the wells?

- A) The largest fragments
- B) The fragments with the most positive charge
- C) The smallest fragments **✓**
- D) All fragments travel exactly the same distance

*Smaller fragments slip through the gel's mesh more easily and therefore travel farther from the wells. Larger fragments are held back and stay closer to the wells. (A common error is to assume larger fragments travel farther — the opposite is true.)*

**7. (Reading 14.03 — the three steps of PCR)**
PCR copies a target stretch of DNA by repeating three steps in each cycle. Which list gives the three steps?

- A) Transcribe, translate, fold
- B) Cut, ligate, transform
- C) Denature, anneal, extend **✓**
- D) Inhale, store, exhale

*One PCR cycle denatures the DNA (~95 °C, strands separate), anneals primers to the target's edges (~50–60 °C), and extends new strands with a heat-stable polymerase (~72 °C). "Cut, ligate, transform" describes recombinant-DNA cloning, not PCR.*

**8. (Reading 14.03 — why PCR needs a heat-stable polymerase)**
PCR uses the heat-stable polymerase Taq. Why is a heat-stable enzyme necessary?

- A) Because the reaction is run in the dark
- B) Because each cycle heats the mixture to about 95 °C to separate the strands, which would destroy an ordinary polymerase **✓**
- C) Because Taq is the only enzyme that can read RNA
- D) Because cold temperatures break apart all proteins

*The denaturing step heats the tube to about 95 °C every cycle. Most proteins, including ordinary DNA polymerases, would denature (unfold and stop working) at that temperature. Taq, from a heat-loving microbe, survives the heat, so the same enzyme keeps copying through every cycle.*

**9. (Reading 14.04 — where CRISPR-Cas9 came from)**
The CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing tool is based on a system found naturally in:

- A) The human nervous system
- B) Plant chloroplasts
- C) Animal muscle tissue
- D) Bacteria, where it acts as a defense against viruses **✓**

*CRISPR began as a bacterial immune system: bacteria use it to recognize and cut the DNA of viruses that have attacked them. Researchers repurposed that natural defense into a programmable tool for editing the DNA of other organisms.*

**10. (Reading 14.04 — recombinant insulin)**
Human insulin used to treat diabetes is now produced by inserting the human insulin gene into bacteria, which then make the hormone. This is possible mainly because:

- A) Bacteria and humans share an almost identical body shape
- B) Insulin is not actually a protein
- C) The genetic code is universal, so bacteria can read a human gene and build its protein **✓**
- D) Bacteria naturally produce human insulin on their own

*Recombinant insulin works because the genetic code is universal — the same DNA-to-protein rules operate in bacteria as in humans. So a human insulin gene placed in bacteria is read and translated into genuine human insulin, replacing insulin once extracted from animals.*
