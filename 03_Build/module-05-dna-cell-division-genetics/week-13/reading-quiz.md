**1. (Reading 13.01 — genotype vs. phenotype)**
In pea plants, purple flower color (P) is dominant to white (p). A plant has the genotype Pp. Which statement is correct?

- A) The plant is homozygous and white
- B) The plant is heterozygous and purple **✓**
- C) The plant is homozygous and purple
- D) The plant is heterozygous and white

*A Pp plant carries two different alleles, so it is heterozygous. Because the dominant P allele is present and masks the recessive p, the plant's phenotype is purple. It still carries p and can pass it to offspring.*

**2. (Reading 13.01 — the monohybrid 3:1 ratio)**
Two heterozygous purple-flowered plants (Pp) are crossed. What phenotype ratio is expected in the offspring?

- A) 1 purple : 1 white
- B) All purple
- C) 3 purple : 1 white **✓**
- D) 9 purple : 3 white

*A Pp × Pp cross gives genotypes 1 PP : 2 Pp : 1 pp. Because PP and Pp both appear purple, three of every four offspring are purple and one is white — a 3:1 phenotype ratio. The single white plant is the only one that inherited two recessive alleles.*

**3. (Reading 13.01 — the "dominant means common" misconception)**
In a certain population, attached earlobes (a recessive trait) are much more common than free earlobes (dominant). What does this tell you about the meaning of "dominant"?

- A) The data must be wrong, because a dominant trait is always more common
- B) "Dominant" refers to which allele is expressed when both are present, not how common a trait is **✓**
- C) The attached-earlobe allele must actually be dominant
- D) Dominance and recessiveness switch depending on population size

*"Dominant" describes which allele is expressed when an organism carries both — it says nothing about how frequent a trait is in a population. A recessive trait can easily be the more common one. Allele frequency and dominance are entirely separate ideas.*

**4. (Reading 13.01 / 13.02 — the Law of Segregation)**
The Law of Segregation states that the two alleles for a trait separate from each other during gamete formation. In which cellular process does this separation actually occur?

- A) Meiosis **✓**
- B) Photosynthesis
- C) Protein synthesis
- D) Diffusion

*The two alleles of a gene separate when homologous chromosomes are pulled apart during meiosis, so each gamete carries only one allele. Mendel deduced this rule long before anyone could observe meiosis under a microscope.*

**5. (Reading 13.02 — the dihybrid 9:3:3:1 ratio)**
Two plants heterozygous for both seed shape and seed color (RrYy × RrYy) are crossed. R (round) is dominant to r (wrinkled) and Y (yellow) is dominant to y (green). What phenotype ratio is expected?

- A) 3 : 1
- B) 1 : 2 : 1
- C) 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 **✓**
- D) 1 : 1 : 1 : 1

*A cross between two double-heterozygotes follows independent assortment and produces the classic dihybrid ratio of 9 (both dominant) : 3 : 3 : 1 (both recessive). It is the signature pattern of two genes assorting independently.*

**6. (Reading 13.02 — probability and the multiplication rule)**
In the cross RrYy × RrYy above, what fraction of offspring are expected to be wrinkled AND green (both recessive)?

- A) 9/16
- B) 3/16
- C) 1/4
- D) 1/16 **✓**

*Because the genes are independent, multiply the separate probabilities. Wrinkled (rr) has a chance of 1/4 and green (yy) has a chance of 1/4, so wrinkled-and-green is 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16 — the single "1" in the 9:3:3:1 ratio.*

**7. (Reading 13.03 — codominance and ABO blood types)**
A person has the genotype I^A I^B and therefore has blood type AB, showing both the A marker and the B marker fully on their red blood cells. This is an example of:

- A) Incomplete dominance, because the markers blend together
- B) Codominance, because both alleles are fully and separately expressed **✓**
- C) A simple recessive trait
- D) Sex-linkage

*In codominance the heterozygote expresses both alleles fully and separately, rather than blending them. A type-AB person makes both the A and the B marker at full strength — not an in-between marker — which is exactly codominance, not incomplete dominance.*

**8. (Reading 13.03 — X-linked inheritance and probability)**
Color blindness is an X-linked recessive trait. A carrier mother (X^C X^c) has children with a father who has normal vision (X^C Y). What fraction of their SONS are expected to be color blind?

- A) None of the sons
- B) 1/4 of the sons
- C) 1/2 of the sons **✓**
- D) All of the sons

*Every son inherits his single X chromosome from his mother. Half of her X chromosomes carry the color-blindness allele (c) and half carry the normal allele (C), so half of the sons will be color blind. Sons receive only a Y from the father, so the father's normal vision cannot mask the trait.*

**9. (Reading 13.04 — nondisjunction and trisomy 21)**
Down syndrome (trisomy 21) results from a person having three copies of chromosome 21 instead of two. What error is the most common cause?

- A) A mutation that changes one DNA base in a gene
- B) Nondisjunction, when chromosomes fail to separate properly during meiosis **✓**
- C) The cell making too much protein
- D) A virus inserting extra DNA

*Trisomy 21 is caused by nondisjunction — when homologous chromosomes (or sister chromatids) fail to separate during meiosis, producing a gamete with an extra chromosome 21. When that gamete is fertilized, every cell of the new individual carries three copies of chromosome 21.*

**10. (Reading 13.04 — reading a pedigree)**
In a pedigree, two parents who do NOT show a trait have a child who DOES show it. What is the most likely mode of inheritance for the trait?

- A) Dominant, because the child clearly inherited it
- B) Recessive, because both parents must have been hidden carriers **✓**
- C) It cannot be inherited at all
- D) The pedigree must contain an error

*If neither parent shows the trait but a child does, the allele must have been carried but hidden in both parents — each was a heterozygous carrier of a recessive allele. A dominant allele could not hide in an unaffected parent, so this pattern points to recessive inheritance.*
