Reading 13.02
Two Traits at Once
Anchor Reading · Independent Assortment, 9:3:3:1, and Probability
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
One trait at a time gives the clean 3:1 ratio you met in Lesson 13.01. But real organisms inherit thousands of traits together — does the gene for seed color care what the gene for seed shape is doing? Mendel asked exactly this. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 13.02. By the end you should be able to explain the Law of Independent Assortment, set up a dihybrid cross and read its 9:3:3:1 ratio, and use the multiplication rule of probability to predict offspring without drawing a sixteen-box grid every time.

Following two genes at once

Mendel did not stop at one trait. He tracked two at the same time — for example seed shape (round R is dominant to wrinkled r) and seed color (yellow Y is dominant to green y). He started with plants that were true-breeding for both: round-yellow (RRYY) crossed with wrinkled-green (rryy). Every offspring in the next generation was round and yellow — both dominant traits — and every one carried the genotype RrYy. A cross following two traits like this is called a dihybrid cross.

The interesting question came in the next generation, when he crossed those RrYy plants with each other. If shape and color traveled together as a pair, he would only ever see the two parent combinations: round-yellow and wrinkled-green. But that is not what happened.

The Law of Independent Assortment

New combinations appeared: round-green seeds and wrinkled-yellow seeds — combinations neither true-breeding grandparent had shown. The two genes had been shuffled independently. This is Mendel’s second rule, the Law of Independent Assortment: the alleles of one gene separate into gametes independently of the alleles of another gene. The seed-shape gene does not “ask” what the seed-color gene is doing. (We now know this holds for genes on different chromosomes, because of the way chromosome pairs line up randomly during meiosis — Week 11.)

Because each gene sorts on its own, a parent that is RrYy can make four kinds of gamete in equal numbers: RY, Ry, rY, and ry. Crossing two such parents means combining four gamete types with four gamete types — a sixteen-box Punnett square.

The 9:3:3:1 ratio

When you fill in all sixteen boxes of an RrYy × RrYy cross and sort the offspring by their appearance, a famous pattern appears — the 9:3:3:1 phenotype ratio:

9 round & yellow (both dominant) · 3 round & green · 3 wrinkled & yellow · 1 wrinkled & green (both recessive)

Notice the logic: 9 of 16 show both dominant traits, 1 of 16 shows both recessive traits, and the remaining 6 are the new mixed combinations. The 9:3:3:1 ratio is the dihybrid fingerprint — whenever you see it in data, two genes are assorting independently, each behaving like its own 3:1 cross laid over the other.

A shortcut: the multiplication rule

Drawing sixteen boxes is slow, and for three or four genes it becomes impossible. Probability gives a faster path. Because the genes are independent, you can treat each gene as its own small cross and then multiply the separate probabilities — the same rule you use for independent coin flips.

Take RrYy × RrYy. For shape alone, Rr × Rr gives 3/4 round and 1/4 wrinkled. For color alone, Yy × Yy gives 3/4 yellow and 1/4 green. To find the chance of an offspring that is round and yellow, multiply: 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16. For wrinkled and green: 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16. The whole 9:3:3:1 ratio falls out of two simple multiplications.

The rule in one line. When events are independent, the probability that all of them happen is the product of their separate probabilities. Genetics leans on this constantly — it turns a sixteen-box grid into one line of arithmetic.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Independent assortment is the reason siblings are not identical: every gamete is a fresh, independent shuffle of the alleles a parent carries. That shuffling is the source of the variation evolution will act on later in the course (Module 6). For now, hold two tools — the 9:3:3:1 dihybrid ratio and the multiplication rule — and you can predict inheritance for as many independent genes as you like.
