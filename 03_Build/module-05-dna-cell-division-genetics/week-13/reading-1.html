📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Why do some children have a parent’s eyes but not their hair? Why can two brown-eyed parents have a blue-eyed child? For most of human history these questions had only folk answers — until a quiet friar counting peas in a monastery garden found the rules hiding inside them. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 13.01. By the end you should be able to use the language of genetics — gene, allele, dominant, recessive, genotype, phenotype, homozygous, heterozygous — and explain the Law of Segregation and how a monohybrid cross predicts the famous 3:1 ratio.

A friar with a question and a garden of peas

In the 1850s and 1860s, a friar named Gregor Mendel ran experiments in the garden of his abbey in what is now Brno. He chose the garden pea because it was easy to grow, bred quickly, and came in clear either-or traits: flowers were purple or white, seeds round or wrinkled, plants tall or short. Most importantly, he could control which plant fertilized which — he could decide the parents. Over about eight years he bred and counted tens of thousands of plants, keeping careful tallies of how the traits showed up generation after generation.

What Mendel did differently was count. Earlier naturalists described inheritance in vague terms — offspring were a “blend” of their parents, like mixing paint. But blending could not explain what Mendel kept seeing: a trait would vanish in one generation and then reappear, unchanged, in the next. Blended paint never un-mixes. Something was being passed down whole, hidden but not lost.

A trait that disappears, then comes back

When Mendel crossed a true-breeding purple-flowered plant with a true-breeding white-flowered one, every plant in the next generation was purple. White had vanished. But when he let those purple offspring breed with each other, white came back — in about one out of every four plants. The numbers were not random: across thousands of plants the ratio kept landing near three purple to one white.

Mendel reasoned that each plant must carry two hidden “factors” for a trait, one from each parent. One factor could mask the other without erasing it. The masking factor he called dominant; the hidden one, recessive. White only showed when a plant happened to inherit two recessive factors with no dominant one to cover them. He had no idea what these factors were made of — DNA, chromosomes, and meiosis were all unknown to him — yet he deduced the rules they obey. We now call his “factors” genes.

The words geneticists use

To say precisely what Mendel found, biology needs a short, exact vocabulary. A gene is the instruction for a trait. A allele is one version of a gene — purple flowers and white flowers are two alleles of the flower-color gene. By convention we write a dominant allele with a capital letter (P) and a recessive allele with the matching lowercase letter (p).

An organism’s genotype is the pair of alleles it actually carries (PP, Pp, or pp). Its phenotype is the trait you can observe (purple or white). If the two alleles are the same — PP or pp — the organism is homozygous for that gene. If they differ — Pp — it is heterozygous. A heterozygous plant (Pp) looks purple, because the dominant P masks the recessive p, but it still carries p and can pass it on.

One careful warning. “Dominant” does not mean “more common.” It only describes which allele is expressed when both are present. A recessive trait can be far more common in a population than a dominant one — dominance is about masking, not about counting heads.

The Law of Segregation and the 3:1 ratio

Mendel’s first great rule is the Law of Segregation: an organism’s two alleles for a trait separate from each other when gametes (egg and sperm cells) are formed, so each gamete carries only one allele. We now know this separation is exactly what happens in meiosis — the division you studied in Week 11, when homologous chromosomes are pulled apart. Mendel deduced the rule decades before anyone could see it happen.

A Punnett square lets us predict offspring. For a cross between two heterozygous parents (Pp × Pp), each parent makes two kinds of gamete — half carry P, half carry p. Combine them:

The four boxes give a genotype ratio of 1 PP : 2 Pp : 1 pp. Because PP and Pp both look purple, the phenotype ratio is 3 purple : 1 white — exactly the pattern Mendel counted in his garden. The lone white plant is the only one to inherit two recessive alleles. This is the engine of Mendelian genetics: separate the alleles, recombine them, and the ratios follow.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Mendel found the grammar of inheritance — how traits are passed on faithfully, copied and recombined but not blended away. It is the same theme this whole module turns on: responsibility in faithful copying, and what we inherit and pass on. He was right long before he could be complete; the molecular story of DNA you began this module is what finally explained why his rules hold. This week, learn to think in his ratios — they are the foundation of everything that follows.
