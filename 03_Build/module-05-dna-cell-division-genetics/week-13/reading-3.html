Reading 13.03
Beyond Dominant and Recessive
Anchor Reading · Incomplete Dominance, Codominance, Multiple Alleles, and Sex-Linkage
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Mendel chose his peas well: each trait had two clean alleles, one fully dominant over the other. But nature is not always so tidy. Some heterozygotes look like a blend; some show both alleles at once; some genes have more than two alleles; and some genes ride on the chromosome that determines sex. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 13.03. By the end you should be able to distinguish incomplete dominance, codominance, multiple alleles (the ABO blood-type system), and sex-linkage — and explain why some traits show up far more often in males.

Incomplete dominance: a true blend

In some genes, neither allele fully masks the other, and the heterozygote shows an intermediate phenotype — a real blend. Cross a true-breeding red snapdragon with a true-breeding white one and the offspring are pink. This is incomplete dominance. It does not mean Mendel was wrong — the alleles are still passed on whole and unchanged. Breed two pink snapdragons and you get red, pink, and white in a 1:2:1 ratio, and red and white reappear cleanly. The factors did not mix; only their visible effect blends in the heterozygote.

Codominance: both at full strength

Codominance is different from incomplete dominance. Here the heterozygote shows both alleles fully and separately — not a blend but both at once. In roan cattle, a cross between a red animal and a white one produces a coat with red hairs and white hairs side by side, not pink. The classic human example is one part of the ABO blood-type system, which we turn to next: a person of blood type AB makes both the A marker and the B marker on their red blood cells, each at full strength.

Multiple alleles: the ABO blood types

Mendel’s peas had two alleles per gene, but a gene can have more than two versions in a population — though any one person still carries only two. Human ABO blood type is the textbook case. There are three alleles: IA, IB, and i. The first two are codominant with each other, and both are dominant over i (which is recessive).

Genotype(s) Blood type (phenotype)
IAIA or IAiType A
IBIB or IBiType B
IAIBType AB (both markers — codominance)
iiType O (neither marker)

Type O appears only when a person inherits two recessive i alleles. Notice that a single gene, with three alleles and a mix of codominance and recessiveness, produces four phenotypes — far richer than a simple dominant/recessive pair.

Sex-linkage: genes on the X chromosome

In humans, sex is set by two chromosomes: females are XX and males are XY. The X chromosome carries many genes that have nothing to do with sex at all — genes for color vision, for blood clotting, and more. A gene on the X is called X-linked, and X-linked traits follow a lopsided pattern between the sexes.

Here is why. A female has two X chromosomes, so a recessive allele on one X can be masked by a normal allele on the other. A male has only one X (his other sex chromosome is the much smaller Y), so whatever allele is on his single X is expressed — there is no second copy to mask it. Biologists say males are hemizygous for X-linked genes. The result: X-linked recessive conditions such as red-green color blindness and hemophilia appear far more often in males than in females.

Worked example. A carrier mother (XCXc, normal vision but carrying one color-blindness allele) has children with a father of normal vision (XCY). Every son gets his single X from his mother. Half her X chromosomes carry the c allele, so half of the sons will be color blind — while none of the daughters will be, because each daughter also receives a normal XC from her father to mask it.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

These patterns do not overturn Mendel — they extend him. The alleles are still copied faithfully and passed on whole; what varies is how they are expressed together. Knowing them lets you read real human inheritance: why a blood type can rule a parent in or out, why a daughter can be a carrier without ever showing a trait, why a condition skips from grandfather to grandson. Next lesson we will use exactly these ideas to read a family’s history in a pedigree.
