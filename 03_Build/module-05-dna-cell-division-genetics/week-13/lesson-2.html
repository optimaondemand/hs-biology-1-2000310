Does the gene for seed shape care what the gene for seed color is doing?
Mendel asked exactly that, and the answer launched modern genetics. Today you will learn the Law of Independent Assortment, set up a dihybrid cross and read its 9:3:3:1 ratio, and use the multiplication rule to predict offspring without drawing all sixteen boxes.

🌱 PART 1 · GENES THAT IGNORE EACH OTHER

Four kinds of gamete

The Law of Independent Assortment says the alleles of one gene separate into gametes independently of another gene’s alleles. So a plant that is RrYy (heterozygous for both round/wrinkled and yellow/green) makes four kinds of gamete in equal numbers: RY, Ry, rY, ry. Cross two such plants and you combine four gamete types against four — a sixteen-box square. When you sort the sixteen offspring by appearance, you get the dihybrid fingerprint:

9 both dominant · 3 dominant/recessive · 3 recessive/dominant · 1 both recessive  →  9:3:3:1
🧮 PART 2 · THE SHORTCUT: MULTIPLY

You do not have to draw sixteen boxes. Because the genes are independent, treat each as its own monohybrid cross and multiply. For RrYy × RrYy: shape gives 3/4 round, 1/4 wrinkled; color gives 3/4 yellow, 1/4 green. So the chance of round and yellow is 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16, and wrinkled and green is 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16. The whole ratio falls out of arithmetic.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

Independent assortment is why every gamete is a fresh shuffle — and why siblings differ. That shuffling is the raw variation evolution will work on in Module 6. With the 9:3:3:1 ratio and the multiplication rule, you can now predict inheritance for any number of independent genes.
