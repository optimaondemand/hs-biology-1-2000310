Genotype versus phenotype

A gene is an instruction for a trait; an allele is one version of that gene. We write a dominant allele with a capital letter (P) and the recessive one lowercase (p). The pair of alleles an organism carries is its genotype (PP, Pp, or pp); what you can observe is its phenotype (purple or white). Two same alleles — PP or pp — is homozygous; two different — Pp — is heterozygous. Keep one warning from the reading front of mind: dominant does not mean more common. It means “expressed when present.”

🧬 PART 2 · SEGREGATION AND THE 3:1 RATIO

Alleles separate; then they recombine

The Law of Segregation says an organism’s two alleles separate when gametes form, so each gamete carries just one. This is exactly what happens when homologous chromosomes are pulled apart in meiosis (Week 11) — Mendel deduced the rule decades before anyone could watch it. A monohybrid cross of two heterozygotes, Pp × Pp, fills four boxes:

Pp
PPPPp
pPppp

The genotype ratio is 1 PP : 2 Pp : 1 pp; because PP and Pp both look purple, the phenotype ratio is 3 purple : 1 white. Test your reading of the square below.

⚡ SELF-CHECK · TAP TO REVEAL
In a Pp × Pp cross, what fraction of the offspring are expected to show the recessive phenotype?
(Tap to reveal the answer.)
1 out of 4 (one quarter). Only the single pp box shows the recessive trait; the other three boxes (PP, Pp, Pp) all show the dominant trait. That is the 3:1 ratio — three dominant to one recessive.
📚 PART 3 · THE MAN WHO COUNTED PEAS

Mendel’s genius was not a microscope — it was patience and arithmetic. He grew tens of thousands of plants and counted, and the counting revealed ratios that no one had noticed before. His example is this module’s theme in miniature: the careful, faithful work of recording what is actually there.

🏛️ A HUMAN STORY · IGNORED FOR THIRTY-FIVE YEARS
Gregor Mendel was an Augustinian friar at St. Thomas’s Abbey in Brno. He published his pea results in 1866 in a paper titled “Experiments on Plant Hybridization.” And then… almost nothing. The scientific world of his day was not ready to think about inheritance in numbers and ratios, and his work was largely overlooked for about thirty-five years. Mendel died in 1884 without seeing his ideas accepted. Then, around 1900, three botanists working separately — Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns, and Erich von Tschermak — rediscovered the same patterns and found Mendel had gotten there first. He had reasoned out the rules of heredity with no knowledge of DNA, chromosomes, or meiosis — right long before the science could explain why. There is a quiet lesson in it about doing honest, careful work even when no one is yet listening.
