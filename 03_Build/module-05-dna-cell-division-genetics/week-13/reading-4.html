Reading a Family’s History
Anchor Reading · Pedigrees, Karyotypes, and Chromosomal Disorders
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
We cannot run controlled crosses on people. So how do geneticists trace a trait through a human family? They read the family’s history as a diagram — a pedigree — and they read an individual’s chromosomes as a karyotype. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 13.04. By the end you should be able to read a pedigree to infer whether a trait is dominant or recessive, explain how nondisjunction in meiosis changes chromosome number, and connect that error to Down, Turner, and Klinefelter conditions.

The pedigree: a family tree with rules

A pedigree is a standardized family tree that tracks a single trait across generations. Its symbols are fixed so any geneticist can read it:

■ A square = a male · ● a circle = a female
A filled-in symbol = the individual is affected (shows the trait); an open symbol = unaffected
A horizontal line joining two symbols = a mating; a vertical line dropping down = their offspring

From the pattern of filled and open symbols you can reason out how a trait is inherited. A few clues: if two unaffected parents have an affected child, the trait must be recessive (the parents were both carriers, hiding a recessive allele). If an affected child has at least one affected parent in every generation, the trait is likely dominant. And if a trait shows up mostly in males and seems to pass from a carrier mother to her sons, suspect X-linked inheritance — the pattern you met in Lesson 13.03.

A pedigree is detective work: you cannot choose the crosses, so you reason backward from the evidence a family already gives you — the same claim-evidence-reasoning habit you practice in every CER.

When chromosomes fail to separate

Mendel’s rules assume each gamete gets exactly one copy of each chromosome. Usually meiosis (Week 11) delivers this faithfully. But sometimes a pair fails to split: homologous chromosomes, or sister chromatids, do not separate properly. This error is called nondisjunction. When it happens, one gamete ends up with an extra chromosome and another with one missing. If such a gamete is fertilized, the resulting individual has the wrong number of chromosomes in every cell.

This is the module’s theme of faithful copying seen from the other side: when copying fails, the consequences ripple through the whole organism. Because each human chromosome carries hundreds of genes, having one too many or one too few has wide effects.

The karyotype and three chromosomal conditions

A karyotype is an organized photograph of all of an individual’s chromosomes, lined up in matched pairs by size. Because it shows chromosome number directly, it is the tool used to detect the results of nondisjunction.

Condition Chromosome change
Down syndrome (trisomy 21)Three copies of chromosome 21 instead of two
Turner syndromeA single X chromosome (XO) — one sex chromosome missing (monosomy)
Klinefelter syndromeAn extra X chromosome in a male (XXY)

All three trace back to the same root cause: a nondisjunction event in the formation of an egg or sperm. The karyotype makes the cause visible — a triple where there should be a pair, or a single where there should be two.

🏛️ A HUMAN STORY · NETTIE STEVENS AND THE CHROMOSOMES OF SEX
In 1905, the American biologist Nettie Stevens studied the cells of mealworm beetles and noticed something decisive: females carried two large matching chromosomes, while males carried one large and one small. She concluded that sex is determined by chromosomes — what we now call the X and Y. It was a foundational discovery, made independently around the same time by E. B. Wilson, yet much of the credit in her lifetime went to others, and her name faded from the textbooks for decades. Around the same period, Thomas Hunt Morgan and his students, working with fruit flies, showed that specific genes are physically carried on chromosomes — tying Mendel’s abstract “factors” to real, visible structures. Stevens’s story echoes Mendel’s ignored decades and Rosalind Franklin’s contested credit from earlier this module: science is a human enterprise, and giving honest credit is part of doing it well.
