Reading the Rocks: The Fossil Record
Lesson · Fossils, Deep Time, and Descent with Modification
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How can rock and bone, dug out of the ground, become evidence that life has changed over millions of years?
This module asks a big question: is life today the same as it has always been, or has it changed — and if it changed, how do we know? This week we gather the evidence. Today we open the first and oldest archive: the fossil record. You will learn how fossils form, why deeper rock layers hold older life (stratigraphy), how index fossils date the layers, and what transitional fossils like Tiktaalik reveal about descent with modification.

🪔 PART 1 · A LIBRARY MADE OF STONE

How a fossil forms

A fossil is a preserved trace of past life — a bone, shell, leaf print, or footprint. Most form when an organism is quickly buried in sediment before it can decay; over long ages, more sediment piles on, the lower layers harden into rock, and minerals slowly replace the buried remains, turning bone to stone. The word itself comes from the Latin fossus, “dug up.” Because preservation is rare, the record is incomplete — but what it holds is real.

📊 PART 2 · READING THE LAYERS

Stratigraphy and the law of superposition

Sediment settles from the top down, so layers stack in time order. The study of these layers is stratigraphy, and its key rule is the law of superposition: in undisturbed rock, deeper layers are older and shallower layers are younger. Some species make especially good clocks: an index fossil comes from a species that was widespread (found in many places) but short-lived (existing only briefly), so finding it pins a layer to a narrow age — like a postmark stamping a date on the rock.

Deeper = older  ·  Shallower = younger  ·  Index fossil = a clock in the rock
Read bottom to top, the layers are a timeline of life.

The trilobite lived earlier.
By the law of superposition, the deeper (bottom) layer is older, so the fossil in it lived first. The fish in the top layer is younger.
🦎 PART 3 · FORMS IN BETWEEN

What a transitional fossil shows

The most striking fossils carry a mix of features from an older group and a newer one. Tiktaalik (about 375 million years old) sits between fish and four-legged land animals: fish scales and gills, but also a flat head, a neck, and sturdy fin bones with wrist-like joints. Archaeopteryx blends dinosaur features (teeth, a long bony tail, clawed fingers) with bird features (feathers and wings). A series of whale ancestors, including Ambulocetus, the “walking whale,” records mammals returning to the sea. These transitional fossils are exactly what we expect if living things descend, with modification, from earlier and different ancestors — the meaning of descent with modification.

A transitional fossil does not show one living species turning into another we see today. It shows an ancient form bridging two major groups — the gaps between kinds of life are not fixed, but the product of slow change over deep time.
