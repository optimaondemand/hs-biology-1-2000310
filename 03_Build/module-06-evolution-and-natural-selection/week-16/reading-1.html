Reading 16.01
Reading the Rocks: The Fossil Record
Anchor Reading · Fossils, Deep Time, and Descent with Modification
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Stand at the edge of a deep canyon and you are reading a book. The bands of rock in the wall are its pages, the lowest band written first and the highest written last. Buried in those pages are the bones, shells, and imprints of creatures that lived and died across millions of years. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 16.01. By the end you should be able to explain how fossils form, why deeper layers are older, and what an index fossil and a transitional fossil each tell us about how life has changed over time.

What a fossil is, and how one forms

A fossil is any preserved trace of past life — a bone, a shell, a leaf print, a footprint, a burrow. Most form the same patient way. An organism dies and is quickly buried in sediment — mud, sand, ash — before scavengers and decay can erase it. Over long stretches of time, layer after layer of sediment piles on top, pressing the lower layers into rock. Minerals seep into the buried remains and slowly replace them, turning bone to stone. Far more often than not, nothing is preserved at all; fossilization is the rare exception, which is why the fossil record is a library with many missing pages — but a real library nonetheless.

The word fossil comes from the Latin fossus, “dug up.” That is exactly what it is: something dug out of the ground that once was alive.

A layered history: stratigraphy

Because sediment settles from the top down, layers stack in time order. The study of these layers is called stratigraphy (from stratum, “a layer”). It rests on a simple, powerful idea called the law of superposition: in undisturbed rock, deeper layers are older and layers nearer the surface are younger. Picture a stack of newspapers thrown on the floor over a week — the one on the bottom is Monday’s, the one on top is today’s. Rock layers work the same way.

Deeper layer = older  ·  Shallower layer = younger
So the fossils in a layer are roughly as old as the layer itself — and the record reads, bottom to top, like a timeline.

Index fossils: clocks in the rock

Some species are especially useful for dating layers. An index fossil comes from a species that was geographically widespread (so it shows up in many places) but existed only for a short slice of time (so it pins a layer to a narrow age). Find that species in a rock layer anywhere in the world, and you know that layer is from the same time. Trilobites and certain ammonites are classic examples. An index fossil is a little like a postmark: it stamps a layer with a date.

Notice the logic: a fossil is a good clock precisely because the species changed — it appeared, spread, and then was replaced. A world of unchanging life would have no index fossils at all.

Transitional fossils: forms in between

The most striking fossils of all are transitional fossils — forms that carry a mix of features from an older group and a newer one, capturing a major change partway through. They are exactly what we expect to find if living things descend, with modification, from earlier and different ancestors. Three well-studied examples:

  • Tiktaalik (about 375 million years old) sits between fish and the first four-legged land animals. It had fish-like scales and gills, yet also a flat, crocodile-like head, a neck, and sturdy fin bones with wrist-like joints — a creature poised between water and land.
  • Archaeopteryx blends features of small meat-eating dinosaurs (teeth, a long bony tail, clawed fingers) with features of birds (feathers and wings) — a snapshot near the origin of birds.
  • A series of whale ancestors, including Ambulocetus (“walking whale”), records the return of mammals to the sea: animals with legs that could still walk on land but were built increasingly for swimming.

A transitional fossil does not show one living species turning into another we see today. It shows an ancient form that bridges two groups — evidence that the boundaries between kinds of life are not fixed, but the product of slow change over deep time.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

The fossil record is the first of several independent lines of evidence you will meet this week. Read bottom to top, it shows that the living things of long ago were different from those today, that whole groups have appeared and vanished, and that some fossils bridge the gaps between major groups. This is the meaning of descent with modification — life changing across deep time. Keep that phrase; the next three readings will add new and independent kinds of evidence that point to the very same conclusion.
