Lesson 16.02
The Same Bones: Comparative Anatomy
Lesson · Homologous, Analogous, and Vestigial Structures
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why are a human arm, a whale flipper, and a bat wing — which do completely different jobs — built from the same set of bones?
Yesterday the rocks gave us our first line of evidence. Today the bones of living animals give us a second. You will learn to tell apart homologous structures (same plan, different jobs), analogous structures (same job, different plan), and vestigial structures (leftovers from an ancestor) — and, crucially, which kind is evidence of common ancestry and which is not.

🦴 PART 1 · ONE PLAN, MANY JOBS

Homologous structures

Homologous structures share the same underlying structure but may do different jobs. The vertebrate forelimb is the classic case: the human arm, whale flipper, bat wing, and cat leg all hold the same bones in the same order — one upper bone, two lower bones, wrist bones, digits — even though they grasp, swim, fly, and walk. Why bend one plan to such different uses? Because all four inherited that plan from a common ancestor, and it was then modified for different ways of life. The same bones, reshaped — descent with modification, written in the skeleton.

🐛 PART 2 · SAME JOB, DIFFERENT PLAN

Analogous structures — and a trap to avoid

Analogous structures do the same job but are built on different structures. A bird wing (a modified limb with bones) and an insect wing (a thin, boneless extension of the body wall) both fly, yet share no common building plan. They arise by convergent evolution — unrelated lineages independently hitting on a similar solution to a similar problem. The trap to avoid: doing the same job does NOT prove close relationship. Shared structure is strong evidence of kinship; shared function is not.

Homologous = same structure, different function  ·  Analogous = different structure, same function
Only homologous structures point to a recent common ancestor.

Classify each pair below. Choose carefully — the difference is the whole point of this lesson.

✅ CLASSIFY EACH EXAMPLE
A human arm and a whale flipper — same bones in the same arrangement, different jobs.
A bird’s wing and an insect’s wing — both used for flight, but built completely differently.
The small internal pelvic bones of a whale — remnants of the hip bones of its land-walking ancestors.
🦵 PART 3 · LEFTOVERS AND EMBRYOS

Vestigial structures and shared development

Vestigial structures are reduced, often functionless remnants of structures that were full-sized in an ancestor — the human appendix and tailbone, whale pelvic bones, the non-seeing eyes of blind cave fish. They make sense only as inheritances kept in reduced form. And in their early development, the embryos of fish, chickens, and humans look strikingly alike, sharing features such as a tail before they grow into their distinct adult forms. Both clues read the body as a record of descent: a shared starting plan, modified along separate paths.

Notice the cross-unit thread: the variation that lets one inherited plan be reshaped comes from the mutations and the shuffling of meiosis you studied in Module 5. Inheritance and variation are the raw material; the bones are the record.
