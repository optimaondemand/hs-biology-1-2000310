📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why do remote islands so often hold animals found nowhere else on Earth — yet ones that look like cousins of the nearest mainland’s species?
Our third line of evidence is a map. Biogeography — the study of where living things are found — turns up patterns that make sense only if species descend, with modification, from ancestors that lived nearby. You will explore island species, the Galápagos finches, and Australia’s marsupials, and meet the two naturalists whose courage gave us the idea of evolution.

🌎 PART 1 · A PATTERN ON THE MAP

Geography, not climate alone

If creatures were simply placed wherever they best fit, the same climates around the world should hold the same animals. They do not. The grasslands of Australia, Africa, and South America have similar climates but wildly different mammals — pouched marsupials dominate Australia, placental mammals dominate the others. The pattern makes sense if species descend, with modification, from ancestors that lived nearby: Australia’s marsupials are concentrated there because they arose and diversified on a long-isolated landmass. Geography, not climate alone, shaped who lives where.

🐋 PART 2 · ISLANDS TELL THE CLEAREST STORY

Endemic species and the Galápagos finches

Remote islands are biogeography’s sharpest test. Their species are often endemic — found nowhere else — yet they most resemble the species of the nearest mainland. That is exactly what we expect if a few mainland ancestors reached the island long ago and then changed in isolation. On the Galápagos, more than a dozen finch species, found nowhere else, differ mainly in beak size and shape — some stout for crushing seeds, some slender for probing — yet all resemble a single finch from the nearby South American coast. A few founders arrived; their descendants diversified to use the islands’ different foods.

A newly studied island 600 miles off a coast has lizards found nowhere else on Earth.
Under common descent, which mainland’s lizards should they most resemble — the nearest coast, or a distant continent with the same climate?
(Tap to flip.)
✅ THE ANSWER
The nearest coast.
Island species descend from ancestors that could actually reach the island — the nearby mainland — not from a distant continent merely because the climate matches. Geography and descent, not climate, predict the resemblance.
⚓ PART 3 · THE NATURALISTS WHO SAW IT

Following the evidence into controversy

The patterns of biogeography were among the clues that led two naturalists, working an ocean apart, to the same revolutionary idea. Their story is this module’s virtue made real.

🏛️ A HUMAN STORY · TWO NATURALISTS, ONE IDEA, AND THE COURAGE TO PUBLISH
As a young naturalist aboard the survey ship HMS Beagle, Charles Darwin spent years observing the natural world — including the Galápagos — and slowly built the idea of evolution by natural selection. Yet he delayed publishing for roughly twenty years, gathering more evidence and bracing for the controversy he knew would come. Then, in 1858, a letter arrived from Alfred Russel Wallace, working in the Malay Archipelago, who had independently reached the same idea and mailed Darwin an essay describing it. Rather than race to claim sole credit, the two men’s work was presented jointly at the Linnean Society of London on 1 July 1858, and Darwin published On the Origin of Species in 1859. Here is this module’s virtue, courage — following evidence into long, public controversy — joined to the honesty of sharing credit for a discovery two people reached at once.
