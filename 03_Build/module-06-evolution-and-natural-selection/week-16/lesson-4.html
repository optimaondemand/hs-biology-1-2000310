📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If a gene from a bacterium can be read by a human cell, what does that tell us about how all living things are related?
Our fourth and final line of evidence is the most intimate: the molecules of life themselves. All living things use DNA and nearly the same genetic code, and the more similar two species’ sequences are, the more recently they shared an ancestor. Best of all, this independent line converges with the fossils, the bones, and the map — and this lesson is where you begin building your Module 6 CER.

🧬 PART 1 · ONE LANGUAGE FOR ALL OF LIFE

The shared code

From bacteria to oak trees to whales, all known life stores its instructions in DNA and reads them with nearly the same genetic code — the same codons standing for the same amino acids you met in Module 5. There is no biological necessity that a fish and a fern share a coding scheme; that they do is strong evidence that all life descends from a single common ancestor. This idea reaches straight back through the course: the DNA structure of Module 2, the replication of Module 5, and the variation produced by mutation and meiosis — all of it is the raw material that molecular comparisons now read as a record of descent.

🧱 PART 2 · THE MORE ALIKE, THE CLOSER THE KIN

Reading relationships in sequence

Over time, small mutations accumulate in DNA and in the proteins it codes for. So the more similar two species’ sequences, the more recently they shared a common ancestor. Compare a protein such as cytochrome c or hemoglobin across many species and the similarity lines up beautifully with how related they appear from anatomy and fossils.

More similar sequence → more recent common ancestor
A caution for the exam: humans did not evolve from chimpanzees. We share a common ancestor — which is exactly why our DNA is so similar.
🔗 PART 3 · WHEN INDEPENDENT LINES AGREE

The power of convergence

Molecular evidence is powerful alone, but its real strength is that it was gathered in a completely different way from fossils, anatomy, and biogeography — and yet points to the same family tree. When several independent lines of evidence, each free in principle to disagree, instead converge on one conclusion, that conclusion is hard to dismiss as coincidence. This is why scientists regard common descent as one of the best-supported ideas in all of biology.

🤖 AI AWARENESS · MACHINE LEARNING AND THE TREE OF LIFE
Comparing whole genomes by hand is impossible — the datasets are enormous — so scientists use computational methods, including some machine-learning approaches, to compare millions of sequences and propose evolutionary trees far faster than any person could. That is the opportunity: handling genomic-scale data and suggesting relationships humans might miss. But here is the pitfall: a computed tree is only as good as the data fed in and the assumptions built into the model, and these tools can be confidently wrong. A tree produced by an algorithm is a hypothesis to test against fossils, anatomy, and other data — not an authority to trust on its own. Use the tool; then interrogate its answer, exactly as you would any scientific claim.
CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH · READING DNA FROM THE DEEP PAST
Recent research suggests DNA can sometimes survive in fossils and ancient remains for tens of thousands of years. Scientists are currently recovering and reading ancient DNA — including the genomes of our extinct relatives the Neanderthals and the Denisovans — letting them study relationships directly from the molecules rather than only from the shape of bone. The work is still developing and demands great care to avoid contamination, but it is opening a new window: reading descent not only in living species, but in the ancient dead themselves.
