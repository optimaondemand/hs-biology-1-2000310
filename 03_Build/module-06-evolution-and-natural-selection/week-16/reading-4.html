Reading 16.04
Written in the Molecules
Anchor Reading · Molecular Evidence and Converging Lines
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
In Module 2 you learned that DNA carries information, and in Module 5 you learned the genetic code that turns that information into proteins. Now comes a remarkable fact: every living thing uses the same kind of molecule and very nearly the same code. A gene from a bacterium can be read by the machinery of a human cell. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 16.04. By the end you should be able to explain how molecular evidence reveals relationships among species, and why this independent line of evidence, converging with the others, makes the case for common descent so strong.

One language for all of life

From bacteria to oak trees to whales, all known life stores its instructions in DNA (or, in some viruses, RNA) and reads those instructions with nearly the same genetic code — the same three-letter codons standing for the same amino acids. There is no biological reason a fish and a fern had to share a coding scheme. That they do is powerful evidence that all living things are branches of a single family tree, inheriting one ancient molecular language from a common ancestor.

This idea reaches straight back through the course: the DNA structure from Module 2, the base-pairing and replication from Module 5, the very variation produced by mutation and meiosis — all of it is the raw material that molecular comparisons now read as a record of descent.

The more alike the sequence, the closer the kin

Here is the heart of molecular evidence. Over long stretches of time, small changes (mutations) accumulate in DNA and in the proteins it codes for. So the more similar two species’ DNA or protein sequences are, the more recently they shared a common ancestor; the more different, the longer ago their lineages split. Compare a protein such as cytochrome c (used in cellular respiration) or hemoglobin (which carries oxygen) across many species, and the degree of similarity lines up beautifully with how closely related those species appear from their anatomy and the fossil record.

More similar sequence → more recent common ancestor
The molecules keep a record of descent, written in the order of their building blocks.

When independent lines agree

Molecular evidence is powerful on its own, but its real strength is this: it was gathered in a completely different way from fossils, from comparative anatomy, and from biogeography — and yet it points to the same family tree. When several independent lines of evidence, each able in principle to have disagreed, instead converge on one conclusion, the conclusion is hard to dismiss as coincidence. A whale’s flipper bones (anatomy), its walking ancestors (fossils), its pelvic remnants (anatomy again), and its DNA similarity to other mammals (molecular) all tell one story.

This convergence is the foundation of strong scientific reasoning, and it is the reason scientists regard common descent as one of the best-supported ideas in all of biology.

🤖 AI AWARENESS · MACHINE LEARNING AND THE TREE OF LIFE
Comparing whole genomes by hand is impossible — the datasets are enormous. So scientists now use machine-learning algorithms to compare millions of sequences and propose evolutionary trees far faster than any person could. That is the opportunity: handling genomic-scale data and suggesting relationships humans might never spot. But here is the pitfall a careful student must remember: a computed tree is only as good as the data fed in and the assumptions built into the model, and these tools can be confidently wrong. A tree produced by an algorithm is a hypothesis to test against fossils, anatomy, and other data — not an authority to trust on its own. Use the tool; then interrogate its answer, exactly as you would any scientific claim.
CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH · READING DNA FROM THE DEEP PAST
Recent research suggests that DNA can sometimes survive in fossils and ancient remains for tens of thousands of years. Scientists are currently recovering and reading ancient DNA — including genomes of our extinct relatives the Neanderthals and the Denisovans — which lets them study relationships directly from the molecules rather than only from bone shape. This work is still developing and demands great care to avoid contamination, but it is opening a new window: a way to read descent not just in living species, but in the ancient dead themselves.
✏️ THIS IS YOUR MODULE 6 CER ANCHOR
This week you have met four independent lines of evidence — fossils, comparative anatomy, biogeography, and molecules. Your Module 6 CER writing assignment asks you to choose three of them and build one unified argument for common descent. Begin collecting your evidence now, in your scientific notebook; this reading and Lesson 16.04 are where that argument starts.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Molecular evidence is the fourth independent line — and the one that ties the others together. All life shares one molecular language; the more alike the sequences, the closer the kinship; and this conclusion agrees with the rocks, the bones, and the map. Next week you turn from the evidence that life evolved to the mechanism of how — natural selection — and forward to Module 7, where these same molecular comparisons become the tool for sorting life into the great tree of classification.
