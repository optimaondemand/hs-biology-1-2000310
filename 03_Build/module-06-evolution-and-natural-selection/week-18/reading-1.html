Reading 18.01
How One Species Becomes Two: Speciation
Anchor Reading · Species, Reproductive Isolation, and Two Roads to a New Species
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
For two weeks you have studied how populations change. Now comes the deeper event: how one species splits into two. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 18.01. By the end you should be able to define a species, explain why reproductive isolation is the key to making a new one, and tell apart the two roads to a split — allopatric (by geographic separation) and sympatric (without it).

What is a species?

We talk easily about “different kinds” of animals and plants, but biology needs a sharper definition. The most widely used is the biological species concept: a species is a group of populations whose members can interbreed in nature and produce fertile offspring. The crucial word is fertile. A horse and a donkey can mate and produce a mule — but the mule is sterile, so it cannot carry the two gene pools forward. By the biological species concept, horses and donkeys are therefore two separate species, not one.

What holds a species together as one unit is gene flow — the steady movement of genes through the population as individuals mate across it, generation after generation. As long as gene flow continues, the whole population keeps sharing one common pool of genetic variation (the variation you traced to mutation and meiosis back in Module 5). That shared pool is what keeps a species a single, recognizable kind.

Speciation begins when gene flow stops

Speciation is the formation of a new species. It cannot happen while two populations are freely exchanging genes, because gene flow keeps mixing them back into one. Speciation begins only when the populations become reproductively isolated — when something reliably prevents them from interbreeding successfully, so gene flow between them stops.

Once the gene pools are cut off, the two populations are on separate paths. The natural selection and genetic drift you studied in Week 17 now act on each population independently. Different mutations arise in each; different pressures shape each; chance nudges each its own way. Over many generations the two populations diverge — they grow steadily more different — until eventually they could not interbreed even if brought back together. At that point, one species has become two.

Biologists group the barriers that block interbreeding into two simple kinds. Prezygotic barriers act before a fertilized egg can form — the two populations breed in different seasons, prefer different mates, or live where they never meet. Postzygotic barriers act after — a hybrid forms but is weak or sterile, like the mule. You only need the big idea: anything that dependably prevents fertile offspring keeps the gene pools apart and lets divergence proceed.

The first road: allopatric speciation

Allopatric speciation (from the Greek for “other homeland”) happens when a geographic barrier physically divides a population. A mountain range rises, a river carves a new course, rising seas isolate an island, a widening canyon splits a forest. The separated groups can no longer reach one another, gene flow stops, and each group diverges on its own.

Two classic examples make the pattern concrete. On the opposite rims of a deep canyon, squirrel populations cut off from each other have diverged into distinct forms. And on separate islands, finch populations isolated from the mainland and from one another have developed into different species, each suited to its island. In every case the story is the same: a barrier divides, gene flow ends, and the separated populations drift apart into new species.

The second road: sympatric speciation

Sympatric speciation (from the Greek for “same homeland”) is the harder, more surprising road: a new species arises without any geographic separation, while the populations still share the same place. How can gene flow stop if nothing physically divides the groups?

In plants, the most common route is polyploidy. An error during cell division leaves an offspring with a doubled set of chromosomes. Because of the chromosome mismatch, this polyploid plant can no longer breed with the original population — yet it can often reproduce with itself or with other polyploids. In a single generation it is reproductively isolated from its parents, even though it grows right beside them. Sympatric speciation can also begin when groups specialize on different niches — for instance, feeding or breeding on different host plants — so that they rarely encounter and mate with one another. Either way, isolation, not distance, is what does the work.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

A species is held together by gene flow; speciation is what happens when that flow is cut. Whether the cut comes from a geographic barrier (allopatric) or from isolation in the same place such as polyploidy (sympatric), the result is the same: separated populations diverge under selection and drift until they can no longer interbreed. This is the branching that has filled the world with millions of species — and it is the engine behind the whole tree of life you will keep meeting from here to the end of the course.
