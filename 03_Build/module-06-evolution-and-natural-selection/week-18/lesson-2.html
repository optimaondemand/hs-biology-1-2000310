When Nothing Changes: Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium
Lesson · The Null Model of Evolution
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How can you prove a population is evolving? Strangely, you start by describing a population that is not.
Today you meet a powerful thinking tool: Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. It describes an imaginary population in which allele frequencies never change — the snapshot of a population standing perfectly still. Real populations never quite meet its five conditions, and that is exactly the point: by showing what no evolution would look like, Hardy–Weinberg gives us a baseline to measure real change against. When a population departs from it, we know evolution is at work.

🧬 PART 1 · A POPULATION HOLDING PERFECTLY STILL

The idea of a null model

Recall from Week 17 that evolution is a change in allele frequencies in a population over time — a shift in how common each version of a gene is. In 1908 two thinkers, working independently, asked a clever question: what would have to be true for those frequencies to never change at all? The answer is the Hardy–Weinberg principle. It says that allele frequencies will stay constant from one generation to the next — the population is in equilibrium — as long as five conditions all hold.

This is what scientists call a null model: a description of “nothing happening,” written down on purpose so we have something to compare reality against. Because real populations almost never meet all five conditions, Hardy–Weinberg rarely describes the real world — and that is its power. When a real population departs from equilibrium, that departure is the signature of evolution. The model tells us what the absence of evolution would look like, so we can recognize its presence.

🧱 PART 2 · THE FIVE CONDITIONS

Break any one, and the population evolves

For allele frequencies to stay frozen, all five must be true at once:

1. No mutation — no new alleles are created.
2. No gene flow — no individuals migrate in or out.
3. No natural selection — every genotype survives and reproduces equally.
4. Random mating — mates are chosen without regard to genotype.
5. Very large population — so chance (genetic drift) has no effect.

Notice that conditions 1, 2, 3, and 5 are simply the four mechanisms of evolution you met last week (mutation, gene flow, selection, and drift) turned off — plus random mating. Break any single one, and the frequencies shift: the population is evolving.

For each scenario, name the broken condition. Tap an answer for instant feedback.

1. Dark moths survive better than light moths on soot-darkened bark, so they leave more offspring each generation.

3. A rare error during DNA copying creates a brand-new allele that did not exist in the population before.
📊 PART 3 · THE EQUATIONS, KEPT SIMPLE

p, q, and the genotype frequencies

Imagine a gene with just two alleles. We call the frequency of one allele p and the other q. Because every allele in the pool is one or the other, the two must add to the whole:

p + q = 1   (allele frequencies)
p² + 2pq + q² = 1   (genotype frequencies)
p² = homozygous dominant · 2pq = heterozygous (carriers) · q² = homozygous recessive

A worked example. Suppose a recessive trait shows up in 1 of every 100 individuals, so q² = 0.01. Then q = √0.01 = 0.1, and p = 1 − 0.1 = 0.9. The carrier frequency (heterozygotes) is 2pq = 2 × 0.9 × 0.1 = 0.18 — about 18 of every 100 individuals carry the allele without showing the trait. From one observed number, the model lets you estimate the hidden rest.

Flip the card to test the worked example yourself.

🔄 FLIP CARD · TAP TO REVEAL
In a population, q² = 0.04 for a recessive trait.
What is q? What is p? What fraction are carriers (2pq)?
(Work it out, then tap to flip.)
✅ THE ANSWER
q = √0.04 = 0.2; p = 1 − 0.2 = 0.8; carriers 2pq = 2 × 0.8 × 0.2 = 0.32.
So about 32% of the population carries one copy of the allele without showing the trait. Take the square root of q² to get q, subtract from 1 to get p, then 2pq gives the carriers.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium is the careful description of a population that is not evolving — held still by five conditions that real life almost never satisfies. Its value is as a measuring stick: when a real population departs from the equilibrium the equations predict, we have evidence that one of the evolutionary mechanisms is at work. To detect change, first describe stillness honestly. That is good science.
