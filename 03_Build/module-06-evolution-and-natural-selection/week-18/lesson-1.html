Lesson 18.01
How One Species Becomes Two: Speciation
Lesson · Species, Reproductive Isolation, and the Two Roads to a New Species
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If natural selection and drift change a population over time, how does one species actually split into two?
For two weeks you have watched populations change. This week you will watch them split. Today you will define what a species is, learn that new species form when populations become reproductively isolated so they can no longer interbreed, and tell apart the two roads to that split: allopatric speciation (a geographic barrier divides a population) and sympatric speciation (a new species arises without any geographic separation).

2

3

🧬 PART 1 · WHAT IS A SPECIES?
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A horse and a donkey can mate and produce a mule — but the mule is almost always sterile and cannot have offspring of its own. So: are horses and donkeys the same species, or two different ones? (No wrong answer — just check in.)

The biological species concept

A species is a group of populations whose members can interbreed in nature and produce fertile offspring. The key word is fertile: what makes a species a single unit is that genes can keep flowing through it, generation after generation. As long as that flow — called gene flow — continues, a population stays one species, sharing one common pool of genetic variation (the variation you traced back to mutation and meiosis in Module 5).

🧱 PART 2 · THE KEY THAT MUST TURN: REPRODUCTIVE ISOLATION

Stop the gene flow, and the populations drift apart

Speciation is the formation of a new species. It happens only when two populations become reproductively isolated — something stops them from successfully interbreeding, so gene flow between them ends. Once the gene pools are cut off, the two populations no longer share their changes. Natural selection and genetic drift (Week 17) then act on each one separately, and over many generations they diverge until they can no longer interbreed even if reunited. That is the moment one species has become two.

Biologists sort the barriers into two simple groups. Prezygotic barriers act before a fertilized egg forms — the populations breed at different times, prefer different mates, or simply never meet. Postzygotic barriers act after — a hybrid forms but is weak or sterile, like the mule. You only need the big picture: anything that reliably blocks fertile offspring keeps the gene pools apart.

Gene flow stops  →  populations diverge under selection & drift  →  reproductive isolation  →  two species
Bridge to Week 17: speciation is just selection and drift acting on populations that can no longer trade genes.
🔗 PART 3 · TWO ROADS TO A NEW SPECIES

Allopatric vs. sympatric speciation

Allopatric speciation (from Greek allo-, “other,” and patria, “homeland”) happens when a geographic barrier splits a population — a mountain range rises, a river changes course, the sea cuts off an island, a canyon divides a forest. The separated groups can no longer meet, gene flow stops, and they diverge. The squirrels on the opposite rims of the Grand Canyon and the finches isolated on separate islands are classic examples.

Sympatric speciation (sym-, “together”) is the harder one: a new species arises without any geographic separation, while the populations still live in the same place. In plants this commonly happens through polyploidy — an error in cell division doubles the chromosome number, and the polyploid offspring can no longer breed with the original plants, isolating it in a single generation. It can also happen when groups specialize on different niches (for example, feeding on different host plants) so they rarely interbreed.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

A species is held together by gene flow. Cut that flow — by a geographic barrier (allopatric) or by isolation in the same place such as polyploidy (sympatric) — and the separated populations diverge under the very selection and drift you studied last week, until they can no longer interbreed. That is how one species becomes two, and it is the engine behind the whole branching tree of life you will keep meeting from here on.
