🧬 PART 1 · A BUSH, NOT A LADDER

The hominid family tree

The famous “march of progress” image — a crouching ape straightening into a modern human — is misleading. Human evolution was not a single ladder climbing toward us. It was a branching bush: many related species, several living at the same time, most of them now extinct. We are one surviving twig, not the top rung.

A few well-known species, in rough order, mark the journey:

Australopithecus afarensis — “Lucy,” about 3.2 million years ago; walked upright (bipedal) but had a small, ape-sized brain.
Homo habilis — among the earliest stone-tool users.
Homo erectus — larger brain, use of fire, among the earliest to migrate out of Africa.
Homo neanderthalensis — the Neanderthals, a close relative of our own species.
Homo sapiens — us, arising about 300,000 years ago.

Across this bush, three trends stand out: walking upright on two legs (bipedalism) came early; brain size generally increased over time; and tool use grew more sophisticated. These are trends in a branching history — not steps on a ladder aimed at producing us.

🧰 PART 2 · THE MISCONCEPTION, CORRECTED

We did not evolve from chimpanzees

Here is the single most common error in this whole subject: the idea that humans evolved from chimpanzees. We did not. Chimpanzees are not our ancestors — they are our cousins. Humans and chimpanzees both descend from a common ancestor that lived roughly 6 to 7 million years ago. From that ancestor, the tree branched: one lineage led, over millions of years, to modern chimpanzees; another led to us. Both branches have been evolving the whole time. A chimpanzee is a fully modern, separately evolved species — not a “halfway” human and not a leftover earlier stage.

common ancestor (~6–7 mya)  →  branches into  →  lineage to chimpanzees  &  lineage to humans
Cousins share an ancestor; they do not descend one from the other.

🔗 PART 3 · CLOSING THE MODULE: ONE ARGUMENT

Evidence, mechanism, and speciation come together

This lesson closes Module 6, so step back and see the whole shape of it. In Week 16 you weighed four independent lines of evidence — fossils, anatomy (homologous structures), biogeography, and molecular sequences — that all point to shared ancestry. In Week 17 you learned the mechanisms that drive change: variation, natural selection, and genetic drift. This week you saw how those mechanisms, acting on isolated populations, cause speciation — the branching that fills the tree of life. Put together, they make one coherent case: life shares common descent, the same tree on which our own branch sits beside the chimpanzee’s.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

Our own story is a branch on the bush, not the top of a ladder. We did not descend from chimpanzees; we share a common ancestor with them, and both branches have evolved ever since. With that, Module 6 closes: evidence, mechanism, and speciation join into a single argument for common descent — the case Darwin had the courage to follow into twenty years of controversy.
