📖 WHAT THIS BUILDS ON
How One Species Becomes Two
Reading 18.01 and Lesson 18.01
Before you begin: finish Reading 18.01 and work through Lesson 18.01, including the biological species concept, reproductive isolation, and the difference between allopatric and sympatric speciation.
Lesson focus: why reproductive isolation is required for one species to become two
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
This assignment has two parts: classify four scenarios as allopatric or sympatric speciation with a brief reason for each, and explain in a few sentences why reproductive isolation is required for speciation to happen. It should take about 15–20 minutes.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
If two populations keep exchanging genes, can they ever become separate species — and what has to change for them to split?
📝 STUDENT DIRECTIONS
1
Part A — Classify the scenarios (8 pts, 2 each). For each scenario, write allopatric or sympatric and give a one-sentence reason naming what stopped gene flow.
Scenario Allopatric / Sympatric + reason
1. A new lake forms and divides one fish population into two that can never reach each other again. 
2. In one field, a chromosome-doubling (polyploidy) event makes plants that cannot breed with the originals beside them. 
3. A mountain range rises and separates a lizard population into an eastern and a western group. 
4. In the same forest, two insect groups begin feeding on different host plants and stop interbreeding. 
2
Part B — Why isolation is required (2 pts). In two or three sentences, explain why reproductive isolation (the end of gene flow) is necessary for one species to become two. Use the word diverge.
✅ SUCCESS CRITERIA
☐
Part A classifies correctly: 1 = allopatric, 2 = sympatric, 3 = allopatric, 4 = sympatric, each with a reason naming what stopped gene flow.
☐
Part B explains that without isolation, gene flow keeps populations mixed, so they cannot diverge into separate species.
