Reading 18.04
Our Own Branch: Human Evolution
Anchor Reading · The Hominid Bush, Common Ancestry, and the Module Synthesis
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Now the same principles you have studied all module turn toward our own species. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 18.04. By the end you should be able to picture the human lineage as a branching bush, name a few key fossil species and the major trends across them, correct the famous “humans came from chimps” misconception using the idea of a common ancestor, and see how the whole module forms one argument for common descent.

A branching bush, not a ladder

You have probably seen the “march of progress” image: a stooped ape gradually standing up into a modern human, as if evolution were a single staircase aimed at us. It is one of the most misleading pictures in all of science. Human evolution was not a straight climb. It was a branching bush — many related species, several of them alive at the same time, the great majority now extinct. We are one surviving twig on that bush, not the top of a ladder.

A handful of well-studied species mark the journey. Roughly 3.2 million years ago lived Australopithecus afarensis — the species of the famous fossil “Lucy.” She walked upright on two legs, yet her brain was small, about the size of a chimpanzee’s. Later came Homo habilis, among the earliest makers and users of stone tools. Homo erectus followed, with a larger brain, the use of fire, and the distinction of being the first to migrate out of Africa. Homo neanderthalensis — the Neanderthals — were close relatives of our own line. And Homo sapiens, our species, arose about 300,000 years ago.

Across this bush, three broad trends stand out. Walking upright — bipedalism — appeared early, even before large brains. Brain size generally increased over time. And tool use grew steadily more sophisticated. These are trends within a branching history, not rungs on a ladder built to produce us.

We did not evolve from chimpanzees

Now to biology’s single most common error. People often say that humans evolved from chimpanzees. We did not. Chimpanzees are not our ancestors — they are our cousins. Humans and chimpanzees both descend from a common ancestor that lived roughly 6 to 7 million years ago. From that shared ancestor the family tree branched in two directions: one branch led, over millions of years, to today’s chimpanzees; the other led to us. Both branches have been evolving the entire time.

This means a modern chimpanzee is not a “halfway” human or a leftover earlier stage of ourselves. It is a fully modern, separately evolved species, just as we are. The relationship is like that of two human cousins: neither is the parent of the other; both share a grandparent. To describe how humans and apes are related, the accurate language is always “shared common ancestor,” never “descended from.”

Closing the module: one argument

This reading closes Module 6, so it is worth seeing the whole shape of what you have built. In Week 16 you weighed four independent lines of evidence for evolution — the fossil record, anatomy (homologous structures), embryology, and molecular sequences — that all converge on shared ancestry. In Week 17 you studied the mechanisms of change: heritable variation, natural selection, and genetic drift. This week you saw how those mechanisms, acting on populations that become reproductively isolated, produce speciation — the branching that fills the tree of life.

Set side by side, the three weeks make a single coherent case. There is abundant evidence of shared ancestry; there are well-understood mechanisms that change populations; and there is a process, speciation, that turns one lineage into many. Together they support common descent — the great branching tree on which our own twig sits beside the chimpanzee’s, both descended from that ancestor of 6–7 million years ago. This is the understanding your Module 6 CER essay asks you to argue in writing.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Our own story is one branch on a vast bush, not the summit of a ladder. We did not descend from chimpanzees; we share a common ancestor with them, and both lines have evolved ever since. With that, the module’s argument is complete: evidence, mechanism, and speciation join into one case for common descent — the case Darwin had the courage to follow into twenty years of controversy. Hold on to this branching tree. In Module 7 you will learn how scientists name and organize every twig on it, sorting the whole household of life.
