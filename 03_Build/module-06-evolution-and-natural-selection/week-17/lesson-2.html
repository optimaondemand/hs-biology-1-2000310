🧬 PART 1 · THREE SHAPES, ONE ENGINE

Directional, stabilizing, disruptive

Directional selection favors one extreme, so the population shifts that way (the peppered moth; a drought favoring bigger, stronger beaks). Stabilizing selection favors the intermediate and trims both extremes, holding a trait steady (human birth weight). Disruptive selection favors both extremes over the middle, which can split a population into two specialized forms (small-beak and large-beak birds where only small and large seeds are common).

Directional = the population moves toward one end.  Stabilizing = the middle wins, the average holds.  Disruptive = both ends win, the middle loses.
🧱 PART 2 · THE PEPPERED MOTH (DIRECTIONAL SELECTION)

Industrial melanism in Britain

The peppered moth (Biston betularia) comes in light and dark forms. On pale, lichen-covered bark, light moths were camouflaged and dark moths were eaten by birds. As coal soot darkened the bark across industrial Britain in the 1800s, the dark form became the hidden one and rose in frequency — industrial melanism. After clean-air laws cut the soot in the 1900s, bark lightened and light moths rebounded. The population tracked its environment in one direction, then back: textbook directional selection.

🔗 PART 3 · THE HUMAN HAND & CLASSIFYING SCENARIOS

Artificial selection: same engine, human driver

In artificial selection, people choose which individuals breed. Wolves became every dog breed; wild teosinte became corn; cattle were bred for milk. The mechanism is identical to nature's — heritable variation plus selective reproduction — only the selector differs. Darwin opened his great book with pigeon breeders for exactly this reason: if human choice could reshape a species in centuries, nature could do far more over deep time. Now classify some scenarios.
