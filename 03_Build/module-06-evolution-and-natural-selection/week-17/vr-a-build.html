Week 17 · VR-A (Build / Model)
Model a Peppered-Moth Selection Scene Over Generations
Solo VR activity · ~30 minutes · 10 pts (completion)
🔬 WHAT YOU’RE DOING
You will build a population of peppered moths — some light, some dark — and place them on two kinds of tree bark: pale, lichen-covered bark and soot-darkened bark. Then you will “run” a bird predator across several generations and watch the frequency of dark and light moths shift as the better-camouflaged moths survive and reproduce more. This makes directional selection something you can see happen, generation by generation. Keep it accurate: selection changes the proportions in the population — individual moths do not change their own color. This is Lesson 17.02, built in space.
🔧 WHAT YOU’LL NEED
A VR headset (recommended) or the ENGAGE desktop app · your completed Lessons 17.01 and 17.02 · your scientific notebook.
📍 HOW TO JOIN
Device: VR headset or desktop · Session type: temporary
Session ID: posted by your teacher at the top of this Canvas assignment
Location: Natural Selection Lab — Peppered Moth Forest (Optima VR library)
On arrival: go to the workbench marked by the Whiteboard IFX. A sticky note there gives your first instruction.
📝 WHAT TO DO, IN ORDER
1
Build the starting population. From the parts bin, place about 20 moths on a tree: a mix of light and dark, mostly light to start. This is your Generation 1 — the variation selection will act on.
2
Set the environment. Switch the bark to the soot-darkened setting (industrial Britain). Notice which moths now stand out against the dark bark and which blend in.
3
Run the predator. Send the bird across the tree. It removes the moths that are easiest to see (the light ones on dark bark). The survivors “reproduce” — replace the eaten moths so the population is full again, in the same proportions as the survivors. Place a sticky note: which color survived more, and why?
4
Repeat for three generations and track it. Run the predator and refill the population two more times. After each round, record on the Drawpad the fraction of dark moths. Watch the dark form climb toward dominance — directional selection.
5
Record your walkthrough. Start a spatial recording. Walk along your scene and explain aloud, in about 60–90 seconds: the starting variation, why dark moths survived more on dark bark, how their reproduction raised the dark frequency, and — importantly — that no individual moth changed color. Use the words variation, differential survival, and directional selection.
📤 WHAT TO SUBMIT
Upload your spatial recording to this Canvas assignment. If you can’t record, submit one snapshot of your scene with the generation-by-generation tally plus a few typed sentences explaining why the dark-moth frequency rose and why individual moths did not change color.
⏱️ TIME · ABOUT 30 MINUTES IN-HEADSET
Facilitator note: Students externalize directional selection by running a predator over a varied moth population on darkened bark and tracking the rising frequency of the dark form. The crucial accuracy point: selection changes the proportions in the population across generations; individual moths never change color. Common sticking points: saying the moths “turned dark to hide” (wrong — populations shift, individuals don’t), and forgetting that survivors’ reproduction is what carries the change forward. A strong submission shows a tracked, generation-by-generation rise in the dark form and a recording that uses “variation,” “differential survival,” and “directional selection” correctly.
✅ SUCCESS CRITERIA (COMPLETION)
☐
A varied moth population is built on soot-darkened bark and run through at least three predator-and-reproduce generations.
☐
The rising frequency of dark moths is tracked across generations.
☐
A spatial recording (or snapshot + written explanation) is submitted that explains the shift and makes clear individuals did not change color.
