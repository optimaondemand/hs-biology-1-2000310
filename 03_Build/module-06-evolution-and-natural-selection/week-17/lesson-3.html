Lesson · Genetic Drift, Gene Flow, and Mutation
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If natural selection is non-random, how can pure chance also change a population — and where does new variation come from in the first place?
Selection is not the whole story. Today you'll learn three more mechanisms of evolution: genetic drift (random change, especially the founder and bottleneck effects), gene flow (alleles moving between populations), and mutation, the ultimate source of all new variation — straight from Module 5.

🧬 PART 1 · GENETIC DRIFT: EVOLUTION BY CHANCE

Luck, amplified in small numbers

Genetic drift is random change in allele frequencies from generation to generation — not because some alleles help survival, but simply because of the luck of who reproduces and which alleles get passed on. Like getting seven heads in ten coin flips, chance pulls small samples away from the average. That's why drift is strongest in small populations: in a population of millions, one chance death barely registers; in a population of twelve, it can erase an allele entirely.

🧱 PART 2 · FOUNDER VS. BOTTLENECK

Two situations make drift especially dramatic. In the founder effect, a few individuals break off and start a new population (island colonizers, an isolated human founding group); the new gene pool reflects only that small, chance sample. In the bottleneck effect, a population crashes (disease, disaster, over-hunting) and the few survivors carry a non-representative set of alleles. Real cases: cheetahs and northern elephant seals both show very low genetic diversity, consistent with severe bottlenecks. Flip the card to test the difference.

🔄 FLIP CARD · TAP TO REVEAL
A disease sweeps through a large beetle population, killing all but a dozen survivors. The survivors happen to be mostly green, though the original population was mostly brown. The rebuilt population is now mostly green.
Founder effect or bottleneck effect — and why?
(Tap to flip.)
✅ THE ANSWER
This is a bottleneck effect.
A bottleneck is a sudden crash that leaves a small, non-representative set of survivors — here, a disease cutting the population to a dozen. (A founder effect would instead be a few individuals leaving to colonize a new place.) Both are drift in small numbers, but the cause differs: crash vs. colonization. Cheetahs and northern elephant seals are real bottleneck examples.
🔗 PART 3 · GENE FLOW, MUTATION, AND RANDOM VS. NON-RANDOM

Movement mixes; mutation supplies; selection is the non-random one

Gene flow is alleles moving between populations through migration and interbreeding. Where drift and selection can make populations diverge, gene flow tends to make connected populations more similar. Mutation is the ultimate source of brand-new alleles — the random DNA change from Module 5. It supplies the raw variation; selection, drift, and gene flow only rearrange it. And remember the key contrast: selection is non-random (it tracks fitness), while drift is random (blind to whether an allele helps or hurts).

🧠 WHICH MECHANISM? · DRIFT, SELECTION, OR GENE FLOW
A flood randomly drowns half of a tiny lizard population. The survivors, by chance, are mostly long-tailed — tail length had nothing to do with surviving the flood. The next generation is mostly long-tailed.
Pollen from a field of red-flowered plants blows into a neighboring field of white-flowered plants, and red-flower alleles start showing up there. Over time the two fields become more alike.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

Evolution is more than selection. Chance (drift, with its founder and bottleneck effects) changes small populations; gene flow mixes populations together; and mutation, from Module 5, supplies the new variation everything else depends on. The crucial distinction: selection is non-random, drift is pure chance. Next lesson, the clearest proof of all that selection is real and ongoing — antibiotic resistance you can watch in days.
