Lesson focus: the three shapes of selection — and how human breeding mirrors nature
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
This assignment has two parts: classify four scenarios by the shape of selection (directional, stabilizing, or disruptive), and explain how artificial selection works by the same mechanism as natural selection. It should take about 15–20 minutes.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
How can you tell which shape of selection is acting on a population — and why is breeding a faster dog or a sweeter ear of corn the same process as natural selection?
📝 STUDENT DIRECTIONS
1
Part A — Classify the selection (8 pts, 2 each). For each scenario, name the shape of selection (directional, stabilizing, or disruptive) and give a one-line reason.
Scenario Shape & reason
After bark darkens with soot, dark peppered moths become far more common than light ones. 
Newborns of intermediate birth weight survive best; very small and very large newborns face greater risk. 
Where only small and large seeds exist, small-beaked and large-beaked birds both thrive but medium-beaked birds do poorly. 
During a drought leaving only large hard seeds, average beak depth in a finch population increases each generation. 
2
Part B — Artificial vs. natural (2 pts). In two or three sentences, explain how artificial selection (e.g., breeding corn from teosinte, or dogs from wolves) uses the same mechanism as natural selection — and name the one thing that differs.
✅ SUCCESS CRITERIA
☐
Part A: moth = directional; birth weight = stabilizing; two-seed birds = disruptive; drought finches = directional — each with a correct reason.
☐
Part B states that both rely on heritable variation + selective reproduction; the difference is that humans (not the environment) choose who breeds.
