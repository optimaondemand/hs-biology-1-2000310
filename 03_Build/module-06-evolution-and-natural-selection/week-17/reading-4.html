Reading 17.04
Evolution You Can Watch: Antibiotic Resistance
Anchor Reading · Natural Selection in Real Time
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Some people imagine evolution as something that only happened in the distant past, too slow to see. But in bacteria, whose generations pass in minutes, you can watch natural selection unfold in days. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 17.04. By the end you should be able to explain, step by step, how exposing bacteria to an antibiotic produces a resistant population — and why the antibiotic does not “teach” the bacteria or cause the mutation.

A population that evolves before your eyes

An antibiotic is a drug that kills bacteria or stops them from growing. When antibiotics first came into wide use, they seemed almost miraculous. But over time, doctors began to notice that drugs which once cured an infection no longer worked: the bacteria had become resistant. This is not a curiosity — it is one of the most important public-health problems of our age, and it is a textbook case of the four conditions of natural selection from Lesson 17.01, playing out fast enough to measure.

How resistance really arises — step by step

Walk through the four conditions:

1. Variation already exists. A bacterial population is enormous and varied. Through random mutation, a few cells happen to carry alleles that make them resistant to a given antibiotic — before the drug ever arrives. These resistant cells are rare, but they are there.

2. The variation is heritable. When a resistant cell divides, its daughter cells inherit the resistance.

3. Selection by the drug. Add the antibiotic. The susceptible cells — the vast majority — die. The rare resistant cells survive.

4. Differential reproduction. The survivors reproduce, and because they pass on resistance, the next generation is largely resistant. In a few rounds, a population that was almost entirely susceptible has become almost entirely resistant. The population evolved — by selection.

⚠️ CLEAR UP A COMMON ERROR
It is tempting to say the bacteria “learned” to resist, or “tried” to survive, or that the antibiotic “caused” the resistance mutation. None of these is true. Bacteria have no intentions; they cannot try. And the resistance variants pre-existed the drug — they arose by random mutation whether or not any antibiotic was ever present. The antibiotic does not create resistance; it merely selects the cells that already had it, by killing the ones that did not. The drug is the selecting environment, exactly as the soot was for the peppered moths.

Why this matters for medicine — and for you

Because every use of an antibiotic selects for resistant cells, overusing and misusing these drugs speeds the spread of resistance. Resistant strains such as MRSA (a resistant Staphylococcus) cause infections that are hard to treat. This is why doctors urge patients to take antibiotics only when truly needed and to finish the full prescription: stopping early can leave behind the hardier, partly resistant survivors to repopulate. Understanding the mechanism is not just academic — it changes how we should behave.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Antibiotic resistance is natural selection you can watch in real time, in a hospital or a petri dish. Variation (from pre-existing mutations), heredity, a selecting environment (the drug), and differential reproduction — the same four conditions, the same engine. The bacteria do not strive and the drug does not teach; the resistant simply out-reproduce the rest. It is the clearest proof a skeptic could ask for that the process Darwin described is real and ongoing — and it is the evidence base you will reason from in this week's writing practice.
