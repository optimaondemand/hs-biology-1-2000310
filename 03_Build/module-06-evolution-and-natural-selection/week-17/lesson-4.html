🧬 PART 1 · THE FOUR CONDITIONS, IN A PETRI DISH

Selection, sped up

Bacteria divide in minutes, so evolution that takes ages in elephants takes days in microbes. (1) Variation: through random mutation, a few cells in a huge bacterial population carry resistance alleles — before any drug arrives. (2) Heredity: a resistant cell passes resistance to its daughters. (3) Selection: add the antibiotic, and the susceptible majority die while the rare resistant cells survive. (4) Differential reproduction: the survivors multiply, so the population becomes largely resistant. Same engine as the peppered moth — only the selecting environment is a drug, not soot.

🧱 PART 2 · WHAT THE DRUG DOES NOT DO
⚠️ CLEAR UP A COMMON ERROR
Bacteria do not “try” to become resistant, do not “learn,” and the antibiotic does not cause the resistance mutation. Bacteria have no intentions. The resistance variants already existed by random mutation before the drug appeared. The antibiotic does not create resistance — it selects it, by killing the cells that lack it. The drug is the selecting environment, nothing more.

🔗 PART 3 · WHY IT MATTERS & A CER PRACTICE

Real stakes, and an argument to build

Every use of an antibiotic selects for resistant cells, so overuse and misuse speed the spread of resistance. Resistant strains such as MRSA are hard to treat. This is why doctors urge taking antibiotics only when needed and finishing the full prescription — stopping early can leave the hardier survivors to repopulate. ✏️ IN-LESSON CER PRACTICE (NOT GRADED — PREPARES YOU FOR THE MODULE CER)
In your notebook, build a short claim–evidence–reasoning argument answering: “Is antibiotic resistance an example of natural selection? Why?”
• Claim: State plainly whether resistance is natural selection.
• Evidence: Cite the facts — resistant cells pre-exist by mutation; the drug kills susceptible cells; survivors reproduce and pass on resistance.
• Reasoning: Link the evidence to the four conditions (variation, heredity, selection, differential reproduction) to show why the population evolves — and why the drug does not “teach” the bacteria.
This is practice for the kind of analytical writing your graded Module 6 CER will ask for. Keep your notes — you'll reuse this reasoning.
✨ CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH · TRACKING RESISTANCE ACROSS THE GLOBE
Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is now one of the most closely watched problems in global health. Recent research suggests that resistance genes can spread not only as bacteria reproduce but also by passing directly between different bacteria, which is part of why scientists are currently building worldwide surveillance networks — sampling hospitals, farms, and even wastewater — to track which resistance genes are emerging and where. Researchers are also exploring new strategies, from combination therapies to entirely new classes of drugs, to stay ahead of resistant strains. The picture is still developing, but the underlying logic is exactly what you learned today: wherever we apply an antibiotic, we select for the cells that can survive it.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

Antibiotic resistance is natural selection you can watch: pre-existing variation, heredity, a selecting drug, and differential reproduction — the same four conditions, sped up. The bacteria do not strive and the drug does not teach; the resistant simply out-reproduce the rest.
