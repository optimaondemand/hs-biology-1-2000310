Reading 17.03
Chance and Migration: Drift, Gene Flow, and the Source of Variation
Anchor Reading · Genetic Drift, Gene Flow, and Mutation
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Natural selection is the most famous mechanism of evolution, but it is not the only one. Populations also change by sheer chance and by the movement of individuals. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 17.03. By the end you should be able to explain genetic drift (including the founder and bottleneck effects), gene flow, and why mutation is the ultimate source of all the variation that the other mechanisms work on.

Evolution, measured in a population

Remember the rule from Lesson 17.01: populations evolve, individuals do not. A useful way to track that change is to watch allele frequencies — what fraction of a population carries each version of a gene. When those frequencies shift from one generation to the next, the population has evolved. Natural selection shifts them in a non-random way (the better-fitted leave more offspring). But frequencies can also shift for reasons that have nothing to do with fitness at all.

Genetic drift: evolution by chance

Genetic drift is random change in allele frequencies from one generation to the next, simply because of the luck of which individuals happen to reproduce and which alleles happen to get passed on. Flip a coin ten times and you might get seven heads — chance alone pulls a small sample away from the expected even split. The same is true of genes: drift is strongest in small populations, where a few lucky or unlucky events can swing the frequencies dramatically. In a population of millions, one chance death barely registers; in a population of twelve, it can erase an allele entirely.

Two situations make drift especially powerful, and both deserve names.

The founder effect: a few start something new

When a small group breaks off from a larger population and starts a new one — a handful of birds blown to an island, a few settlers founding an isolated community — that small group carries only a chance sample of the original population's alleles. The new population's gene pool is shaped by who happened to be in the founding group, not by the full variety of the parent population. This is the founder effect. Some alleles common in the founders may have been rare in the source; others may be missing entirely. Many island species and some human founder populations show frequencies very different from their mainland ancestors for exactly this reason.

The bottleneck effect: a crash that reshapes the pool

A bottleneck effect happens when a population is suddenly slashed — by disease, disaster, or over-hunting — leaving only a small, non-representative set of survivors. The survivors carry whatever alleles they happened to have, which may not match the variety of the original population, and the rebuilt population descends from just those few. Cheetahs show unusually low genetic variation, consistent with a severe bottleneck in their past. Northern elephant seals, hunted down to a few dozen individuals in the 1800s before recovering, today carry strikingly little genetic diversity. A bottleneck differs from a founder event in cause — a crash rather than a colonization — but the genetic result is similar: a small founding pool, shaped by chance.

Gene flow: alleles on the move

Gene flow is the movement of alleles between populations — individuals migrate and interbreed, or pollen drifts from one stand of plants to another, carrying genes across. Where drift and selection can make populations diverge, gene flow tends to do the opposite: it mixes gene pools and makes connected populations more similar over time. Cut off the flow — raise a mountain range, flood a land bridge — and populations are free to drift and adapt apart. That isolation is, again, a doorway to next week's topic, the formation of new species.

Mutation: the ultimate source of new variation

Selection, drift, and gene flow all rearrange variation that already exists — but where does brand-new variation come from in the first place? From mutation, the random change in DNA you studied in Module 5. A mutation can produce an allele that never existed before. Most mutations are neutral or harmful, but a rare few are useful, and those become the raw material the other mechanisms act on. Without mutation, the other processes would eventually run out of variety to work with. Mutation supplies the deck; selection, drift, and gene flow deal the cards.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Evolution is richer than selection alone. Chance (drift, especially in small populations), migration (gene flow), and the constant trickle of new variation (mutation) all change allele frequencies. The key distinction to carry forward: selection is non-random — it tracks fitness — while drift is random, blind to whether an allele helps or hurts. Together these mechanisms set the stage for the grandest result of all: the splitting of one species into many.
