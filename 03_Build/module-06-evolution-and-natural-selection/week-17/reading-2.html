Reading 17.02
Three Shapes of Selection (and the Human Hand)
Anchor Reading · Directional, Stabilizing, Disruptive, and Artificial Selection
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Natural selection is one engine, but it does not always push a population in the same direction. Depending on which individuals leave the most offspring, selection takes one of three shapes. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 17.02. By the end you should be able to tell directional, stabilizing, and disruptive selection apart, and explain how artificial selection — what breeders have done for millennia — is the same process steered by human choice.

Directional selection: one extreme wins

In directional selection, individuals at one end of a trait's range do best, so the whole population shifts that way over generations. The classic case is the peppered moth (Biston betularia) in Britain. These moths come in light and dark forms. Before heavy industry, light moths blended with pale, lichen-covered bark and were hard for birds to spot; the rare dark form stood out and was eaten. As coal soot from factories darkened tree bark across industrial regions in the 1800s, the situation reversed: now the dark moths were camouflaged and the light ones were conspicuous. Over decades, dark moths became far more common in sooty areas — a shift biologists call industrial melanism. After clean-air laws reduced the soot in the twentieth century, bark lightened again and light moths rebounded. The population tracked its environment, in one direction and then back.

Other examples follow the same shape: a drought that leaves only large, hard seeds can favor finches with deeper, stronger beaks, shifting average beak size upward in just a few generations.

Stabilizing selection: the middle wins

In stabilizing selection, the intermediate form does best and both extremes are selected against, so variation narrows and the average holds steady. A well-known example is human birth weight. Very small newborns historically faced greater health risks, and very large newborns posed greater risks during birth; babies of intermediate weight survived best. Selection here is not pushing the population toward a new value — it is trimming the extremes and keeping the trait centered. Stabilizing selection is probably the most common form in nature, quietly maintaining traits that already work.

Disruptive selection: both extremes win

In disruptive selection, both extremes are favored and the middle is selected against — the opposite of stabilizing. Imagine a habitat with two kinds of seeds, small soft ones and large hard ones, and few in between. Birds with small beaks handle the small seeds well; birds with large beaks crack the large ones; birds with medium beaks do poorly at both. Over time the population can split into two groups specialized for the two food types, with fewer intermediates. Because it splits a population into divergent forms, disruptive selection can be a first step toward the formation of new species — the topic of next week's lessons on speciation.

The human hand: artificial selection

Long before anyone understood genes, humans were running selection on purpose. In artificial selection (selective breeding), people — not the environment — decide which individuals reproduce, choosing the ones with traits they want. Every breed of dog, from the great Dane to the dachshund, descends from wolves shaped over thousands of years of human choice. The plump ear of corn was bred from a wild grass called teosinte, whose “ear” is a small, hard, few-kerneled thing you would barely recognize. Dairy cattle were bred for ever-greater milk yield. The mechanism is identical to nature's: heritable variation exists, and only some individuals are allowed to pass on their traits. The only difference is who does the selecting.

Darwin's clever argument: He opened his great book not with finches but with pigeon breeders. If humans could reshape a species so dramatically in a few centuries simply by choosing who breeds, then nature — selecting over millions of years — could do far more. Artificial selection was his familiar bridge to the unfamiliar idea.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

One engine, three shapes: directional pushes a population to an extreme, stabilizing holds the middle, disruptive splits toward both ends. And the same engine, steered by human choice, gave us our crops, our livestock, and our dogs. Recognizing the shape of selection lets you read what an environment is doing to a population — and disruptive selection points straight ahead, to how one species can become two.
