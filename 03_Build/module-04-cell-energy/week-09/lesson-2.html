Splitting Sugar: Glycolysis
Lesson · The First Stage of Respiration
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A whole sugar molecule goes in, the cell spends energy to begin — and pockets only two ATP. Where did the rest of the energy go?
Today we take respiration’s first step: glycolysis, the splitting of glucose. It happens in the cytoplasm, needs no oxygen, and is the most ancient step of all. You will follow one glucose becoming two pyruvate, balance the ATP ledger, and see why most of glucose’s energy is still locked away afterward.

🪒 PART 1 · SUGAR-SPLITTING IN THE CYTOPLASM
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Yeast can turn sugar into energy even sealed in a jar with no air — it is how bread dough rises and how grape juice ferments. What does that tell you about the very first step of breaking down sugar? (No wrong answer — just check in.)

The word tells you the deed

Glycolysis is Greek for sugar-splitting: glykys, “sweet,” plus lysis, “splitting” (the same lysis from a bursting cell in Module 3). One molecule of glucose (six carbons) is split into two molecules of pyruvate (three carbons each). It happens in the cytoplasm, outside the mitochondrion, and needs no oxygen. Because it is so ancient — older than oxygen-rich air — nearly every living thing still uses it, from bacteria to you. When you sprint and run short of oxygen, your muscles fall back on this same first step.

💰 PART 2 · THE LEDGER: SPEND TWO, EARN FOUR, KEEP TWO

Glycolysis must spend energy to make energy. The cell invests 2 ATP to start, then earns back 4 ATP — for a net of 2 ATP. It also produces 2 NADH, electron carriers that matter later.

The glycolysis ledger (per glucose) Amount
ATP invested to start− 2 ATP
ATP produced+ 4 ATP
Net ATP gained+ 2 ATP
Electron carriers made2 NADH
Pyruvate produced2 (three carbons each)

🔒 PART 3 · WHY THE PAYOFF IS STILL AHEAD

Glycolysis only cracks the door

Two ATP is a modest wage for breaking apart a whole sugar — and that is exactly the point. The great majority of glucose’s energy is not released here. It is still held in the bonds of the two pyruvate and in the electrons riding on the two NADH. To collect it, the cell must send the pyruvate into the mitochondrion, where the Krebs cycle and the electron transport chain finish the job. If oxygen is present, the real harvest is still to come — and that is where tomorrow’s lesson goes.
