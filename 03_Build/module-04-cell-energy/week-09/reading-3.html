Reading 9.03
The Krebs Cycle
Anchor Reading · The Second Stage of Respiration
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
The pyruvate from glycolysis now enters the mitochondrion, and here something important happens: the carbon you exhale is set free, and the cell loads up the carriers it will cash in for energy. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 9.03. By the end you should be able to locate the Krebs cycle, explain that it releases CO₂ and loads NADH and FADH₂, and see why those carriers matter more than the ATP it makes.

A doorway step: pyruvate becomes acetyl-CoA

Each pyruvate (three carbons) that drifts into the mitochondrion is first reworked into a molecule called acetyl-CoA (two carbons). Where did the third carbon go? It leaves as a molecule of carbon dioxide — the first CO₂ of the mitochondrial stages. This grooming step also loads one NADH. Because glycolysis made two pyruvate from each glucose, this happens twice, releasing two CO₂ before the cycle proper even begins.

Around the wheel, in the matrix

Acetyl-CoA enters the Krebs cycle — also called the citric-acid cycle, because its first product is citric acid (the sour molecule of citrus fruit). It runs in the matrix, the fluid center of the mitochondrion. It is called a cycle because it ends with the very molecule it started with, ready to accept the next acetyl-CoA and turn again, like a waterwheel.

Each turn of the wheel does three things: it releases CO₂, it makes a small amount of ATP directly, and — most importantly — it loads the electron carriers NADH and FADH₂. Because each glucose yields two acetyl-CoA, the cycle turns twice per glucose.

The breath you exhale begins here

Follow the carbon. Glucose had six carbon atoms. Two left as CO₂ in the doorway step (one per pyruvate), and the remaining four leave as CO₂ over the two turns of the cycle. Add them up — six carbons in, six CO₂ out — and the bookkeeping closes. That carbon dioxide diffuses out of the cell, into your blood, and out through your lungs. The next time you breathe out, remember: those carbon atoms were, moments ago, part of a sugar — and before that, part of a leaf, and before that, part of the air. The carbon cycle you will study in Module 10 runs straight through this matrix.

The cycle’s real job is not ATP

It is tempting to judge the Krebs cycle by the ATP it makes, but that ATP is small. Its true purpose is to harvest high-energy electrons and park them on carriers — NADH and FADH₂. Think of these carriers as fully loaded delivery trucks. They do not release their energy here; they carry it a short distance to the inner mitochondrial membrane, where the electron transport chain will unload them and use that energy to make the bulk of the cell’s ATP.

So the Krebs cycle is best understood as a loading dock. It strips the energy out of glucose’s carbon skeleton, packs it onto carriers, and sends those carriers to the place where the real ATP payoff happens. Tomorrow’s reading follows them there.

🏛️ A HUMAN STORY · THE PAPER THAT WAS REJECTED
The German-born biochemist Hans Krebs worked out the steps of this cycle in 1937. When he submitted his findings, the journal Nature declined to publish the paper, and Krebs sent it elsewhere. The work he could not at first get printed turned out to be one of the central discoveries of twentieth-century biology — the very hub of how cells release energy — and in 1953 it earned him the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. It is a quiet reminder that even foundational ideas can be doubted, delayed, or dismissed when they are new, and that patience and confidence in careful evidence are part of the scientific temperament.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

The Krebs cycle is where glucose finally gives up its carbon — and where the cell quietly stocks its shelves for the great energy harvest to come. Hold three things: the address (the matrix), the waste set free (CO₂, the breath you exhale), and the cargo loaded (NADH and FADH₂). Those carriers are the thread that ties this lesson to the next.
