When the Oxygen Runs Out: Fermentation
Lesson · Anaerobic pathways
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Sprint hard and your muscles burn within seconds. Bread dough rises overnight. What do these have in common?
Both are fermentation — the backup plan a cell uses when oxygen runs short. Today you will see why a cell would grind to a halt without oxygen, how fermentation rescues it by regenerating NAD⁺, and the two forms it takes — lactic-acid and alcoholic. You will also see its price: just 2 ATP.

⏳ PART 1 · THE TRAFFIC JAM WHEN OXYGEN IS GONE

Why the line stops without oxygen

In Weeks 8–9 you traced how a cell harvests energy from glucose. Glycolysis splits glucose and, to keep going, must hand its electrons to a carrier called NAD⁺, turning it into NADH. With oxygen present, those electrons end up at the electron transport chain, where oxygen accepts them — freeing the carrier and regenerating NAD⁺.

Take the oxygen away and the chain backs up: NADH cannot unload, NAD⁺ runs out, and glycolysis itself stops for want of that one recyclable part. The whole assembly line halts. The cell needs a way to regenerate NAD⁺ without oxygen — and that is fermentation.

🍞 PART 2 · TWO PATHS: LACTIC-ACID AND ALCOHOLIC

What fermentation does — and what it costs

The word fermentation comes from the Latin fervere, “to boil” — for the froth of fermenting juice looked like a gentle boil. Fermentation has one job: take electrons off NADH, dump them onto pyruvate, and so regenerate NAD⁺ — letting glycolysis keep splitting glucose. It makes no ATP of its own; the only ATP a fermenting cell harvests is the 2 ATP from glycolysis. Two routes do this:

Lactic-acid fermentation: pyruvate → lactate. This is the burn in hard-working muscle, and the souring that bacteria use to make yogurt and cheese.

Alcoholic fermentation: yeast turn pyruvate into ethanol + CO₂. The CO₂ leavens bread; the ethanol is the basis of brewing.

🧬 PART 3 · AN ANCIENT BACKUP PLAN

Old as life itself

Fermentation is life’s oldest energy strategy. The earliest cells arose in a world with almost no free oxygen, and fermentation — thin trickle of energy though it is — was enough to keep them alive. Even now it is everywhere: in your sprinting muscles, in the yeast that lifts your bread, in the bacteria that turn milk into yogurt. It is a reminder that a small, reliable supply of energy, faithfully kept flowing, is often enough to do the work of living.
