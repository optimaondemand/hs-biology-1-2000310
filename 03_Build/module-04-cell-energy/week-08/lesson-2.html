🌿 PART 1 · PUTTING MATTER TOGETHER WITH LIGHT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A 200-pound tree grew from a tiny seed. If you weighed the soil in its pot before and after, you’d find the soil barely changed. So where did most of the tree’s mass come from? (No wrong answer — just predict.)

The equation, read as a sentence

Photosynthesis takes low-energy raw materials and, powered by light, builds a high-energy sugar. Organisms that do this are autotrophs (“self-feeders”), or producers. Here is the whole process in one balanced summary:

6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
carbon dioxide + water + light → glucose + oxygen

Notice the bookkeeping: the carbon in the sugar came from CO₂, the energy came from light, and O₂ is released as a by-product. Same atoms as Module 2 — just rearranged into a form that holds energy.

🏛️ A HUMAN STORY · THE WILLOW, THE CANDLE, AND THE MOUSE
Around 1648, the physician Jan Baptista van Helmont planted a young willow in a weighed pot of dried soil and, for about five years, added nothing but water. The tree gained roughly 164 pounds; the soil lost only about two ounces. He concluded the mass came from water. His conclusion was wrong — he had no notion that air carried anything useful — but his method of careful weighing was a genuine revolution. Then in 1771 the minister Joseph Priestley found that a candle in a sealed jar soon went out and a mouse faltered — yet a sprig of mint somehow restored the “spoiled” air. A plant, he saw, releases the very thing that flame and breath use up. He could not yet name it oxygen, but he had caught photosynthesis from the outside. Two patient experimenters, each holding one true piece of a puzzle neither could finish alone.
🔬 PART 2 · INSIDE THE CHLOROPLAST

Two addresses, two stages

Photosynthesis happens in the chloroplast. Its structure explains its function. A double membrane wraps the organelle (a fossil of its endosymbiotic origin — Module 3). Inside are flattened sacs called thylakoids, stacked into piles called grana; this is where the light-dependent reactions happen. Around the thylakoids is a fluid, the stroma, where the Calvin cycle happens. Keep these two addresses straight — the rest of the week lives in them.

🌍 PART 3 · WHY PRODUCERS FEED THE WORLD

The base of every food web

Only producers can build food from sunlight and air. Everything else — you, a deer, a mushroom — is a heterotroph that must eat. So nearly every meal traces back to a green cell catching light, and the O₂ in your next breath was released by a leaf. You will follow this all the way to Module 10, where producers anchor every food web and feed the carbon cycle.

✨ FROM TODAY’S RESEARCH · CAN WE IMPROVE PHOTOSYNTHESIS?
Photosynthesis feeds the world, but it is not perfectly efficient — the enzyme RuBisCO, which you’ll meet in Lesson 8.04, sometimes grabs oxygen by mistake instead of carbon dioxide, wasting energy. Recent research suggests it may be possible to make crop photosynthesis more efficient: scientists are currently exploring engineered tweaks to these reactions in plants such as tobacco and rice, hoping to raise yields for a growing population. The results so far are promising in some field trials but not yet settled, and many years of testing remain. It is a striking thought — that humans might one day fine-tune the very process that has fed life for billions of years.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

Photosynthesis is one equation with two addresses: light captured in the thylakoid, sugar built in the stroma. Hold the equation and the chloroplast map, and the next two lessons simply fill in each stage. And remember what the equation quietly accomplishes: a leaf turns air and light into food and breath for the rest of us — the plainest possible picture of this module’s thread of service.
