📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
The light reactions made ATP and NADPH. But energy carriers are not food — you cannot eat ATP. In this final stage the cell spends that energy to build actual sugar from carbon dioxide. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 8.04 and the foundation for your graded Module 4 CER. By the end you should be able to explain the Calvin cycle, correct a stubborn misconception about it, and read a leaf-disk assay as a measurement of how fast photosynthesis is running.

Building sugar in the stroma

The second stage of photosynthesis is the Calvin cycle, named for the chemist Melvin Calvin who worked out its steps. It runs in the stroma — the fluid around the thylakoids — and it takes three things: the ATP and NADPH made by the light reactions, plus carbon dioxide (CO₂) from the air.

The cycle’s job is carbon fixation: capturing carbon from a free-floating gas and pinning it into a solid molecule. An enzyme with the nickname RuBisCO — recall from Lesson 8.01 that enzymes are protein catalysts — grabs CO₂ and attaches it to a waiting molecule. Powered by the ATP and reduced by the NADPH, the cycle then assembles these fixed carbons, three at a time, into three-carbon sugar molecules. Two of those combine to build one molecule of glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), the sugar named in the overall equation. The carbon that was thin air is now food.

⚠️ CLEAR UP A COMMON ERROR · “THE CALVIN CYCLE NEEDS DARKNESS”
The Calvin cycle is often called the light-independent reactions, and that name fools many students into thinking it must happen in the dark — or even that it is “the night shift” of a plant. Neither is true. “Light-independent” only means the cycle does not use light directly: no photon strikes it. But it is utterly dependent on the ATP and NADPH that the light reactions make — and those run out within minutes once the light is gone. So in real life the Calvin cycle runs during the day, fed by the light reactions happening right next door. Put a plant in the dark and the Calvin cycle keeps going only until its supply of ATP and NADPH is spent — then it stops. The correct idea: no direct light needed, but the products of light are absolutely required.

The two stages, handed off

Step back and see the whole machine. In the thylakoid membrane, the light reactions turn light + water into ATP, NADPH, and released O₂. In the stroma, the Calvin cycle spends that ATP and NADPH, together with CO₂, to build glucose. The energy carriers are the hinge between them. Light becomes chemical energy; chemical energy becomes sugar; sugar becomes the food and fuel of nearly everything alive.

Measuring photosynthesis: the leaf-disk assay

How do you measure a process you cannot see? A classic classroom method uses a clever trick. Small disks are punched from spinach leaves and their internal air is drawn out (with a syringe) so they become waterlogged and sink in a cup of liquid. Then they are placed under light in a solution that supplies CO₂.

As each disk photosynthesizes, the light reactions release oxygen, and that O₂ collects inside the leaf tissue. Gas is lighter than water, so the disk gradually becomes buoyant and floats back to the surface. The number of disks floating — and how quickly they rise — is therefore a direct measure of the rate of photosynthesis. More light should mean faster oxygen production, and therefore faster floating. A dark control (disks kept in the dark) should stay sunk, because with no light the light reactions make no oxygen.

Sample data: number of disks (out of 10) floating, recorded every 2 minutes
Light condition 0 min 2 min 4 min 6 min 8 min 10 min
High light 0 2 5 8 10 10
Medium light 0 1 2 4 6 8
Low light 0 0 1 1 2 3
Dark (control) 0 0 0 0 0 0

Read the table as a story: under high light the disks float quickly (fast photosynthesis); under low light they float slowly; in the dark they never float at all. The dark control is what tells you light is the cause — it is the same in every way except the one thing you are testing.

✏️ THIS IS YOUR MODULE 4 CER
The graded writing assignment for this whole module is built directly on this reading. You will use leaf-disk data like the table above to argue how light intensity affects the rate of photosynthesis, and to justify why using the mechanism you learned in Lesson 8.03 — that light drives the reactions that release oxygen. Read this page slowly and keep your notes: the claim (more light, faster photosynthesis), the evidence (the floating numbers and the dark control), and the reasoning (light powers the reactions that make the O₂ that floats the disks) are exactly what the CER will ask you to put in writing.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

With the Calvin cycle, the argument of this whole module closes: light became chemical energy, and chemical energy became sugar built from air. A floating spinach disk is that invisible process made visible — a rate you can count. And the glucose the leaf makes does not stay in the leaf; it becomes the energy that, through every food web, feeds the living world. That is the leaf’s service, and it is the heart of your CER.
