The Light Reactions
Lesson · Turning Light into Chemical Energy
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A photon travels ninety-three million miles to strike a leaf. What does the leaf do with it in the next split second?
Today we open the first stage of photosynthesis: the light-dependent reactions, in the thylakoid membrane. You will trace light to excited electrons, to a proton gradient, to the energy carriers ATP and NADPH — and discover that the oxygen you breathe is a leftover from splitting water.

☀️ PART 1 · CATCHING LIGHT, SPLITTING WATER
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Plants release the oxygen we breathe. The oxygen atoms have to come from one of the plant’s raw materials. Which raw material do you think the O₂ comes from? (No wrong answer — just predict.)

Light excites, water resupplies

In the thylakoid membrane, the pigment chlorophyll absorbs light. The energy excites electrons — lifts them to a higher energy level — and the chlorophyll passes them on to do work. To replace the electrons it lost, chlorophyll pulls new ones from water, splitting it apart (photolysis) and releasing electrons, H⁺, and O₂. That O₂ is the by-product you breathe.

⚡ PART 2 · FROM ELECTRONS TO PACKAGED ENERGY

A gradient, a turbine, two carriers

The excited electrons travel down an electron transport chain in the thylakoid membrane. Their energy pumps H⁺ into the thylakoid’s interior, building a steep gradient — a dammed-up reservoir. When the H⁺ flow back out, they spin a protein turbine, ATP synthase, which builds ATP. This flow-driven assembly is called chemiosmosis — remember the name, because the very same trick reappears in cellular respiration (Module 9). At the end of the chain the electrons are loaded, with H⁺, onto a carrier to make NADPH.

light + water → ATP + NADPH (kept) + O₂ (released)
The ATP and NADPH are handed to the Calvin cycle (Lesson 8.04). The oxygen is let go.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

The light reactions are the moment sunlight becomes biology: light in, water split, oxygen out, ATP and NADPH made. Hold that one-line summary. Next lesson the cell spends those carriers to build sugar — and that sugar, traveling out through every food web, is the leaf’s service to the rest of the living world. The oxygen, meanwhile, is simply a gift it leaves behind.
