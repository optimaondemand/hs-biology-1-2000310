🧮 PART 1 · WHY A CELL NEEDS A CURRENCY
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
You cannot pay for groceries with a barrel of crude oil, even though it holds enormous energy — you need cash. Why might a cell face the same problem with the energy in a molecule of food? (No wrong answer — just check in.)

Metabolism: building up and breaking down

All of a cell’s chemical reactions together are its metabolism. They come in two directions. Catabolic reactions break large molecules into small ones and release stored energy (think: tearing down). Anabolic reactions build large molecules from small ones and store energy (think: building up). A living cell does both at once — the energy released by tearing down is captured to power building up. Memory hook: cata- sounds like “cut” (break), ana- like “assemble” (build).

🔌 PART 2 · ATP, THE RECHARGEABLE BATTERY

Three phosphates, and a bond under strain

ATP stands for adenosine triphosphate. The name does the explaining: tri- means three, and ATP carries a chain of three phosphate groups. All three are negatively charged and repel each other, so the bond holding the third phosphate is under strain — like a compressed spring. Break that bond and energy is released for work; what remains is ADP (adiphosphate) plus a free phosphate. Reattach a third phosphate — using energy from catabolism — and you have recharged ATP. The same molecules cycle millions of times a second; ATP is a rechargeable battery, not a fuel tank.

ATP  ⇔  ADP + phosphate + energy
Left to right: spend energy. Right to left: store energy. This is energy coupling — catabolism recharges ATP, which powers anabolic work.

Sort a few reactions to make the two directions stick.

🔩 PART 3 · ENZYMES, THE TOOLS THAT MAKE IT POSSIBLE

Lowering the hill

Most reactions need a starting push — a hump of activation energy — before they will go. A cell cannot just heat itself to force reactions along without cooking. So it uses enzymes: biological catalysts, almost all of them proteins. (Recall from Module 2 that a protein’s function comes from its folded shape.) An enzyme has a pocket, the active site, shaped to fit one particular molecule, the substrate — like a key in a lock. By holding the substrate just so, the enzyme lowers the activation energy, so the reaction runs fast at body temperature, and the enzyme is freed to do it again. Wreck the shape with heat or extreme pH and the enzyme is denatured — the active site no longer fits, and the tool stops working.

🏛️ A HUMAN STORY · THE WORD “ENZYME” AND THE DEATH OF VITALISM
For a long time many naturalists believed living chemistry needed a mysterious “life force” that no test tube could reproduce — a view called vitalism. The story of enzymes is part of how that idea fell. In the nineteenth century the very word enzyme was coined (from Greek roots meaning roughly “in leaven,” as in yeast). Then in 1897 the chemist Eduard Buchner showed that juice squeezed from yeast — with no living cells left in it at all — could still ferment sugar. The chemistry of life, it turned out, was carried out by ordinary molecules obeying ordinary chemistry: catalysts, not magic. It is the same lesson as Wöhler’s synthesis of urea you met in Module 2 — life’s reactions are chemistry we can study, measure, and one day even imitate.
