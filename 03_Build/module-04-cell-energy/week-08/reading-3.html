Reading 8.03
The Light Reactions
Anchor Reading · Turning Light into Chemical Energy
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
A photon of sunlight has traveled ninety-three million miles to strike a leaf. In the next fraction of a second a green cell will catch that light and lock its energy into chemistry. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 8.03, the first stage of photosynthesis — the light-dependent reactions. By the end you should be able to locate where they happen, trace light to the energy carriers ATP and NADPH, and explain why the oxygen you breathe comes from splitting water.

The address: the thylakoid membrane

The light reactions take place in the thylakoid membranes — the flattened sacs stacked into grana inside the chloroplast. Embedded in those membranes are pigments, molecules that absorb light. The most important is chlorophyll, which absorbs red and blue light strongly but reflects green — which is exactly why leaves look green to you. The pigment is not just decoration: it is the antenna that catches the photon.

Catching light, and splitting water

When light strikes chlorophyll, its energy excites electrons — kicks them to a higher energy level. The excited electrons are passed off to begin doing work, and the chlorophyll must replace the electrons it lost. It does so by pulling them from an unexpected source: water. A water molecule is split apart — a step called photolysis (“splitting by light”) — releasing electrons, hydrogen ions (H⁺), and oxygen.

That oxygen is the key to a fact worth pausing on: the O₂ you breathe comes from split water, not from carbon dioxide. It is a leftover — a by-product the plant has no use for and simply releases. Every molecule of oxygen in the air around you was, at some point, pulled out of water by a chlorophyll molecule catching light.

From excited electrons to packaged energy

The excited electrons are handed down a series of proteins in the thylakoid membrane called the electron transport chain. As the electrons move along it, their energy is used to pump hydrogen ions into the space inside the thylakoid, building up a steep concentration gradient — a kind of dammed-up reservoir of H⁺. When those ions are allowed to flow back out, they rush through a remarkable protein turbine called ATP synthase, and the flow drives it to assemble ATP from ADP and phosphate. This way of making ATP — a flow of ions across a membrane spinning a molecular turbine — is called chemiosmosis, and you will meet it again in cellular respiration (Module 9). At the end of the chain, the electrons (together with H⁺) are loaded onto a carrier molecule to make NADPH.

Inputs (what goes in) Outputs (what comes out)
Light (the energy source) ATP — energy carrier → Calvin cycle
Water (H₂O) (split for electrons) NADPH — energy carrier → Calvin cycle
  O₂ — released to the air (by-product)

So the light reactions take light + water and produce ATP and NADPH — the chemical energy that the next stage will spend — while releasing oxygen. The light has been turned into something the cell can hold.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

The light reactions are the moment sunlight becomes biology. They hand the next stage two full wallets — ATP and NADPH — and they hand the rest of the planet its oxygen. Hold this clearly: light in, water split, oxygen out, ATP and NADPH made. In the next reading the cell spends that energy to build the sugar that ultimately feeds every heterotroph — the leaf’s quiet service made literal.
