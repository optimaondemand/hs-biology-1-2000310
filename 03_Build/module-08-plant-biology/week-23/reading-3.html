Reading 23.03
Roots and Stems: Every Feature Answers a Job
Anchor Reading · Structure Shaped to Function
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
You now know the three tissue systems. This reading follows them into two of the plant's great organs: the root and the stem. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 23.03. By the end you should be able to explain how a root's structure fits its jobs — anchorage, absorption, and storage — and how a stem's structure fits its jobs — support, transport, and storage. The theme is one you have met before and will meet again: form follows function.

Roots: anchor, absorb, and store

A root does three jobs. It anchors the plant in the soil so wind and water cannot topple it; it absorbs water and dissolved minerals; and in many plants it stores food for later. Every feature of a root answers one of these jobs.

Absorption is helped enormously by root hairs — tiny outgrowths of the epidermis that vastly increase the surface area in contact with the soil. More surface means more water and minerals taken up. Different plants anchor and gather in different patterns: a taproot system has one thick main root driving straight down (a carrot or a dandelion), good for reaching deep water and storing food; a fibrous root system spreads many thin roots through the topsoil (grasses), good for gripping soil and gathering surface water.

At the growing tip sits the root cap, a tough thimble of cells that protects the delicate tip as the root pushes through abrasive soil. Running up the center of the root is the vascular tissue — the xylem and phloem that carry absorbed water upward and bring food down for storage.

Stems: support, transport, and store

A stem also does three jobs. It supports the plant and holds the leaves and flowers up toward the light; it transports materials between the roots and the leaves; and some stems store food. (A surprising example: a potato is an underground stem, not a root — a swollen stem packed with stored starch.)

Transport runs through vascular bundles — strands of xylem and phloem threaded up the stem, connecting root to leaf. Along the stem, the points where leaves attach are called nodes; the stretches of bare stem between them are internodes. Reading a stem is partly reading its nodes and internodes.

Stems come in two broad kinds. Herbaceous stems are soft and green (a bean seedling, a sunflower). Woody stems are hard and thickened (a tree trunk, a shrub branch). In a woody stem, the wood is mostly xylem built up year after year, and the living inner bark contains the phloem. So even a tree trunk is the three tissue systems again — just grown massive and strong.

The theme: read the structure, name the job

Root: root hairs → absorb  |  root cap → protect the tip  |  taproot → anchor & store
Stem: vascular bundles → transport  |  nodes → hold leaves  |  woody stem → support
Point at any feature and you can name the job it answers — form follows function.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Roots and stems are the plant's foundation and framework — the parts that gather water below and lift the leaves above. Together with the leaf (your next reading), they complete the plant's body plan: roots absorb, stems transport, leaves make food. Hold on to the vascular tissue running through both root and stem — in Week 24 you will watch water climb that xylem all the way from a root hair to the top of a tall tree.
