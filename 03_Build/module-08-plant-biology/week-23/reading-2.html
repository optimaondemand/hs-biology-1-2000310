Reading 23.02
Three Tissue Systems: How Cells Become a Working Plant
Anchor Reading · Dermal, Ground, and Vascular Tissue
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
A single cell cannot run a whole plant. Cells of the same kind gather into tissues, and tissues cooperate to do the plant's work. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 23.02. By the end you should be able to name the plant's three tissue systems — dermal, ground, and vascular — describe what each one does, and match a plant structure to the tissue system doing its job.

From single cells to three great systems

A whole plant is organized into just three tissue systems, and — here is the beautiful part — each of the three runs continuously through the entire plant, from the tip of the deepest root to the edge of the highest leaf. They are not separate organs stacked on top of one another; they thread through the plant together, like an organ system running through a body. Think of the three as the plant's skin, its bulk, and its plumbing.

Dermal tissue: the plant's skin

The dermal tissue is the plant's outer covering, a layer called the epidermis. Its main job is protection — it is the boundary between the plant and the outside world. On leaves and stems, the epidermis secretes a waxy layer called the cuticle, a waterproof coat that reduces water loss and helps a land plant keep from drying out.

The dermal layer is not a solid wall, though. It contains guard cells that open and close small pores called stomata, letting gases move in and out. And on a root, the epidermis grows root hairs — thin extensions that reach into the soil and absorb water and minerals. Same tissue system, shaped to the job of the part it covers.

Ground tissue: the working bulk

Between the dermal covering and the vascular plumbing lies the ground tissue — the bulk of the plant, and the place where much of its work happens. Ground tissue does three big jobs: photosynthesis, storage, and support.

The photosynthetic and storage cells are called parenchyma — the soft green cells of a leaf's interior (the mesophyll) are parenchyma, and so are the starch-storing cells of a potato. For support, plants use two more cell types: collenchyma, which gives flexible support (the strings in a celery stalk), and sclerenchyma, which gives rigid support and forms tough fibers (the gritty stone cells in a pear, the hard fibers in rope). Ground tissue is where a plant feeds itself, stores what it makes, and stiffens itself between the veins.

Vascular tissue: the plumbing

The vascular tissue is the transport system — the plant's plumbing — and it comes in two pipelines. Xylem carries water and minerals upward from the roots to the rest of the plant. Phloem carries sugars — the food made in the leaves — to wherever the plant needs them. This is the same vascular tissue that, back in Module 7, let ferns and other plants grow tall by lifting water; here you see it as a system woven through the whole body. You will study xylem and phloem in depth in Week 24; for now, hold the pair firmly: xylem = water up, phloem = sugars.

The big idea: three systems, one continuous body

Dermal (skin) · Ground (bulk) · Vascular (plumbing)
protect & control water → make, store & support → transport water and sugar
Each system runs unbroken through root, stem, and leaf — form follows function at the scale of the whole plant.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Once you can name the three tissue systems, every plant part you meet next makes sense as a place where these systems do particular work. A root is where dermal tissue grows absorbing hairs; a stem is where vascular bundles run in strength; a leaf is where ground tissue is stacked full of chloroplasts. That is the plan for the rest of this week — and it is the classical lesson of biology restated at plant scale: to understand any structure, ask what tissue it is made of and what that tissue does.
