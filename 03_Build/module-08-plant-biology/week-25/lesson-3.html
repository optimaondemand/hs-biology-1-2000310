Lesson 25.03
Plant Hormones: Coordinating a Body Without Nerves
Lesson · Auxin, Gibberellins, Ethylene, and Abscisic Acid
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A plant has no brain and no nerves. So how does it “know” when to grow, when to ripen its fruit, when to drop its leaves, and when to shut its pores in a drought?
The answer is hormones — chemical signals that travel through the plant. Today you meet four: auxin (elongation, bending toward light), gibberellins (stem growth, germination), ethylene (a gas — fruit ripening, leaf drop), and abscisic acid / ABA (the stress hormone — closes stomata, keeps seeds dormant).

The four hormones at a glance

Auxin promotes cell elongation; made at the shoot tip, it drives phototropism (bending toward light) and apical dominance. Gibberellins promote stem elongation and seed germination, breaking dormancy. Ethylene is a gas that triggers fruit ripening and the drop of leaves and flowers. Abscisic acid (ABA) is the stress hormone: it closes stomata in drought and keeps seeds dormant, generally opposing the growth-promoting hormones.

🧠 PART 3 · COORDINATION WITHOUT A BRAIN

Why hormones are the plant’s control system

Notice that these hormones do not all push the same way. Auxin and gibberellins say “grow”; ABA often says “wait and conserve.” A plant’s behavior is the balance of these competing chemical signals. With no brain and no nerves, a plant still acts as one coordinated body — growing, timing, and protecting itself entirely through chemistry. This sets up Lesson 25.04 (tropisms) and a striking forward link to Module 9, where you will meet the human endocrine system — hormones coordinating your body the same way.
