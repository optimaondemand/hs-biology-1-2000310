🌞 PART 1 · THREE TROPISMS AND ONE MECHANISM
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before today’s lesson, would you have said a plant can “sense” things — like light or gravity or touch? (No wrong answer — just check in.)

Growth in a direction

A tropism is directional growth in response to a stimulus. Phototropism is growth toward light: light strikes one side, auxin moves to the shaded side, those cells elongate more, and the shoot bends toward the light. Gravitropism is growth in response to gravity — shoots up, roots down. Thigmotropism is growth in response to touch — a vine coiling around a support. In each case the plant senses a stimulus and responds with directional growth, driven by hormones (chiefly auxin) rather than nerves.

📚 A MOMENT IN THE HISTORY OF SCIENCE · DARWIN, HIS SON, AND A 46-YEAR QUESTION
Charles Darwin’s last major book was not about evolution but about plants: The Power of Movement in Plants (1880), written with his son Francis. Experimenting with grass and canary-grass seedlings, they showed that a seedling bends toward light — and, crucially, that the part that senses the light is the shoot tip, while the bending happens lower down. That implied some influence traveled from the tip to the growing region below. Darwin could not identify what it was. Nearly half a century later, in 1926, Frits Went showed that the mobile signal is a chemical: he let it diffuse out of cut shoot tips into blocks of agar, then placed the agar on one side of a decapitated shoot, and the shoot bent. The substance was identified chemically later and named auxin, the hormone you are about to meet. Darwin’s patient watching opened a question it took another scientist 46 years to answer: a model of wonder and honest, patient science — this module’s virtue in a single story.

✍️ PART 3 · IN-LESSON CER PRACTICE

Can plants sense and respond to their world?

Now put your reasoning to work. Below is a scaffold for building a short Claim–Evidence–Reasoning argument — the same skill you use in every module essay. Draft your answer to the question in your notebook, following the three steps.

🧠 CER SCAFFOLD · DRIVING QUESTION
“Can plants sense and respond to their world?”
1
CLAIM — a one-sentence answer
State your position clearly. Starter: “Plants do sense and respond to their environment — and they do it without a nervous system.”
2
EVIDENCE — give at least two specific facts
Pull from what you have studied this week. For example: phototropism (a shoot bends toward light); auxin redistribution to the shaded side; ABA closing stomata in drought; gravitropism (roots grow down, shoots up); thigmotropism (a vine coils around a support).
3
REASONING — connect the evidence back to the claim
Explain why your evidence proves the claim. In each example, a stimulus is detected (light, gravity, touch, drought) and produces a purposeful, directional response — carried out by chemical signals (hormones) rather than nerves. That is sensing and responding, even if it is slower than an animal’s reaction.
Note: This is practice for the CER skill — a short paragraph or three sentences in your notebook. It is not the graded module essay. Assignment 25.04 will ask you to write this up briefly.

Looking ahead to Module 9: plant hormones parallel the human endocrine system — both are chemical-messenger systems coordinating a whole body. It is a striking case of convergent solutions to the same problem: how does a living thing sense and respond to its world? Plants answer with chemistry alone; you will soon see how the human body answers with both nerves and hormones.
