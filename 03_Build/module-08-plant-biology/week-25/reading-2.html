Reading 25.02
From Pollen to Fruit: Double Fertilization, Seeds, and Sprouting
Anchor Reading · The Pollen Tube, Two Sperm, and How a Seed Begins
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
In Reading 25.01 pollen reached the stigma. But pollination is only the delivery — the pollen is still on the surface, and the egg is buried deep in the ovary. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 25.02, and it follows the rest of the journey: how a pollen grain grows a tube down to the egg, how flowering plants perform a striking double fertilization, and how that single event turns an ovule into a seed and an ovary into a fruit. You will end with germination — the moment a seed wakes and a new plant begins.

The pollen tube: a bridge to the egg

Once a pollen grain lands on the sticky stigma, it does something remarkable: it grows a long, thin pollen tube that pushes down through the style toward an ovule waiting in the ovary. The tube is the plant’s solution to a distance problem — the male cells cannot swim across dry land the way a fern’s can across a film of water, so the pollen builds its own private tunnel straight to the egg.

Down that tube travel two sperm cells. That number matters: flowering plants do not deliver one sperm, but two — and both will be used. This is the setup for the event that gives flowering plants their name for cleverness.

Double fertilization: two sperm, two jobs

Double fertilization is a hallmark of flowering plants — something no other group of plants does. Both sperm cells are put to work:

• The first sperm fertilizes the egg, forming a zygote. The zygote grows into the embryo — the tiny, dormant future plant.

• The second sperm joins with two polar nuclei to form the endosperm — a nutrient-rich food tissue whose whole purpose is to nourish the embryo as it develops and later as it sprouts.

So the two products of double fertilization are the embryo (the new plant) and the endosperm (its packed lunch). This is efficient: the plant does not build a food supply until it knows an embryo has actually been made.

The two products, side by side

sperm 1 + egg → zygote → embryo (the future plant)
sperm 2 + two polar nuclei → endosperm (food for the embryo)
Two sperm, two products — that is why it is called double fertilization.

Ovule becomes seed; ovary becomes fruit

After fertilization, the parts of the flower are repurposed. The ovule develops into the seed — a package holding the embryo, its stored food, and a tough protective seed coat. The seed is a plant embryo sealed in a container, able to wait for good conditions and to travel far from its parent.

Meanwhile the whole ovary ripens into the fruit. A fruit protects the seeds inside it and helps disperse them: an animal eats a sweet fruit and carries the seeds away in its gut, or wind and water move a light or floating fruit to new ground. An apple, a bean pod, a tomato, and a maple’s winged “helicopter” are all fruits — ripened ovaries doing the work of spreading seeds.

Germination: waking the seed

A seed can wait a long time. When conditions are finally right — enough water, oxygen, and a suitable temperature — the seed germinates: it sprouts, and the embryo begins to grow. At first the young plant cannot yet make its own food, so it lives on the stored food in the seed (the endosperm and food packed with the embryo). Only once it pushes up leaves into the light can it photosynthesize on its own (the process you studied in Module 4) and stop depending on its stored supply.

This is why the endosperm mattered so much: it is the fuel that carries the embryo from a dormant seed to a self-feeding green plant.

Looking back: the seed as a land invention

In Module 7 you met the seed as one of the great inventions that let plants leave the water behind — a drought-proof embryo that could wait and travel. Back then you saw that seeds mattered. Now you have seen how a seed is actually made: from double fertilization, to embryo and endosperm, to a coated seed inside a dispersing fruit. The land adaptation and its mechanism now fit together into one story.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Every fruit you eat and every field of grain is the product of double fertilization — the embryo feeds the next plant, and the endosperm (in wheat, rice, and corn) feeds much of the world. You now have the full arc from pollination to a sprouting seedling. Next you will ask a different question: once a plant is growing, how does it coordinate that growth — when to ripen, when to drop leaves, when to sprout — without a brain or nerves? The answer is chemical signals, and that is Reading 25.03.
