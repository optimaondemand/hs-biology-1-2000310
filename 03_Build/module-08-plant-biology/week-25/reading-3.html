Chemical Messengers: How a Plant Runs Itself Without Nerves
Anchor Reading · Auxin, Gibberellins, Ethylene, and Abscisic Acid
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
A plant has no brain and no nerves — yet it “knows” when to grow toward light, when to ripen its fruit, when to drop its leaves in autumn, and when to hold its stomata shut in a drought. How does a body with no nervous system coordinate all of that? The answer is hormones — chemical signals that travel through the plant and tell cells what to do. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 25.03. By the end you should be able to name four major plant hormones, give one effect of each, and explain how chemical signals let a plant act as a coordinated whole.

What a plant hormone is

A hormone is a chemical signal made in one part of an organism that travels to another part and changes what those cells do. Plant hormones are made in tiny amounts, but they have large effects — they are the plant’s way of sending instructions from cell to cell and from organ to organ. We will meet four of the most important: auxin, gibberellins, ethylene, and abscisic acid. Notice as you read that they do not all push in the same direction: some promote growth, and one mainly restrains it.

Auxin: the elongation and direction hormone

Auxin promotes cell elongation — it makes cells stretch longer. It is produced at the shoot tip and is central to two things you will study closely in the next lesson: phototropism, the bending of a shoot toward light, and apical dominance, the way a growing tip tends to suppress the side branches below it so the plant grows upward first. Auxin is the hormone of where and how much a plant grows.

Gibberellins: stems that stretch, seeds that wake

Gibberellins promote stem elongation and seed germination. They help a seed break out of dormancy — the resting state you read about in Reading 25.02 — signaling that it is time to sprout. Where auxin drives directional growth, gibberellins tend to drive overall lengthening and the awakening of a resting seed.

Ethylene: the ripening gas

Ethylene is unusual: it is a gas. It triggers fruit ripening and the aging and dropping — called abscission — of leaves and flowers. Because ethylene is a gas, it drifts from fruit to fruit, and ripening spreads.

This is the science behind the old saying that “one ripe apple spoils the barrel.” A ripening apple releases ethylene into the air around it; that ethylene reaches the apples nearby and speeds their ripening too, until the whole barrel turns at once. The same trick works on purpose: a ripe banana in a bag of hard fruit will hasten them along.

Abscisic acid (ABA): the stress hormone

Abscisic acid — ABA — is the plant’s “stress” hormone. In a drought, ABA signals the guard cells to close the stomata — the tiny pores on a leaf — so the plant loses less water through evaporation. (You will study stomata and water loss more in the transpiration work of this module.) ABA also keeps seeds dormant until conditions improve, holding them back from sprouting at the wrong time.

Notice that ABA generally opposes the growth-promoting hormones. Auxin and gibberellins say “grow”; ABA often says “wait, conserve, protect.” A plant’s behavior at any moment is the balance struck between these competing signals — a chemical tug-of-war that tunes growth to conditions.

The big idea: coordination without a nervous system

Auxin → elongation, bending toward light  ·  Gibberellins → stem growth, germination
Ethylene → fruit ripening, leaf/flower drop  ·  ABA → closes stomata, keeps seeds dormant

Step back and see what these four accomplish together. With no brain, no nerves, and no ability to move, a plant still behaves as a single coordinated body — growing, timing, protecting itself — entirely through chemical signals. Hormones are the plant’s coordination system.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Chemical coordination is not only a plant trick. In Module 9 you will meet the human endocrine system — hormones that coordinate your body, from growth to blood sugar. Plants and animals solved the same problem — how does a whole body act as one? — with the same kind of tool: chemical messengers. Hold onto that parallel. And hold onto auxin especially, because in the next reading you will watch it steer a growing shoot toward the light, one of the clearest cases in all of biology of a plant that senses and responds.
