Lesson 25.01
Flower Structure and Pollination
Lesson · Reading the Flower from the Outside In
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A plant cannot travel to find a mate. So how does a flowering plant reproduce while standing perfectly still?
Today you learn to read a flower like a diagram — naming each part from the outer sepals inward to the female carpel at the center, telling the male parts from the female parts, and describing how pollination carries pollen from an anther to a stigma using wind, water, and animals.

🌸 PART 1 · READING THE FLOWER, OUTSIDE IN
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of the last flower you looked at closely. What did you notice first — and what do you think it was for? (No wrong answer — just check in.)

Four rings, outside to inside

A flower is built in rings. On the outside are the sepals, which protect the bud before it opens. Inside them are the petals, whose color and shape attract pollinators. Then come the male parts — the stamens, each an anther (makes pollen) on a stalk called a filament. At the very center is the female part — the carpel (or pistil): a sticky stigma on top, a style stalk, and an ovary at the base holding the ovules, which contain the egg cells.

🧩 PART 2 · PART, JOB, AND SEX

Match each flower part to its role

Work through each one. Watch the male-versus-female distinction — it is the most common mix-up.

🧩 MATCH IT · PART → ROLE

The stigma is the sticky top of the carpel. Why is it sticky, and is it male or female?
The ovary sits at the base of the carpel. What does it contain?
What is the main job of the sepals?
🐝 PART 3 · POLLINATION: THE POLLEN’S JOURNEY

Getting pollen from anther to stigma

Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma — just the delivery. Because a plant cannot move, an agent carries the pollen: wind, water, or animals such as bees, butterflies, and birds. Animal-pollinated flowers earn that help with color, scent, and sugary nectar — a partnership that, over long time, can drive coevolution between flower and pollinator (a direct echo of Modules 6–7).
