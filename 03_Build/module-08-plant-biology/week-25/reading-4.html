Reading 25.04
Tropisms: A Plant Senses and Responds to Its World
Anchor Reading · Phototropism, Gravitropism, Thigmotropism, and the Auxin Mechanism
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Set a houseplant on a windowsill and come back in a week: it has turned toward the glass. Nobody moved it. It has no eyes, no nerves, no muscles — and yet it responded to where the light was. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 25.04. By the end you should be able to describe the major tropisms — directional growth responses — name the hormone behind them, and explain the mechanism well enough to argue a real claim: that plants sense and respond to their environment without a nervous system.

What a tropism is

A tropism is directional growth in response to a stimulus — the plant grows toward or away from something in its surroundings. This is how a plant “moves”: not by traveling, but by growing in a chosen direction. The response is slower than an animal flinching from a hot stove, but it is genuine: a stimulus comes in, and a purposeful, directional response follows.

Phototropism: growing toward light — and how

Phototropism is growth toward light. This is where the auxin from Reading 25.03 does its most famous work. When light strikes a shoot from one side, auxin moves to the shaded side of the shoot. Auxin promotes cell elongation, so the cells on the shaded side stretch longer than the cells on the lit side. When one side of a stem grows longer than the other, the stem bends — and because the shaded side grew more, it bends toward the light.

That is the mechanism in one sentence: auxin to the shaded side → shaded cells elongate more → shoot bends toward the light. No decision, no nerves — just a hormone redistributed and cells stretching in response.

Gravitropism and thigmotropism

Gravitropism (also called geotropism) is growth in response to gravity. Shoots grow up (away from the pull of gravity) and roots grow down (toward it). This is why a seed can sprout in the dark underground and still send its root down and its shoot up — it senses which way is down.

Thigmotropism is a growth response to touch. A climbing vine that brushes against a fence or a trellis will grow so as to coil around the support, gripping it for a way up toward the light. A plant that touches something solid grows differently than one touching nothing at all — it feels its way upward.

Sense and respond — with chemistry, not nerves

Phototropism → toward light  ·  Gravitropism → shoots up, roots down  ·  Thigmotropism → toward touch
All three: a stimulus is sensed, and the plant responds with directional growth, driven by hormones (chiefly auxin).

Put the pieces together and a claim emerges. In each tropism the plant detects something in its environment — light, gravity, touch — and produces a purposeful, directional response, using chemical signals rather than a brain and nerves. The response is slower than an animal’s, but it is a real sense-and-respond system. A plant is not a passive object; it is an organism reading its world and acting on it.

Looking ahead: two answers to one problem

Every living thing faces the same challenge: sense the environment, then respond to it. Plants solve it with hormones and directional growth — no nerves at all. In Module 9 you will see how the human body solves it two ways at once: fast electrical signals through the nervous system, and slower chemical signals through the endocrine system. Plant hormones are a striking parallel to that endocrine system — a case of convergent solutions to the same problem. Watching a shoot bend toward a window is watching one of biology’s two great answers to the question of how a living thing responds to its world.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

This reading closes Week 25 with the module’s virtue — wonder — on full display: a rooted, brainless plant that nonetheless senses and answers its world. In the lesson you will use exactly this evidence to build a short claim–evidence–reasoning argument that plants sense and respond without a nervous system. Come to the lesson ready to marshal the facts you just read: phototropism and auxin, gravitropism, thigmotropism, and (from Reading 25.03) ABA closing the stomata under drought.
