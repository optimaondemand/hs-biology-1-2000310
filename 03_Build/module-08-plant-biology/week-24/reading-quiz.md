**1. (Reading 24.01 — xylem structure and function)**
Which statement correctly describes xylem?

- A) It is made of living cells that carry sugar in both directions
- B) It is made of living cells that carry water downward to the roots
- C) It is made of dead, hollow cells that carry water and dissolved minerals upward from roots to leaves **✓**
- D) It is made of dead cells that pump sugar from leaves to fruits

*Xylem is built from dead, hollow cells (tracheids and vessel elements) reinforced with lignin, stacked into continuous tubes. It carries water and dissolved minerals one way — upward, from roots to leaves. Sugar transport and any living, bidirectional flow belong to phloem.*

**2. (Reading 24.01 / 24.03 — xylem vs. phloem, reversal misconception)**
In a tree trunk, which tissue carries **water and minerals upward** from the roots, and what kind of cells is it made of?

- A) Xylem, made of dead, hollow cells **✓**
- B) Phloem, made of dead, hollow cells
- C) Xylem, made of living cells with companion cells
- D) Phloem, made of living cells

*Water and minerals move up through the xylem, which is made of dead, hollow cells. Phloem is the living tissue that carries sugar. Reversing the two — the most common error on this topic — mixes up both what is carried and which cells do it.*

**3. (Reading 24.03 — phloem cargo and cells, reversal misconception)**
Sugar (sucrose) made in the leaves travels down to the roots to be stored. Which tissue carries this sugar, and is it living or dead?

- A) Xylem, and it is dead
- B) Xylem, and it is living
- C) Phloem, and it is dead
- D) Phloem, and it is living **✓**

*Sugar is transported by phloem, which is made of living cells (sieve-tube elements with companion cells). Xylem carries water and minerals, not sugar, and its conducting cells are dead. Watch the direction too: phloem can move sugar up or down, from source to sink.*

**4. (Reading 24.01 — cohesion-tension; cohesion = hydrogen bonding)**
The cohesion-tension mechanism depends on water molecules sticking to one another so the water forms one continuous column. What causes this sticking-together (cohesion)?

- A) Lignin gluing the water molecules together
- B) Hydrogen bonding between water molecules **✓**
- C) The plant spending ATP to link the molecules
- D) Air pressure pushing the water up from the roots

*Cohesion is water molecules clinging to one another through hydrogen bonding — the same property of water introduced in Module 2. This lets the water in the xylem behave as a single unbroken thread that transpiration can pull upward. The plant spends no cellular energy on the lift.*

**5. (Reading 24.02 — transpiration is the driver, not just a problem)**
A student claims, "Transpiration is simply a harmful water leak — it does nothing useful for the plant." What is the best correction?

- A) Correct — transpiration only wastes water and should be stopped
- B) Transpiration is useful only because it cools the roots
- C) Transpiration is also the engine of water transport: evaporation at the leaves creates the pull that draws water up the xylem **✓**
- D) Transpiration is what the plant uses to make sugar directly

*Transpiration does cost the plant water, but it is not merely a problem. The evaporation of water from the leaves creates the tension (pull) that lifts the whole water column up the xylem — the transpiration pull at the heart of cohesion-tension. It is both the cost of open stomata and the driving force of water transport.*

**6. (Reading 24.02 — stomata and guard cells)**
What happens to a leaf's guard cells when a stoma **opens**?

- A) The guard cells take up water, become turgid, and bow apart **✓**
- B) The guard cells lose water, become flaccid, and sag together
- C) The guard cells die and leave the pore permanently open
- D) The guard cells fill with sugar and burst

*Guard cells open a stoma by taking up water and becoming turgid (firm), which changes their shape so they bow apart. When they lose water and go flaccid, the stoma closes. This lets the plant balance taking in CO₂ against losing water — a response made without any nerves.*

**7. (Reading 24.02 — potometer data; which condition lowers transpiration)**
A class measures transpiration on a potometer under four conditions. Which condition would **lower** the transpiration rate compared with still, average air?

- A) Blowing a fan across the shoot (wind)
- B) Shining a bright lamp on the shoot (light)
- C) Warming the air around the shoot (higher temperature)
- D) Sealing a clear plastic bag over the shoot (higher humidity) **✓**

*Wind, bright light, and higher temperature all speed transpiration up (they remove humid air, open stomata, or speed evaporation). Higher humidity is the exception: moist air already holds water vapor, so the gradient out of the leaf is gentle and transpiration slows. Humidity is the one condition here that lowers the rate.*

**8. (Reading 24.03 — pressure-flow, source to sink)**
According to the pressure-flow mechanism, phloem sap moves because:

- A) Dead cells pump the sap upward using transpiration
- B) Sugar is loaded at the source, water follows by osmosis to raise the pressure, and sap flows to the lower-pressure sink **✓**
- C) The sink actively sucks sap upward against a pressure difference
- D) Gravity alone pulls all sap downward to the roots

*In pressure-flow, sugar is actively loaded into the phloem at a source (like a leaf); water then enters by osmosis, raising the pressure there. At the sink, sugar is unloaded and water leaves, lowering the pressure. Sap flows in bulk from the high-pressure source to the low-pressure sink, carrying its sugar with it.*

**9. (Reading 24.04 — nitrogen must be fixed by bacteria)**
The air is about 78% nitrogen gas (N₂), yet plants often need more nitrogen. Why can't a plant simply use the nitrogen in the air?

- A) Nitrogen gas is too heavy to enter the leaves
- B) Plants make all the nitrogen they need by photosynthesis
- C) Plants cannot use N₂ directly; nitrogen-fixing bacteria must first convert it into a usable form like ammonia **✓**
- D) The nitrogen in air is a different element from the nitrogen in proteins

*Plants cannot break apart the tightly bonded N₂ molecule on their own. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria, such as Rhizobium in the root nodules of legumes, convert N₂ into ammonia the plant can absorb — a mutualism in which the plant supplies the bacteria with sugar. Photosynthesis provides carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen, not nitrogen.*
