Reading 24.01
Xylem and the Rising of Water: How a Tree Drinks
Anchor Reading · The Cohesion-Tension Mechanism
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Stand at the foot of a tall oak and ask a simple question: how does water reach the topmost leaf, ten or twenty stories above the roots, with no pump and no beating heart? A plant has no muscles and spends no cellular energy on the lift — yet it raises tons of water a day against gravity. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 24.01. By the end you should be able to describe the plumbing that carries water upward — the tissue called xylem — and explain the elegant mechanism, cohesion-tension, that pulls the water to the very top.

Xylem: pipes made of dead cells

Water travels up through a vascular tissue called xylem. Here is the first surprise: the cells that make up a xylem tube are dead. As they mature, these cells — called tracheids and vessel elements — empty out their living contents, leaving only hollow, open-ended walls. Stacked end to end, they form long, continuous pipes that run from the roots, up the stem, and out into every leaf. The walls are reinforced with lignin, a tough, waterproof material that keeps the tubes from collapsing under the strain of the water column and gives wood much of its strength.

Xylem carries water and the dissolved minerals pulled in from the soil, and it carries them in one direction only: upward, from roots to leaves. It is a one-way road. (Hold onto that fact — in Reading 24.03 you will meet a second tissue, phloem, that does the opposite job in the opposite way, and students who confuse the two lose points on every exam.)

The puzzle: lifting water with no pump

Gravity pulls water down; the plant must move it up, sometimes to enormous heights. It cannot push the water from below with any force strong enough to reach the crown of a tall tree. So it does something cleverer: it does not push from the bottom at all. It pulls from the top.

The pull begins at the leaves. Water constantly evaporates from the wet inner surfaces of the leaf and escapes as vapor through tiny pores called stomata — a process called transpiration, which you will study closely in Reading 24.02. Each molecule that leaves tugs on the one behind it. That steady loss at the top creates a pull — a negative pressure, or tension — reaching all the way down the xylem to the roots.

Cohesion-tension: why the column does not break

A pull at the top would be useless if the water simply snapped apart into pieces. It does not — because of a property of water you met back in Module 2. Water molecules cling to one another through hydrogen bonding. This sticking-together is called cohesion, and it turns the water in a xylem tube into a single, continuous, unbroken thread running from root to leaf. When the top of the thread is pulled by transpiration, the whole thread moves up together, like water drawn up a drinking straw when you sip.

A second stickiness helps too: adhesion, the tendency of water to cling to the walls of the narrow xylem tubes, which helps resist the downward pull of gravity. Cohesion holds the column together; adhesion helps hold it up along the pipe walls; tension from transpiration draws it upward. Together they are the cohesion-tension mechanism.

Transpiration at the leaf → tension in the xylem → the cohesive water column is pulled upward
No pump. No cellular energy for the lift. The sun’s evaporation does the work.

A machine powered by the sun, not the plant

This is the part worth pausing over. The plant spends no ATP to raise water up the xylem. The energy comes from outside — from sunlight warming the leaves and driving evaporation. The plant simply arranges dead pipes, a continuous water thread, and open pores at the top, and lets physics do the lifting. A structure of dead cells, filled with a self-sticking liquid, moving water higher than any hand-pump could reach: this is worth a moment of genuine wonder.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Cohesion-tension is a bridge between units. It reaches back to Module 2, where water’s bent shape and hydrogen bonding first appeared — the very same cohesion now hauls water up a redwood. It reaches back to Module 4, because the whole system exists to deliver water to the leaf, where photosynthesis happens. And it points forward: in Reading 24.02 you will meet transpiration itself — both the “cost” of opening the leaf to the air and, at the same time, the engine that drives everything you read about today.
