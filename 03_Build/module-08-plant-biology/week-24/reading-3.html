Reading 24.03
Phloem and the Flow of Sugar: The Plant’s Second River
Anchor Reading · Source, Sink, and Pressure-Flow
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
A plant makes its food in the leaves — but roots buried in the dark soil, a swelling fruit, and a growing shoot tip all need that food too. So the plant runs a second transport system, entirely separate from the water pipes. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 24.03. By the end you should be able to describe phloem, the living tissue that carries sugar; explain the pressure-flow mechanism that moves it from source to sink; and — most important for your exams — tell phloem and xylem apart without hesitation.

Phloem: living pipes for a sugary cargo

Where xylem is built from dead cells, phloem is built from living ones. Its main conducting cells are sieve-tube elements, joined end to end into tubes, each kept alive and running by an attached companion cell that does much of its metabolic work. Phloem’s cargo is not water and minerals but sugars — mainly sucrose — the food made during photosynthesis, dissolved into a syrupy sap.

And unlike xylem’s one-way climb, phloem flow is bidirectional: sugar can travel up or down the plant, in whichever direction the need lies. Sap might flow down to the roots in one season and up to a swelling fruit in another.

Source to sink: where sugar comes from and where it goes

Phloem always carries sugar from a source to a sink. A source is any place where sugar is made or released — most often the leaves, busy with photosynthesis. A sink is any place where sugar is used up or stored away: growing root tips, developing fruits and seeds, or a storage root like a potato tuber.

Because a leaf is a source and a root is often a sink, sugar frequently moves downward — the opposite of water in the xylem. But roles can switch with the seasons: in spring, sugar stored in a root becomes a source that feeds the bursting new buds above. Phloem simply follows the need.

The pressure-flow hypothesis

How does the sap move? By a difference in pressure that the plant creates on purpose. At the source, the plant actively loads sugar into the phloem. The sap there becomes very concentrated, so — by osmosis (the water movement you met in Module 3) — water follows the sugar in from the neighboring xylem. That inflow of water raises the pressure at the source end.

At the sink, the opposite happens: sugar is unloaded to be used or stored, the sap grows dilute, water leaves by osmosis, and the pressure drops. Now there is high pressure at one end of the tube and low pressure at the other — and any fluid in a pipe flows from high pressure toward low. This bulk flow sweeps the whole sap along, carrying its dissolved sugar from source to sink. That is the pressure-flow hypothesis.

Load sugar at the source → water enters by osmosis → pressure rises → sap flows to the low-pressure sink → sugar unloaded

Do not reverse them: xylem vs. phloem

This is the single most common mistake students make on this topic — swapping the two tissues. Learn this table cold, and the mistake disappears.

Feature XYLEM PHLOEM
Cells Dead, hollow (tracheids, vessel elements) Living (sieve-tube elements + companion cells)
Cargo Water + dissolved minerals Sugars (mainly sucrose)
Direction One-way — upward (roots → leaves) Bidirectional — source → sink
Driving force Transpiration pull (tension); no plant energy Pressure difference from active sugar loading

A memory hook: xylem lifts water up using dead pipes; phloem carries food (the “ph” sounds like “f”) in living pipes, either direction.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Phloem completes the plant’s circulatory picture and knits together several earlier units. The sugar it carries was built in Module 4 by photosynthesis; the osmosis that drives the flow you first met in Module 3; and phloem works alongside the xylem of Reading 24.01 as two rivers running in one trunk — water climbing up, sugar flowing to wherever it is needed.
