Module 8 CER Writing Assignment
Reading the Potometer: How the Environment Drives a Plant’s Water Loss
Claim · Evidence · Reasoning · Integration · 12 pts · counts toward the CER writing category
📖 WHAT THIS IS
This is the writing that pulls Module 8 together. Across three weeks you studied how a plant is built (tissues, roots, stems, leaves), how it moves water and sugar (xylem, phloem, transpiration), and how it reproduces and responds. Now you become the analyst. Below is a set of potometer data — measurements of how fast a leafy shoot takes up water under different conditions. Your task is to read that data, explain how environmental conditions change the rate of water loss, and then justify the mechanism behind it: the cohesion-tension pull that lifts water from root to leaf. This CER is assigned at the start of Week 23 and due at the end of Week 25, so the whole module can inform your argument. Your teacher will read and score this one personally — it is not auto-graded.
📊 THIS IS A DATA-ANALYSIS CER
Unlike a CER built only from remembered facts, this one starts from numbers. Your claim must be grounded in the pattern you see in the table, your evidence must cite specific values from it, and your reasoning must explain the mechanism that produces the pattern. A strong essay does not just say “wind increased the rate” — it says by how much, and why the physics of cohesion-tension makes that happen.
🎯 YOUR TASK
Using the potometer transpiration data below, explain how environmental conditions affect a plant’s rate of water loss, and justify the cohesion-tension mechanism that moves water from the roots to the leaves.
📈 YOUR DATA · A CLASS POTOMETER INVESTIGATION
A class set up a potometer — a leafy shoot sealed into a water-filled tube — and measured how fast water was taken up by tracking an air bubble moving along the graduated tube. Water uptake is a close stand-in for the water lost by transpiration. Starting from a baseline, they changed one condition at a time and recorded the rate. This is sample class data for you to analyze.
Condition (one change from the baseline) Water uptake rate
(mm of bubble movement per minute)
Baseline — bright light, still air, 20°C, normal humidity4.0
Wind added (a fan blows across the shoot)8.5
Temperature raised to 30°C6.0
Humidity raised (a clear bag placed over the shoot)1.5
Light removed (shoot placed in darkness)1.0
A note on the tool: a potometer measures water uptake, which closely tracks the water lost by transpiration. Higher numbers mean faster water loss.
🧬 YOUR CONCEPT BANK
These are the ideas you studied this module. Use the ones that best explain the data — a strong essay connects each number to a mechanism.
Concept What it says · how it connects to the data
TranspirationWater vapor is lost from the leaves, mostly through the stomata. It is both the cost of opening stomata for CO₂ and the engine that pulls water upward.
Stomata & lightGuard cells open the stomata in the light (for photosynthesis) and close them in the dark — explains why darkness slows water loss to a trickle.
WindMoving air blows away the humid layer that builds up at the leaf surface, keeping the escape gradient steep — so water leaves faster.
Temperature & humidityHeat speeds evaporation, raising the rate; high humidity shrinks the difference between the wet leaf and the moist air, so less water escapes.
Cohesion-tensionEvaporation at the leaf creates tension (a pull). Because water molecules cling together by hydrogen bonding (cohesion — Module 2), the water forms an unbroken column, so the pull at the top lifts the whole column up the xylem. Adhesion to the xylem walls helps.
The linkFaster transpiration means water is pulled up faster — so the same conditions that raise water loss at the leaf also drive water uptake at the root. That is why the potometer works.
📝 BUILD YOUR ARGUMENT IN FOUR MOVES
Write in connected paragraphs, not a list. The wording is yours — these are the moves a strong CER makes.
1
Claim. State, in one or two sentences, the pattern the data shows: a plant’s rate of water loss rises with wind, heat, and light, and falls with high humidity and darkness. Make it a claim you will defend with numbers.
2
Evidence. Cite specific values from the table. Compare each condition to the 4.0 mm/min baseline — for example, wind more than doubled the rate to 8.5, while high humidity dropped it to 1.5 and darkness to 1.0. Use at least three comparisons. Numbers are what separate an argument from an opinion.
3
Reasoning — why the pattern happens, and how the water rises. This is the heart of the essay. Explain the mechanism behind the numbers: light opens the stomata (dark closes them); wind sweeps away the humid boundary layer; heat speeds evaporation; high humidity flattens the gradient. Then justify cohesion-tension: evaporation at the leaf creates tension, and because water molecules hold together by hydrogen bonding (cohesion, from Module 2), that pull draws an unbroken column of water up the xylem — no pump required. Explain why a faster transpiration rate means the leaf is pulling water up faster, which is what the potometer’s rising uptake measures.
4
Integration. Tie the argument to the larger story. Water’s cohesion is a property of its bent, hydrogen-bonding molecule (Module 2) — the same water chemistry, now doing structural work in a tree. The plant faces a genuine trade-off: it must open its stomata to gain CO₂ for photosynthesis (Module 4), but every open stoma loses water. You may close on the module’s virtue, wonder: a tall tree lifts water dozens of meters with no moving parts, powered only by sunlight and the stickiness of water.
✨ THE VIRTUE THIS PRACTICES · WONDER
This module’s virtue is wonder. It is easy to walk past a tree without a second thought — yet that tree is silently hauling hundreds of liters of water skyward every day, with no heart and no pump, using nothing but evaporating sunlight and the fact that water molecules cling to one another. The potometer lets you measure that invisible pull. Wonder is not the opposite of rigor; it is what careful measurement earns. Stephen Hales measured plant water in 1727 without knowing the mechanism; you now have both the data and the explanation. Let your essay show that you can be precise about a thing and still find it astonishing.

☐

☐

📊 CER SCORING RUBRIC (3 PTS × 4 CRITERIA = 12 PTS)
Criterion Strong (3) Developing (2) Beginning (1)
Claim Specific and data-grounded; names the full pattern (rises with wind/heat/light, falls with humidity/dark). States a direction but partial or vague about which factors do what. Missing, or just restates the prompt.
Evidence Cites 3+ specific values, each compared to baseline. Uses some data, but few numbers or no baseline comparison. No data cited, or data misread.
Reasoning Explains the mechanism for the conditions AND justifies cohesion-tension (pull + cohesion lifting the xylem column). Links data to a cause but the cohesion-tension mechanism is thin or partly wrong. Describes the data without explaining why.
Integration Reads as one coherent argument and ties to water’s properties (M2) and/or the stomata–photosynthesis trade-off (M4); within the word range. Components present but loosely connected. Listed independently without connection.
Total possible: 12 pts
