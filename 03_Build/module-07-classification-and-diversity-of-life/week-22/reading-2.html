Reading 22.02
The Animal Kingdom: Movement and the Body Plan
Anchor Reading · Invertebrates, Vertebrates, and Body-Plan Trends
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
If plants are the household’s cooks, animals are its travelers. Animals cannot make their own food, so they move to find it — and that single fact has shaped their bodies in astonishing ways. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 22.02. By the end you should be able to say what makes an animal an animal, tell an invertebrate from a vertebrate, and describe body-plan trends such as symmetry and cephalization — the concentrating of senses into a head.

What makes an animal an animal

Animals are multicellular heterotrophs — many-celled living things that cannot make their own food and must consume other organisms to get it. Unlike plant cells, animal cells have no cell walls, which lets tissues bend, contract, and flex. Most animals are motile: they move, at least at some stage of life, and movement is the thread that runs through their whole story. To move toward food is to need muscles to do it, senses to aim it, and nerves to coordinate it.

The animal kingdom is vast, but it splits first along one famous line: whether an animal has a backbone.

The invertebrates: animals without a backbone

The invertebrates are animals with no backbone, and here is the surprise: they are the overwhelming majority of animal life. The group runs from simple sponges and cnidarians (jellyfish, corals, sea anemones), through worms and mollusks (snails, clams, octopuses), to the echinoderms (sea stars, urchins). But one invertebrate group towers over all others in numbers: the arthropods.

Arthropods — insects, crustaceans (crabs, shrimp), and arachnids (spiders, scorpions) — wear a hard external skeleton and jointed legs, and they are the most numerous animals on Earth. Insects alone outnumber all other animal species combined. So the common belief that “most animals are things with backbones, like us” is exactly backward: most animals are invertebrates, and most of those are arthropods.

The vertebrates: animals with a backbone

The vertebrates are the animals with a backbone — an internal spine of bone or cartilage. They belong to the phylum Chordata, and though they are far fewer in species than the invertebrates, they include the animals we know best: fish, amphibians (frogs, salamanders), reptiles (lizards, snakes, turtles), birds, and mammals (including us). A backbone anchors a strong internal skeleton and protects the nerve cord, and it is one reason vertebrates include the largest animals ever to live.

Reading the body plan: symmetry and cephalization

Beyond the backbone, animals share deep patterns in how their bodies are laid out. Symmetry is one: a sea anemone has radial symmetry — body parts arranged around a center, like spokes — which suits an animal that meets its world from all sides. Most active animals have bilateral symmetry — a left and a right that mirror each other — which suits an animal that moves forward in one direction.

Bilateral symmetry travels with a second trend: cephalization, the concentration of nerves and sense organs into a defined head that leads the way. An animal that moves forward benefits from putting its eyes, mouth, and brain at the front — where it meets the world first. Many animals also have a body cavity that houses and cushions their organs. These trends are not a ladder from “worse” to “better”; they are different solutions to the shared demands of a moving life.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

A moving, food-seeking life demands muscles, senses, and nerves gathered into a head — the very features that reach their fullest form in the human body. That is the bridge into Module 9 (Human Body Systems), where you will study one vertebrate — ourselves — system by system. Remember the big picture: most animals are invertebrates, most invertebrates are arthropods, and body plans like bilateral symmetry and cephalization are the shared grammar of animal life.
