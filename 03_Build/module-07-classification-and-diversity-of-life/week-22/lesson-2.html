What every animal shares

Animals are multicellular heterotrophs: many-celled, and unable to make their own food, so they must consume other organisms. Their cells have no cell walls (unlike plants), which lets tissue bend and contract, and most animals are motile — they move. To move toward food you need muscles to do it, senses to aim it, and nerves to run it, so movement quietly shapes the whole body plan.

🦙 PART 2 · THE FIRST GREAT SPLIT

Backbone or no backbone?

Invertebrates have no backbone — sponges, cnidarians (jellyfish, corals), worms, mollusks (snails, octopuses), echinoderms (sea stars), and above all the arthropods (insects, crustaceans, arachnids), which are the most numerous animals on Earth. Vertebrates have a backbone and belong to phylum Chordata: fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals. Vertebrates are the animals we know best — but in sheer number of species they are a small minority.

Most animals are invertebrates  ·  most invertebrates are arthropods
The backboned animals we picture first are the exception, not the rule.

A frog leaps from a lily pad; inside its body runs a bony spine. Invertebrate or vertebrate?

👀 PART 3 · READING THE BODY PLAN

Symmetry and cephalization

A sea anemone has radial symmetry — parts arranged around a center, like spokes — useful for an animal that meets its world from every side. Most active animals have bilateral symmetry — a matching left and right — which suits moving forward in one direction. And bilateral animals show cephalization: they gather nerves and senses into a head at the leading end. Put your eyes, mouth, and brain where you meet the world first, and you meet it prepared. These are not steps from “worse” to “better,” but different answers to the demands of a moving life.

🏛️ A HUMAN STORY · ARISTOTLE’S FIRST GREAT SORT
Long before the modern kingdom system, the Greek philosopher Aristotle (384–322 BCE) spent years observing animals — dissecting, watching, and describing hundreds of kinds along the shores of the Aegean. He divided them into animals with blood and animals without blood, a split that lines up remarkably well with what we now call vertebrates and invertebrates. He grouped by careful observation of shared features, not myth, and he took the humble creatures — insects, shellfish, sea urchins — as seriously as the grand ones. He even noted that dolphins breathe air and bear live young, which set them apart from fish, and his writings on animals, such as History of Animals, shaped how people thought about the living world for centuries. Aristotle got plenty wrong, but his instinct was exactly right: that the living world has an order, and that patient, courteous observation can begin to read it. Every time you sort an animal by asking “backbone or not?” you echo a question first asked over two thousand years ago.
