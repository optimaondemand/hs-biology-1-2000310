Florida Biodiversity Across Kingdoms
Anchor Reading · The Whole Household of Life in One State
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
You have spent this module naming the great branches of life. Now look out the window. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 22.04, and its argument is simple: nearly every branch of the tree of life is represented right here in Florida. From microbes in a spring to a manatee in an estuary, the whole household is present. By the end you should be able to give examples of Florida life spanning several kingdoms and domains, and see how classification turns a walk outdoors into a reading of the tree of life.

The unseen domains: bacteria and archaea

Begin with what you cannot see. Florida’s clear springs and its vast wetlands teem with single-celled life from the domains Bacteria and Archaea. Bacteria drive the decay and nutrient cycling that keep water and soil productive; in the low-oxygen muck of wetlands and marshes, archaea such as methane-producing microbes carry out chemistry found in few other places. These invisible domains are the foundation the visible household is built upon.

Protists and fungi: the in-between kingdoms

In Florida’s estuaries — where rivers meet the sea — float countless protists: single-celled and simple many-celled eukaryotes such as the phytoplankton that photosynthesize at the base of the marine food web, and the algae that grow as epiphytes on blades of seagrass. Protists are the kingdom that does not fit neatly anywhere else — a reminder that the tree’s branches are not always tidy.

In the shade of Florida’s forests live the fungi — the mushrooms, molds, and the vast underground threads that decompose fallen wood and leaves. Fungi are heterotrophs like animals, but they feed by absorption, breaking material down where it lies. Without them, the forest would bury itself in its own dead matter.

The plant kingdom, Florida edition

The plants you named in Lesson 22.01 are all here. Along the coast, tangled mangroves hold the shoreline together and shelter young fish. On the sandy uplands stands the longleaf pine, a conifer whose fire-adapted forests once covered much of the Southeast, with saw palmetto spreading beneath it. Underwater, meadows of seagrasses — true flowering plants that returned to the sea — feed the manatee and nurse the coast. From gymnosperm pines to angiosperm grasses, the plant kingdom’s whole span grows within the state.

The animal kingdom, from spineless to spined

And the animals fill every corner. Florida’s waters and forests swarm with invertebrates — insects, spiders, crabs, and shrimp (arthropods, the most numerous animals of all), along with snails, corals, and sea stars. Among the vertebrates, Florida is famous: the gentle Florida manatee, a marine mammal grazing seagrass; the burrowing gopher tortoise, a reptile whose burrows shelter dozens of other species; the pink roseate spoonbill, a wading bird of the mangroves; and the American alligator, a reptile at the top of the wetland food chain. Mammal, reptile, bird — the vertebrate branches all appear.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Classification is not a dusty list of names — it is a lens. Once you can name the domains and kingdoms, a single Florida ecosystem reveals itself as the whole tree of life gathered in one place: microbes, protists, fungi, plants, and animals, each doing its work. That is the courtesy this module has been practicing — the ordered naming that lets us see how the household fits together. And it sets up Module 10 (Ecology), where these named organisms become the players in Florida’s food webs and energy flow.
