Reading 22.03
Viruses: Are They Alive?
Anchor Reading · Structure, Replication, and a Question at the Edge of Life
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
We have named plants, animals, fungi, protists, and the two great domains of single cells. But there is something the tree of life leaves out — something that causes colds, flu, and COVID-19, yet may not be alive at all. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 22.03. By the end you should be able to describe what a virus is built from, explain how it copies itself by hijacking a host cell, and understand why scientists genuinely disagree about whether viruses belong among the living.

A virus is not a cell

Start with what a virus is not: it is not a cell. Every living thing you have studied — every bacterium, protist, plant, fungus, and animal — is built of one or more cells. A virus has no cell. It is far simpler and far smaller: just a piece of genetic material — either DNA or RNA — wrapped in a protein coat called a capsid. Some viruses add an outer envelope of borrowed membrane. And that is essentially all a virus is: instructions in a container.

A common mix-up is worth clearing up now: a virus is not a bacterium. Bacteria are living, single-celled organisms with their own metabolism; viruses are neither cells nor alive in the ordinary sense. This is also why antibiotics, which attack structures inside bacterial cells, do nothing to a virus — there is no cell for them to attack.

Borrowed machinery: how a virus replicates

On its own, a virus does nothing. It has no way to take in nutrients, no way to release energy, no way to build proteins — it cannot carry out metabolism, and it cannot reproduce by itself. A virus is an obligate intracellular parasite: to make copies of itself, it must get inside a living host cell and hijack that cell’s machinery.

The basic pattern is simple to picture. The virus attaches to a host cell and injects or delivers its genetic material. That material then acts like a hostile instruction set: it commands the cell’s own ribosomes and enzymes to stop their usual work and instead build hundreds of new virus parts. Those parts assemble into new viruses, which are released — often bursting and destroying the host cell in the process — to infect more cells. Everything the virus “does,” it does with equipment it did not build and cannot own.

Why viruses sit outside the tree of life

In Lesson 20 you met the three domains — Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya — the great branches of cellular life. Viruses appear on none of them. Because they are not cells and cannot run their own metabolism, most biologists place viruses outside the domains and outside the tree of life altogether. When we sort the household of life, viruses are the guests we cannot quite seat.

The classic criteria of life — a useful checklist: living things are made of cells, carry out metabolism (use energy), reproduce, respond to their environment, grow, and maintain internal balance. Test a virus against each one and you see the problem.

A question that stays open

And yet the answer is not simple. Viruses have genes, they evolve by natural selection (which is why the flu shot changes every year), and they carry the same kind of genetic code as cellular life. So they are not merely inert chemistry, either. Most definitions of life exclude them — because they lack cells and cannot metabolize or reproduce on their own — but reasonable scientists still debate the point. This is a genuine boundary case, and it is exactly the kind of question that rewards careful claim, evidence, and reasoning. In the lesson, you will take a position and defend it.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Deciding what counts as alive forces you to say clearly what “alive” even means — and to use the cell theory from Module 3 and the criteria of life from Module 1 as your tools. The virus does not fit neatly, and that is the point: the household of life has a doorway, and viruses stand in it. Next lesson we come home to Florida to see how full that household really is.
