**1. (Reading 19.01 — order of the hierarchy)**
Which list places the levels of classification in the correct order, from broadest to most specific?

- A) Species, Genus, Family, Order, Class, Phylum, Kingdom, Domain
- B) Kingdom, Domain, Phylum, Order, Class, Family, Species, Genus
- C) Domain, Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species **✓**
- D) Domain, Phylum, Kingdom, Class, Family, Order, Genus, Species

*The Linnaean hierarchy runs Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species, from the broadest, most inclusive group to the narrowest. The mnemonic "Dear King Philip Came Over For Good Soup" fixes the order.*

**2. (Reading 19.01 — broadest vs. most specific)**
Which statement correctly compares two levels of the hierarchy?

- A) A species is the most specific level, with members sharing the most traits **✓**
- B) A genus contains more different kinds of organisms than a phylum, since it ranks higher
- C) A kingdom is a more specific level than a species, so its members share more traits
- D) A domain contains the fewest organisms of any level, since it is the most exclusive group

*Species is the narrowest rank; its members share the greatest number of traits. As you move down the ranks, groups get smaller and members become more alike. Domain, at the top, is the broadest and most inclusive level.*

**3. (Reading 19.01 — narrower rank = more shared traits; targets a common misconception)**
Two organisms are classified in the same **family** but different genera. A third pair is classified only in the same **kingdom**. What can you conclude?

- A) The kingdom-only pair is more closely related, because a broad group means more in common
- B) Relationship cannot be judged from classification, because ranks are only labels
- C) Both pairs are equally related, because all of them share the same kingdom
- D) The family pair is more closely related, because a narrower level means more shared traits **✓**

*Sharing a narrower (lower) taxon means organisms have more characteristics in common and are more closely related. Organisms that share only a broad level like kingdom are more distantly related. It is a common error to think sharing broader levels means closer relationship — the opposite is true.*

**4. (Reading 19.02 — correct binomial formatting; targets a common misconception)**
Which of the following is the **correctly written** scientific name for the gray wolf?

- A) *canis lupus*
- B) *Canis lupus* **✓**
- C) Canis Lupus
- D) *canis Lupus*

*A scientific name is written with the genus capitalized, the specific epithet lowercase, and the whole name italicized (or underlined when handwritten). "Canis Lupus" wrongly capitalizes the epithet — the single most common error — and "canis lupus" fails to capitalize the genus.*

**5. (Reading 19.02 — the two parts of a name)**
In the scientific name *Panthera leo* (the lion), what do the two parts represent?

- A) The genus (*Panthera*) and the specific epithet (*leo*) **✓**
- B) The kingdom (*Panthera*) and the phylum (*leo*)
- C) The common name (*Panthera*) and the Latin name (*leo*)
- D) The order (*Panthera*) and the family (*leo*)

*Binomial nomenclature gives each species a two-part name: the genus comes first (capitalized) and the specific epithet second (lowercase). These are the bottom two ranks of the Linnaean hierarchy. A shared genus name signals that species are close relatives.*

**6. (Reading 19.02 — why scientific names; targets a common misconception)**
A student says, "Common names work fine, so scientific names are unnecessary." Which fact best shows why scientific names are needed?

- A) Common names are always shorter and easier for scientists to remember
- B) Every organism has exactly one common name used worldwide
- C) Scientific names are used only for animals, never for plants
- D) The same common name, such as "robin," can refer to different species **✓**

*Common names are unreliable: one name can mean different creatures (the American and European "robins" are only distantly related birds), and one creature can have many names. A scientific name is universal, precise, and stable, so scientists everywhere mean exactly the same species — that is why common names are not sufficient.*

**7. (Reading 19.03 — what a dichotomous key is)**
What is a dichotomous key used for, and how is it structured?

- A) It ranks organisms from most to least advanced using a single trait
- B) It lists every known species in alphabetical order by common name
- C) It identifies an organism through a series of paired, either/or choices **✓**
- D) It measures how closely related two organisms are by comparing their DNA

*A dichotomous key ("divided in two") identifies an organism by a chain of steps, each offering two contrasting statements. At each step you choose the one that fits, which sends you onward or gives a name. It relies on observable traits, so anyone can follow it to the same result.*

**8. (Reading 19.03 — using a key)**
A short key reads: **1a.** Wings present → go to 2; **1b.** Wings absent → go to 3; **2a.** One pair of wings → Housefly; **2b.** Two pairs of wings → Butterfly. An unknown insect has **wings, in two pairs**. Which path and identification are correct?

- A) 1a → 2b, so the insect is a Butterfly **✓**
- B) 1b → 3, so the insect cannot be identified
- C) 1a → 2a, so the insect is a Housefly
- D) 1b → 2b, so the insect is a Butterfly

*Because wings are present, choose 1a and go to step 2. Two pairs of wings matches 2b, giving Butterfly. Following the paired choices in order — not guessing — leads to the correct name, and any careful user reaches the same answer.*

**9. (Reading 19.04 — good key characteristics; targets a common misconception)**
Which characteristic would make the **best** first step in a dichotomous key?

- A) "Is the organism beautiful?"
- B) "Does the organism have a backbone, yes or no?" **✓**
- C) "Is the organism interesting to study?"
- D) "Is the organism usually found near your home?"

*A good key characteristic is observable, binary (exactly two answers), and contrasting. "Has a backbone (yes/no)" is observable and either/or, so every user answers it the same way. "Beautiful" and "interesting" are opinions, and "found near your home" is an outside fact a user cannot check on the specimen — all poor choices.*

**10. (Reading 19.04 — constructing a key)**
When building a dichotomous key for a group of organisms, why is it best to start with a trait that divides the whole group roughly in half?

- A) Because the first trait in any key must always be about color
- B) Because the largest organism should always be identified first in a key
- C) Because a key can only ever contain two organisms in total
- D) Because an even split narrows the possibilities quickly at each step **✓**

*Starting with a trait that splits the group about evenly narrows the field fastest, so fewer steps are needed to isolate each organism. You then keep choosing observable, binary, contrasting traits and splitting until every organism stands alone at the end of its own path.*
