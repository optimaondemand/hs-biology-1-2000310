Reading 20.02
Reading and Building Cladograms: Monophyletic Groups
Anchor Reading · Clades, Nodes vs. Tips, and How Trees Are Assembled
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Yesterday you learned the parts of a tree. Today you learn to read one without being fooled — and to see how one is built. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 20.02. You will meet the monophyletic group (a clade), learn the single most important reading rule — relatedness comes from shared nodes, not from how close two tips happen to sit on the page — and see how ranking taxa by shared derived characters assembles a tree.

A clade: an ancestor and all its descendants

A monophyletic group — also called a clade — is a common ancestor together with all of its descendants, and nothing else. Picture a node on the tree and everything that branches above it: that complete package is a clade. Mammals form a clade; birds form a clade. The test is simple: put your finger on a node and sweep upward. If you have captured every tip descended from that ancestor and no outsiders, you have a monophyletic group.

The word means “single tribe.” It is the natural unit of classification, because it groups organisms strictly by ancestry — the ordered household of life, kept in true families.

The reading rule: nodes, not neighbors

Here is the mistake almost everyone makes at first: assuming that two tips drawn next to each other must be the most closely related. They need not be. Relatedness is measured by the most recent common ancestor — the lowest node that two tips share — not by their left-to-right position. A branch can be swiveled at any node like a mobile hanging from the ceiling: rotating it changes the order of the tips along the bottom but changes nothing about who is related to whom. The connections, not the spacing, carry the meaning.

Read the nodes, not the neighbors.
Two tips are most closely related when they share the most recent (lowest) common ancestor — even if a third tip is drawn physically closer to one of them.

Building a tree from shared derived characters

To build a cladogram, scientists start with a character matrix — a table of which taxa have which derived traits — and an outgroup to set the baseline. They then rank the taxa by how many shared derived characters each has in common with the group. A taxon sharing only the oldest trait branches off early, low on the tree. A taxon sharing that trait plus newer ones branches off later, higher up and closer in to the rest of the group. Each new shared derived character marks a new node.

Consider a lamprey, a shark, a lizard, and a mouse. All have a backbone; that groups them but does not rank them. Jaws are shared by shark, lizard, and mouse (not the lamprey), so the lamprey branches off first. Four limbs are shared by lizard and mouse (not the shark), so the shark branches next. Hair is unique to the mouse. The nested pattern of shared traits is the tree.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

A clade is ancestry made visible: one ancestor and all its descendants. Read a tree by its nodes, and no clever rearrangement of the tips can trick you. Build one by nesting shared derived characters, and the tree writes itself from the evidence. Next you will see how modern trees are built not from bones alone, but from the molecules inside them.
