Lesson focus: Turning a sequence-difference table into a branching order, and reasoning about the molecular clock
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
This assignment has three short parts: rank species by their sequence difference from a human protein, sketch the branching order, and explain the molecular clock and one of its limits. It should take about 20 minutes.
🧬 THE DATA · AMINO-ACID DIFFERENCES FROM THE HUMAN VERSION OF A PROTEIN
Species Differences from human
Gorilla2
Rabbit9
Snake22
Tuna (fish)31
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
How can the number of sequence differences between species reveal both their relatedness and roughly when their lineages split?
📝 STUDENT DIRECTIONS
1
Part A — Rank by relatedness (3 pts). Using the table, list the four species in order from most closely related to humans to least closely related, and state the rule you used.
2
Part B — Sketch the branching order (3 pts). Draw a simple tree (or describe it in words) showing, from the human line: which species branches off most recently, and which branches off earliest. Label the order of branch points.
3
Part C — The molecular clock (4 pts). In two or three sentences, explain the molecular-clock idea (how sequence differences estimate time since divergence) and name one limitation that means it is a calibration, not an exact stopwatch.
✅ SUCCESS CRITERIA
☐
Part A ranks Gorilla → Rabbit → Snake → Tuna, and states that fewer differences means more closely related.
☐
Part B shows the Gorilla branching off most recently (closest to the human line) and the Tuna branching off earliest.
☐
Part C states that more differences generally mean more time since divergence, and names a real limit (e.g., mutation rates are only roughly steady; they vary by gene, organism, or over time; selection can speed or slow change).
