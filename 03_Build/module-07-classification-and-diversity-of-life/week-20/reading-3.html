Reading 20.03
Molecular Phylogenetics and Molecular Clocks
Anchor Reading · Building Trees from Sequences, and Timing the Branches
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
So far we have built trees from visible traits. But the richest record of ancestry is written in a language you already know: DNA. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 20.03. You will see how sequence data builds modern trees, and meet the molecular clock — the idea that the number of sequence differences between two species can estimate how long ago they diverged, a useful calibration with real limits, not a perfect stopwatch.

Trees written in sequence

Today most phylogenetic trees are built largely from DNA and protein sequences. The logic is the same one you used with bones, but the characters are now the letters of the code. As lineages diverge, mutations — the copying changes you studied in Module 5 — slowly accumulate independently in each line. So two species that split long ago have had more time to pile up differences, while two that split recently still read almost the same. Scientists line up the sequences, count the differences, and let that pattern of similarity build the tree.

This is the same molecular evidence for common descent you met in Module 6 — now put to work as a measuring tool. Sequences give an enormous number of characters (every position is a datum), which is why they can resolve relationships that anatomy alone leaves fuzzy.

The molecular clock

If mutations accumulate at a roughly steady average rate over long spans of time, then the amount of sequence difference between two species can serve as a clock — more differences generally mean more time since they last shared an ancestor. This is the molecular clock idea. Calibrated against a few well-dated fossils, it lets scientists estimate when two lineages diverged, not just the order in which they did.

More sequence differences → (usually) more time since divergence
The clock is a calibrated estimate, not a precise stopwatch.

Why it is a calibration, not a stopwatch

The clock’s power comes from an assumption — a steady mutation rate — and that assumption is only roughly true. Mutation rates can differ between genes, between kinds of organism, and across different stretches of time. A gene under strong natural selection may change faster or slower than the background rate. So a molecular-clock date is an estimate with error bars, best trusted when it is calibrated against independent evidence such as dated fossils and when several genes agree. Used carefully, it is a remarkable tool. Trusted blindly, it can mislead.

This honesty about limits is itself good science: a number is only as trustworthy as the assumptions behind it.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Molecular data gave classification a new and powerful set of characters — the letters of the genetic code — and the molecular clock added a way to estimate when branches split. Both are strong tools held with honest caution. In tomorrow’s lesson you will see what happened when this new molecular evidence arrived: the tree of life itself had to be redrawn.
