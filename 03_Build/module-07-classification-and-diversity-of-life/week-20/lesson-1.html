🌳 PART 1 · DRAWING A FAMILY HISTORY

Phylogeny, tree, and the parts you read

A phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a group — who descended from whom. We draw it as a branching phylogenetic tree (a simple one is a cladogram). The tips are the taxa being compared. A node is a splitting point standing for a common ancestor; the branch point is where one lineage divides into two. Read from the base upward and you read forward in time, from ancient shared ancestors to the living groups at the tips. This picture rests squarely on common descent from Module 6 — if life shares ancestors, its relationships have a shape, and a tree recovers that shape.

🪪 PART 2 · WHAT DEFINES A GROUP

Shared derived characters and the outgroup

A group on a tree is defined by a shared derived character — a trait that is new in a recent common ancestor and inherited by all its descendants. Hair marks mammals; a backbone is too old and widely shared to separate a fish from a mouse. To decide which traits count as “new,” scientists include an outgroup: a taxon just outside the group of interest that sets the baseline. A trait the outgroup also has is ancestral; a trait it lacks but the others share is derived.

Shared derived character = a new trait that marks a branch  ·  Outgroup = the baseline that tells “old” from “new”

🔬 PART 3 · WHAT A TREE IS — AND WHAT IT DOES

What is it? A phylogenetic tree is a branching diagram of hypothesized relationships, with tips for taxa, nodes for common ancestors, and branch points for divergences. What does it do? It lets us read relatedness: two taxa are close relatives when they share a recent node, and a group is defined by the shared derived character that arose in its ancestor. The outgroup keeps us honest about where the comparison begins.

This is the tool the rest of the module runs on. Get comfortable finding nodes and naming the trait that marks a branch, and you can read the ancestry of any group.
