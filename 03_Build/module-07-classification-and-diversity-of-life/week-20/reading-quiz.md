**1. (Reading 20.01 — nodes)**
On a phylogenetic tree, what does a **node** (a branch point) represent?

- A) A living species available for study today
- B) A common ancestor from which the branches above it descend **✓**
- C) A place where two unrelated lineages merged into one
- D) A random point with no biological meaning

*A node is a point where a lineage splits; it stands for a common ancestor shared by all the tips that branch above it. Reading from the base upward is reading forward in time, from ancient ancestors toward the living taxa at the tips.*

**2. (Reading 20.01 — shared derived characters)**
Hair is found in mammals but not in fish, amphibians, or reptiles. On a tree, hair is best described as:

- A) An ancestral trait shared by all vertebrates
- B) A shared derived character that marks the mammal group **✓**
- C) An analogous structure that evolved many times
- D) A trait too common to define any group

*A shared derived character is a trait that is new in a recent common ancestor and inherited by all its descendants. Hair arose in the ancestor of mammals and marks that whole branch. A trait shared far more widely, such as a backbone, is too old to distinguish the smaller group.*

**3. (Reading 20.01 — outgroup)**
Why do scientists include an **outgroup** when building a phylogenetic tree?

- A) To add more species so the tree looks larger
- B) To provide a baseline for deciding which traits are ancestral and which are derived **✓**
- C) Because the outgroup is always the most important species
- D) To prove the tree can never be changed

*An outgroup is a taxon known to sit just outside the group of interest. A trait the outgroup also has is judged ancestral; a trait it lacks but the studied group shares is judged derived. Without an outgroup there is no baseline for telling "old" traits from "new" ones.*

**4. (Reading 20.02 — monophyletic group; core definition)**
Which of the following correctly defines a **monophyletic group (clade)**?

- A) Any group of species that look similar to one another
- B) A common ancestor together with all of its descendants **✓**
- C) A group that shares the same habitat
- D) An ancestor and only some of its descendants

*A clade is a common ancestor plus every one of its descendants, and nothing else — the natural unit of classification because it groups organisms strictly by ancestry. A group that leaves out some descendants, or is based only on appearance or habitat, is not monophyletic.*

**5. (Reading 20.02 — nodes not tips; targets a common misconception)**
On a cladogram, a crocodile is drawn right next to a lizard, while a bird is drawn farther away. A student concludes the crocodile must be most closely related to the lizard. Why is this reasoning flawed?

- A) Crocodiles and lizards are actually the same species
- B) Relatedness is read from the most recent shared node, not from how close two tips are drawn **✓**
- C) Birds cannot be placed on the same tree as reptiles
- D) The species drawn on the left is always the most ancestral

*Relatedness is measured by the most recent common ancestor — the lowest node two tips share — not by their left-to-right position. Two tips drawn side by side are not automatically the closest relatives; you must trace the branches back to the shared node.*

**6. (Reading 20.02 — rotating branches; targets a common misconception)**
A cladogram is redrawn by rotating one of its branches, changing the left-to-right order of the tips. What happens to the relationships shown?

- A) The relationships change, because the tip order defines relatedness
- B) The relationships stay exactly the same, because the nodes are unchanged **✓**
- C) The tree becomes invalid and must be discarded
- D) One species turns into another

*Rotating a branch is like turning a hanging mobile: the order of the tips along the bottom changes, but the nodes — and therefore which taxa share which common ancestors — do not. The connections, not the spacing, carry the meaning.*

**7. (Reading 20.03 — sequence difference and divergence)**
Two species' DNA sequences differ at only a few positions, while two other species differ at hundreds of positions. Assuming a roughly steady mutation rate, what does this suggest?

- A) The pair with few differences shared a common ancestor more recently **✓**
- B) The pair with hundreds of differences shared a common ancestor more recently
- C) Sequence differences tell us nothing about relationships
- D) The two pairs must have diverged at exactly the same time

*Mutations accumulate over time, so fewer sequence differences generally mean less time has passed since two lineages split. The pair with only a few differences therefore shares a more recent common ancestor — the reasoning behind molecular phylogenetics.*

**8. (Reading 20.03 — molecular clock and its limits; targets a common misconception)**
Which statement best describes the **molecular clock**?

- A) It measures the exact date of every evolutionary split with no error
- B) It assumes mutations accumulate at a roughly steady rate, giving estimated divergence times with real limits **✓**
- C) It proves that all species mutate at identical, unchanging rates
- D) It works only for species alive today

*The molecular clock uses the amount of sequence difference to estimate how long ago two lineages diverged, assuming a roughly steady average mutation rate. Because that rate is only approximately constant — it can vary by gene, organism, and time — a clock date is a calibrated estimate with error bars, not a precise stopwatch.*

**9. (Reading 20.04 — classification is revisable; targets a common misconception)**
Scientists move a species to a different branch of the tree after new DNA evidence contradicts its old placement. What does this best illustrate?

- A) That classification is unreliable and should be ignored
- B) That science self-corrects, revising conclusions when new evidence points elsewhere **✓**
- C) That the species itself physically changed
- D) That classification, once set, can never be altered

*Classification is a living science. A tree is our best current hypothesis, so groups can be merged, split, or moved as new evidence arrives. Revising a conclusion to fit the evidence is a strength of science, not a weakness — the tree now fits the evidence better than before.*

**10. (Reading 20.04 — Woese, rRNA, three domains)**
Carl Woese compared **ribosomal RNA (rRNA)** sequences and made which discovery that reshaped classification?

- A) That all bacteria are actually one closely related group
- B) That the organisms called "bacteria" were really two deeply different groups — Bacteria and Archaea — leading to the three-domain system **✓**
- C) That molecular data are useless for studying microbes
- D) That plants and animals belong to the same domain as bacteria

*In 1977, Woese used rRNA sequences — comparing molecules rather than appearances — and found that "bacteria" fell into two profoundly different lineages, Bacteria and Archaea. This led him to propose reorganizing life into three domains. His idea was resisted at first, but as molecular evidence accumulated it became standard.*
