Reading 20.01
Trees of Relationship: Cladograms and Phylogenetic Trees
Anchor Reading · Nodes, Branches, Shared Derived Characters, and the Outgroup
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Last module you gathered the evidence that life shares common ancestry. Now we draw it. A family tree of species is called a phylogenetic tree, or, in its simplest branching form, a cladogram. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 20.01. By the end you should be able to read a simple tree — find a common ancestor, follow where lineages split, and tell an ancestral trait from a shared derived character — and explain why scientists anchor a tree with an outgroup.

A picture of shared ancestry

A phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a group of organisms — the story of who descended from whom. Because that history is a story of splitting lineages, we draw it as a branching diagram. A phylogenetic tree (or cladogram) is exactly that: a hypothesis about how a set of species are related, shown as a pattern of branches. It rests directly on the idea you built last module — common descent. If living things share ancestors, then their relationships have a shape, and a tree is our attempt to recover that shape from evidence.

Notice the word hypothesis. A tree is not a photograph of the past; it is our best current reconstruction from the evidence we have. Good trees can be tested, revised, and sometimes overturned — a theme we return to at the end of the week.

The parts of a tree

Every tree has the same working parts. The tips at the ends of the branches are the taxa — the species or groups being compared. A node is a point where the tree splits; it stands for a common ancestor from which the lineages above it descend. A branch point is that moment of splitting, where one lineage divides into two. Reading from the base upward, you are reading forward in time — from older shared ancestors toward the living groups at the tips.

Tip = a taxon  ·  Node = a common ancestor  ·  Branch point = where a lineage splits
Two tips that meet at a recent, low node are close relatives; two that only meet at a deep, ancient node are distant ones.

What defines a group: shared derived characters

How do scientists decide which species belong together on a branch? They look for a shared derived character — a trait that is new in a recent common ancestor and inherited by all of its descendants. Hair, for example, is a shared derived character of mammals: it appeared in the ancestor of mammals and marks that whole branch. Because a fish, a lizard, and a mouse all have a backbone, a backbone cannot tell you which two are most closely related — it is an older, shared trait. But hair is derived: it separates mammals from the rest. The newer the trait, the finer the grouping it defines.

This is the heart of tree-building: relationships are read from which traits are shared and how recently they arose, not from overall similarity in appearance.

The outgroup: a baseline for the tree

To decide whether a trait is ancestral (old) or derived (new), scientists include an outgroup — a taxon known to sit just outside the group of interest. Suppose we are comparing a shark, a frog, a lizard, and a mouse. A shark, which branched off earliest, makes a good outgroup. A trait the shark also has (a backbone) is judged ancestral for the rest; a trait the shark lacks but the others share (four limbs) is judged derived. The outgroup is the baseline against which “new” is measured — without it, we would have no way to tell which end of the tree is the old end.

An outgroup is a courtesy the tree pays to honesty: it forces us to state where our comparison begins rather than assuming it.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

A phylogenetic tree turns the evidence for common descent into a working map of life’s relationships. Learn its parts — tips, nodes, branch points — and its logic — shared derived characters, judged against an outgroup — and you can read the relatedness of any group at a glance. Tomorrow you will practice reading these trees carefully, and meet the most common mistake people make when they do.
