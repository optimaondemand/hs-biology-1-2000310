Anchor Reading · Why Trees Get Revised, and How Woese Redrew the Tree of Life
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
A tree is a hypothesis, and hypotheses can change. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 20.04. You will see why classification is revised as new evidence — especially molecular evidence — arrives, and why that revising is a sign of health, not weakness. Then you will meet Carl Woese, whose comparison of a single molecule split the microbial world in two and redrew the whole tree of life — the story that opens next week.

Classification changes — and that is a strength

Because a phylogenetic tree is our best current reconstruction from the evidence, it is always open to revision. As new evidence arrives, groups get merged, split, or moved. Species once placed together on the strength of appearance are sometimes pulled apart when their molecules tell a different story; species that looked unrelated are sometimes joined. This is not a flaw in the science. It is self-correction — the willingness to change a conclusion when the evidence points elsewhere, the same honesty you met in the very first module of this course.

A classification that could never change would not be science; it would be a creed. The household of life is re-ordered, courteously and carefully, whenever the evidence asks for it.

Why molecular evidence reshaped the tree

For a long time, organisms were grouped mainly by how they looked. But appearances can mislead — unrelated organisms can converge on similar shapes, and microbes especially offer few visible features to compare. When scientists gained the ability to read sequences, they suddenly had a vast, shared set of characters that even two bacteria carry. Comparing those sequences revealed relationships no microscope could see — and in one famous case, overturned a division of life that had stood for a century.

🏛️ A HUMAN STORY · CARL WOESE AND THE DISCOVERY OF A THIRD DOMAIN
In 1977, the microbiologist Carl Woese did something new: instead of comparing how microbes looked, he compared the sequence of a molecule that every cell carries — ribosomal RNA (rRNA), part of the machinery that builds proteins. His results were startling. The single-celled organisms everyone had lumped together as “bacteria” were not one group at all. They fell into two deeply different lineages, as unlike each other as either was from plants and animals. Woese named them Bacteria and Archaea, and proposed reorganizing all of life into three domains. His idea was resisted and largely ignored for years — it asked biologists to overturn a familiar picture on the strength of a single molecule. But as more molecular evidence accumulated, the case became overwhelming, and the tree of life was redrawn. The three-domain scheme is now standard. It is a model of the module’s virtue, courtesy — letting the evidence, not habit, order the household of life — and of the courage to hold an unpopular conclusion until others caught up.
💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Classification is a living science: it grows, corrects itself, and improves as evidence accumulates. Woese’s work shows both the power of molecular data and the honesty at the heart of the discipline. Next week you will build on exactly this — the three domains of life, Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya, and how molecular sequence data reorders the whole living world.
