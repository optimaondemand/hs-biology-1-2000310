Classification Is a Living Science
Lesson · Why Trees Get Revised, and How Woese Redrew the Tree of Life
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If our tree of life keeps getting redrawn, does that mean the science is unreliable — or exactly the opposite?
A tree is a hypothesis, and hypotheses can change. Classification is revised as new evidence — especially molecular evidence — arrives: groups are merged, split, or moved. That revising is a strength; science self-corrects. You’ll meet Carl Woese, whose comparison of one molecule split the microbial world in two and set up next week’s three domains of life.

🌱 PART 1 · A TREE THAT GROWS AND CORRECTS ITSELF
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Scientists move a species to a new branch after DNA evidence contradicts its old placement. Does that revision make classification less trustworthy? (No wrong answer — just check in.)

Merged, split, moved — and better for it

Because a tree is our best current reconstruction, it is always open to revision. As evidence arrives, groups are merged, split, or moved. Species once grouped by appearance are sometimes pulled apart when their molecules disagree; others long thought unrelated are joined. This is self-correction — the same honesty you met in Module 1’s nature of science — not a weakness. The household of life is re-ordered, carefully, whenever the evidence asks.

🦠 PART 2 · WHEN MOLECULES REDREW THE TREE

One molecule, two hidden kingdoms

For a long time, organisms were grouped mainly by how they looked — and microbes offer few visible features to compare. When scientists could read sequences, they gained a shared set of characters even two bacteria carry, revealing relationships no microscope could see. In one famous case, this overturned a long-standing picture of life that textbooks had used for decades.

🏛️ A HUMAN STORY · CARL WOESE AND THE THIRD DOMAIN
In 1977, the microbiologist Carl Woese tried something new. Rather than compare how microbes looked, he compared the sequence of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) — part of the machinery every cell uses to build proteins. The result stunned biology: the single-celled organisms everyone had lumped together as “bacteria” were not one group but two deeply different lineages, as unlike each other as either was from plants and animals. That 1977 work by Woese (with George Fox) found a third deep lineage of life (the archaea); the three-domain system, with the names Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya, was formally proposed in 1990. His idea was resisted and largely ignored for years — it asked biologists to overturn a familiar picture on the strength of one molecule. But as molecular evidence piled up, the case became overwhelming and the tree of life was redrawn; the three-domain scheme is now standard. Here is this module’s virtue, courtesy — letting the evidence, not habit, order the household of life — joined to the courage to hold an unpopular conclusion until the evidence won.

Why did classification change when molecular data arrived?
(Think first — then tap to flip.)
✅ THE ANSWER
Molecular sequences gave a new, shared set of characters — readable even in microbes with few visible traits — that revealed relationships appearances had hidden or gotten wrong.
Woese’s rRNA comparison showed “bacteria” were really two domains (Bacteria and Archaea), so the tree was redrawn into three domains. New evidence → revised classification. That is science self-correcting.
