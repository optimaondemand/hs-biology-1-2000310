Bacteria and Archaea: Life Without a Nucleus
Lesson · Prokaryotic Structure and the Roles That Run the World
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The smallest, simplest cells on Earth run some of its largest chemical cycles. How does so much depend on so little?
Today we meet the two prokaryotic domains up close. You will describe how a prokaryotic cell is built — a nucleoid, ribosomes, a cell wall, sometimes a flagellum — and how it copies itself by binary fission. Then you will survey the astonishing metabolic diversity and the ecological jobs these microbes do: decomposing, fixing nitrogen, living in your gut, and thriving as extremophiles where nothing else can.

The prokaryotic body plan

A prokaryote (recall Module 3) has no membrane-bound nucleus and no organelles. Its loop of DNA sits loose in a region called the nucleoid. It still has ribosomes for building proteins, a cell membrane, and almost always a cell wall; many have a whip-like flagellum for swimming. To reproduce, it copies its DNA and splits — fast, asexual binary fission. That speed is why one cell can become a visible colony overnight.

Bacterial wall = peptidoglycan  ·  Archaeal wall = different chemistry (no peptidoglycan)
Same simple body plan, different molecules — part of why they are two domains, not one.
🏛️ A HUMAN STORY · THE DRAPER WHO SAW A NEW WORLD
In the 1670s a Dutch cloth merchant named Antonie van Leeuwenhoek — not a trained scholar, but a maker of astonishingly good hand lenses — turned one on a drop of pond water and rainwater scraped from between his teeth. Wriggling in the droplet were countless tiny living things he called animalcules. He was the first human being to see bacteria. Many doubted him at first; the claim seemed impossible. But careful observers repeated his work, and an entire invisible living world — the one this whole lesson is about — slowly came into view. It is a fitting reminder that a patient, honest eye at the edge of the known can open a door no one suspected was there.
⚙️ PART 2 · MANY WAYS TO MAKE A LIVING

Metabolic diversity and ecological roles

What prokaryotes lack in size they make up in metabolic diversity: some are autotrophs (making food by photosynthesis or from chemicals), others heterotrophs; some need oxygen (aerobic), others are poisoned by it (anaerobic). That range lets them do essential jobs: as decomposers returning nutrients to soil, as nitrogen fixers converting air nitrogen into a form plants can use (a key step in Module 10's nitrogen cycle), and as the gut microbiome that helps you digest food. Only a minority are disease-causing pathogens. The Archaea, meanwhile, include famous extremophiles — heat-lovers in hot springs, salt-lovers in brine, and methanogens making methane in marshes and animal guts.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

A prokaryote is a cell stripped to essentials — and that simplicity, paired with vast metabolic range, lets these microbes run the cycles the whole living world depends on. Their quiet, faithful, mostly unseen work is a form of service the household of life could not do without. Next we cross the great divide into Eukarya and meet its most varied members: the protists.
