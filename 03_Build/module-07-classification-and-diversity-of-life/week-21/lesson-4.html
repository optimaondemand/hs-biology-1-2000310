Fungi: The Great Recyclers
Lesson · Absorptive Nutrition, Hyphae, and Partnerships Below Ground
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A mushroom grows quietly in the soil like a small plant. So why is it no plant at all — and closer, in some ways, to you?
Today we meet the last of the week's four groups: the fungi. You will see how a fungus feeds — heterotrophic by absorption, digesting food outside its body — how its body of hyphae and chitin walls differs from a plant, and why fungi are the world's great decomposers and vital partners to plants and algae. We'll also correct the old error that put them in the plant kingdom.

🍄 PART 1 · NOT A PLANT AT ALL

Absorptive nutrition and a body of threads

A fungus makes no food of its own — it has no chloroplasts and cannot photosynthesize. It is a heterotroph that feeds by absorption: it secretes enzymes out into its surroundings, digests food outside its body, then soaks up the small molecules. Its body is a hidden web of fine threads called hyphae, together forming a mycelium; the walls are built of chitin, not the cellulose of plants. It reproduces by scattering countless tiny spores. Mushrooms, molds, and yeasts are all fungi. Once again, form follows function: a body of threads is a body built to absorb.

✅ THE ANSWER
No — fungi are their own kingdom.
Three reasons: (1) they are heterotrophs that feed by absorption — no photosynthesis; (2) their cell walls are chitin, not the cellulose of plants; (3) their body is threadlike hyphae, not roots, stems, and leaves. Looking plant-like is not being a plant.
♻️ PART 3 · RECYCLERS AND PARTNERS

The jobs fungi do

Fungi are the planet's chief decomposers. With bacteria, they break down leaves, dead wood, and remains, returning nutrients to the soil so plants can use them again — without this work, dead matter would pile up and nutrients would stop circulating. Many fungi are also mutualists. In mycorrhizae, fungal threads partner with plant roots: the fungus supplies water and minerals, the plant supplies sugars (a link you'll revisit in Modules 8 and 10). A lichen is a fungus living with an alga or a cyanobacterium — the photosynthetic partner makes food, the fungus provides shelter and moisture. A minority of fungi are pathogens, and fungi also give us food and medicine, including the first antibiotic, penicillin, made by a mold.

CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH · THE “WOOD-WIDE WEB”
Recent research suggests that the mycorrhizal threads linking one plant's roots to a fungus may sometimes connect to the roots of other plants as well, forming shared underground networks — popularly nicknamed the “wood-wide web.” Scientists are currently exploring whether resources such as sugars or nutrients, and possibly even chemical signals, can move between plants through these fungal connections. It is a genuinely exciting idea, but an actively debated one: researchers disagree about how common these networks are and how much actually passes through them in a real forest, and some caution that popular accounts have run ahead of the evidence. It is a good example of science in progress — a striking hypothesis still being carefully tested.

💪 THE IDEA THIS BUILDS

Fungi are a kingdom of their own: heterotrophs that digest by absorption, built of chitin-walled threads, recycling the dead and partnering with the living. Together with the prokaryotes and protists, they show how much larger the household of life is than the plants and animals we notice first — and how careful, evidence-guided naming lets us know it.
