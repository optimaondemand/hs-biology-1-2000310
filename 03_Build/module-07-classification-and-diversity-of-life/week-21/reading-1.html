The Three-Domain System
Anchor Reading · How Molecules, Not Appearances, Draw the Deepest Branches
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Last week you learned that classification groups living things by shared ancestry, and that a scientist named Carl Woese used the molecule rRNA to read those relationships. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 21.01. By the end you should be able to name the three domains of life and, more importantly, explain why molecular evidence — not the way organisms look under a microscope — is what defines them. This idea is the seed of your Module 7 CER.

Two ways to divide the living world

For most of the twentieth century, biologists sorted life into a handful of kingdoms — plants, animals, fungi, protists, and the tiny things without a nucleus, the bacteria. The dividing lines were drawn mostly by appearance: what an organism looked like, how it moved, whether it made its own food. Under a microscope, all the organisms without a nucleus — the prokaryotes — looked much alike: small, simple cells with no membrane-bound nucleus and no complex organelles. It seemed natural to lump them all into one group.

But appearance can mislead. Two organisms can look almost identical and still be only distantly related — and two that look different can be close kin. To read true relationships, biologists needed a deeper kind of evidence than shape alone. They found it in molecules.

The molecular surprise: three domains, not two

Every living cell builds proteins on tiny machines called ribosomes, and ribosomes are built partly from a molecule called ribosomal RNA (rRNA). Because rRNA does the same essential job in every organism, it changes only very slowly over long stretches of time — which makes it a superb molecular clock. When scientists compared rRNA sequences across many organisms, they found something no microscope had revealed: the prokaryotes were not one group but two, as different from each other in their molecules as either was from a plant or an animal.

Life divides at the top into three domains: Bacteria  ·  Archaea  ·  Eukarya
Domains sit above kingdoms — they are the deepest, oldest branches of the tree of life.

The twist: Archaea are our closer cousins

The three domains are Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya (all the organisms with a true nucleus — protists, fungi, plants, and animals, including you). Here is the part that overturned the old picture. Bacteria and Archaea are both prokaryotic; side by side under a lens they look nearly the same. Yet their molecules tell a startling story: Archaea are more closely related to Eukarya — to us — than they are to Bacteria. The two groups that look most alike are not, in fact, the closest kin.

This is the heart of the lesson. If we had trusted appearance, we would have kept all the nucleus-less cells in one bin and never seen this relationship at all. It was molecular sequence data — reading the organisms' own genetic text — that uncovered the true three-domain structure. Structure fooled us; sequence set us straight.

How this fits with kingdoms

The three-domain system does not throw out kingdoms; it puts a larger, deeper level above them. The older six-kingdom scheme sorted life into Bacteria, Archaea, Protists, Fungi, Plants, and Animals. In the newer view, domains sit above kingdoms: the domain Eukarya contains the kingdoms Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia, while Bacteria and Archaea are each both a domain and, in effect, a great group of their own. Think of it as adding a top shelf to a well-organized cabinet — the ordered naming of the whole household of life, with the biggest categories first.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

The three-domain system is more than a new set of labels. It is a case study in how we know: two lines of evidence — how organisms look and what their molecules say — pointed to different family trees, and the molecular evidence won because it reads ancestry directly. Keep this contrast in mind; it is exactly the argument your Module 7 CER will ask you to make. The next three readings tour the astonishing diversity these domains contain: the prokaryotes, the protists, and the fungi.
