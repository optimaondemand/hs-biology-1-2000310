Reading 21.03
Protists: The Eukaryotic Grab-Bag
Anchor Reading · The Diverse Eukaryotes That Are Not Plants, Animals, or Fungi
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
We now enter the domain Eukarya — organisms whose cells have a true nucleus. Its first and strangest group is the protists. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 21.03. By the end you should be able to describe why protists are so diverse and hard to define, and explain why one group of them — the ocean's phytoplankton — is quietly among the most important life on the planet.

Defined by what they are not

A protist is a eukaryote that is not a plant, an animal, or a fungus. That is an unusual way to define a group — by exclusion — and it is a clue to something important: the protists are not a single, tidy branch of the tree of life. They are a catch-all, a collection of many only-distantly-related lineages that happen to share a level of organization: mostly unicellular, eukaryotic, and everything left over once the three familiar kingdoms are set aside. Biologists keep the word “protist” for convenience while knowing it does not name one clean family.

“Protist” is a convenient label, not a single clean branch of the tree.
This is itself a lesson from molecular data: DNA shows the group is far more varied than its one name suggests.

Three informal lifestyles

Because the group is so mixed, biologists often sort protists loosely by how they live rather than by ancestry:

  • Animal-like protists (protozoa) ingest their food and are often able to move. The shape-shifting Amoeba engulfs prey by flowing around it; the slipper-shaped Paramecium beats rows of tiny hairs to swim and sweep in food. They behave, in miniature, like tiny hunters.
  • Plant-like protists (algae) carry out photosynthesis. They range from single cells to giant kelp. Among them are the phytoplankton — microscopic drifting algae — which we will return to below.
  • Fungus-like protists, such as the slime molds, absorb nutrients from decaying matter, much as true fungi do, though they are not closely related to them.

These labels describe function, not family: an “animal-like” protist is no relation to animals. They simply help us organize a chaotic group.

The mightiest of the small: phytoplankton

Drifting in the sunlit surface of every ocean are countless phytoplankton — photosynthetic protists too small to see. Their importance is hard to overstate. Because they photosynthesize across the vast area of the seas, they are a major source of the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere — a great share of every breath you take traces back to them. And because they turn sunlight into food, they are the base of nearly every aquatic food web: the tiny animals that graze on them feed the fish, which feed the larger fish, seabirds, and whales.

This looks forward to Module 10, where you will trace how energy flows from producers up through an ecosystem. Here is the point to carry: the producers at the bottom of the ocean's energy pyramid are mostly these overlooked protists. Small does not mean unimportant.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

The protists teach two lessons at once. First, that a familiar label can hide great diversity — molecular data reveal “the protists” to be many distant relatives, not one family. Second, that some of the most consequential organisms on Earth are invisible: the phytoplankton feed the oceans and help fill the air. Next we meet the last of the four groups — the fungi, the great recyclers — and find that they, too, have been widely misunderstood.
