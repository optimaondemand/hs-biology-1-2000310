Reading 21.04
Fungi: The Great Recyclers
Anchor Reading · Absorptive Nutrition, Hyphae, and Partnerships Below Ground
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Because they grow in soil and often look plant-like, fungi were once grouped with plants. They are nothing of the kind. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 21.04. By the end you should be able to describe how a fungus is built and how it feeds, and explain the roles fungi play as the world's chief decomposers and as partners to other living things.

Why fungi are not plants

A mushroom pushing up from the forest floor looks a little like a small, pale plant — but the resemblance ends there. A plant is an autotroph: it makes its own food by photosynthesis. A fungus makes no food of its own at all. It has no chloroplasts and cannot photosynthesize. Instead it is a heterotroph, and it feeds in a way all its own: heterotrophic by absorption. The fungus secretes digestive enzymes out into its surroundings, breaks food down outside its body, and then absorbs the small, digested molecules. It digests first and eats second — the reverse of an animal.

Fungal cell walls are made of chitin — not the cellulose of plant walls.
Different wall, different feeding, no photosynthesis: a fungus is not a plant.

A body built of threads

Most of a fungus is not the mushroom you see. The main body is a hidden web of fine threads called hyphae, and a whole tangled mat of them is a mycelium, spreading unseen through soil or wood. This threadlike form is perfectly matched to the job of absorption: threads pack enormous surface area into a small space, so the fungus touches, digests, and soaks up nutrients from every direction. Their cell walls are built not of cellulose (as in plants) but of chitin — the same tough material in an insect's shell. When it is time to reproduce, fungi release vast numbers of tiny spores that drift on the air and grow wherever they land. Familiar fungi include the mushrooms of the woods, the molds that fuzz old bread, and the yeasts that raise bread and ferment drinks.

Here again form follows function: a body made of threads is a body built to absorb.

Recyclers, partners, and more

Fungi are the planet's principal decomposers. Along with bacteria, they break down fallen leaves, dead wood, and animal remains, returning locked-up nutrients to the soil so plants can use them again. Without this work, dead matter would simply pile up and the nutrients life depends on would never circulate.

Many fungi are also mutualists — partners that help another organism while being helped in return. In mycorrhizae, fungal threads wrap around and into a plant's roots: the fungus gathers water and minerals for the plant and receives sugars in return. The great majority of land plants rely on such partnerships, a connection you will meet again in Module 8 (plant roots) and Module 10 (ecosystems). A lichen is another partnership — a fungus living together with an alga or a cyanobacterium, the photosynthetic partner making food while the fungus provides shelter and moisture.

A minority of fungi are pathogens that cause disease in plants or animals. And fungi are of enormous value to us: they give us bread, cheese, and fermented foods, and important medicines — the first antibiotic, penicillin, was discovered as a substance made by a mold.

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Fungi complete the picture of this week's diversity. They are not plants but a kingdom of their own: heterotrophs that digest by absorption, built of chitin-walled threads, and reproducing by spores. As decomposers they recycle the dead; as partners they feed forests and clothe bare rock. Together with the prokaryotes and the protists, they show that the “household of life” is far larger and stranger than the plants and animals we notice first — and that careful naming, guided by evidence, is how we come to know it.
