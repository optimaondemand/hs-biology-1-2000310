Protists: The Eukaryotic Grab-Bag
Lesson · A Diverse Catch-All — and the Ocean's Hidden Producers
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What do you call a group defined only by what it is not — and why does one of its smallest members matter to every breath you take?
Today we meet the protists: eukaryotes that are not plants, animals, or fungi. They are a true catch-all, mostly single cells, sorted loosely into animal-like, plant-like, and fungus-like ways of living. You will see why they are so hard to define — and why the plant-like phytoplankton are among the most important organisms on Earth.

Why “protist” is a convenient label

A protist is a eukaryote — a cell with a true nucleus — that is not a plant, animal, or fungus. Defining a group by exclusion is a warning sign, and here it is accurate: the protists are a catch-all, many only-distantly-related lineages, mostly unicellular, that share a level of organization rather than a close common ancestor. Molecular data (the same tool from Lesson 21.01) show the group is far more varied than its single name suggests. Biologists keep the word for convenience while knowing it does not name one clean branch of the tree.

🐢 PART 2 · THREE WAYS OF LIVING

Animal-like, plant-like, fungus-like

Because the group is so mixed, biologists sort protists loosely by how they live. Animal-like protists (protozoa) ingest food and often move — the shape-shifting Amoeba engulfs prey; the slipper-shaped Paramecium beats tiny hairs to swim. Plant-like protists (algae) photosynthesize; among them are the phytoplankton. Fungus-like protists (slime molds) absorb nutrients from decaying matter. These labels name function, not family: an “animal-like” protist is not a relative of animals.

🌊 PART 3 · THE MIGHTIEST OF THE SMALL

Why phytoplankton matter

Drifting in every sunlit sea are countless phytoplankton — photosynthetic protists too small to see. Because they photosynthesize across the enormous area of the oceans, they are a major source of Earth's atmospheric oxygen: a great share of every breath traces back to them. And because they turn sunlight into food, they are the base of nearly every aquatic food web — the producers that feed the tiny grazers, which feed the fish, seabirds, and whales.
