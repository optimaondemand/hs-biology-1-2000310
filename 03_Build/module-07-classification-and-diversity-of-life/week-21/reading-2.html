Reading 21.02
Bacteria and Archaea: Life Without a Nucleus
Anchor Reading · Prokaryotic Structure, Metabolism, and the Roles That Run the World
📖 WHAT YOU ARE READING TODAY
Two of the three domains — Bacteria and Archaea — are made of prokaryotic cells: no nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles. They are the oldest, smallest, and most numerous form of life on Earth. This is the anchor reading for Lesson 21.02. By the end you should be able to describe how a prokaryotic cell is built and name the ecological jobs these organisms do — jobs without which the living world would grind to a halt.

What a prokaryotic cell is

Recall from Module 3 that a prokaryote has no membrane-bound nucleus and no organelles such as mitochondria. Its single loop of DNA sits in an open region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid — not walled off, just gathered there. The cell still has ribosomes to build proteins, a cell membrane, and, in almost all cases, a protective cell wall. Many have whip-like flagella for movement. When it is time to reproduce, a prokaryote simply copies its DNA and splits in two — a fast, asexual process called binary fission. Under good conditions some can divide every twenty minutes, which is why a single cell can become a visible colony overnight.

A small difference in the wall is one of the clues that these are two separate domains, not one.

Alike in shape, different in chemistry

Bacteria and Archaea look alike, but their chemistry differs. The wall of a bacterium is built from a tough mesh called peptidoglycan; the wall of an archaeon is made of other materials entirely and contains no peptidoglycan. Their cell membranes are built from different kinds of lipids, too. These molecular differences — invisible to the eye but plain in the chemistry — are exactly the sort of evidence that revealed them as two separate domains, just as you read in Lesson 21.01.

A staggering variety of ways to make a living

What prokaryotes lack in size they more than make up for in metabolic diversity — the sheer number of different ways they get energy and carbon. Some are autotrophs that build their own food, whether by photosynthesis (like the cyanobacteria that first filled Earth's air with oxygen) or by harvesting energy from chemicals in rock and water. Others are heterotrophs that consume organic matter. Some require oxygen (aerobic); others are poisoned by it and live only where oxygen is absent (anaerobic). No other group of organisms can occupy so many chemical niches.

This is a lesson in function following form: a simple cell, freed from the costs of complexity, can specialize in an astonishing range of chemistries and so live almost anywhere.

The unseen work that keeps ecosystems running

It is tempting to think of bacteria only as germs, but the vast majority are harmless or essential. As decomposers, they break down dead matter and return nutrients to the soil. As nitrogen fixers, certain bacteria pull nitrogen gas from the air and convert it into a form plants can use — a process you will meet again in Module 10 as a key step in the nitrogen cycle, and the reason many crops depend on bacteria living in their roots. Trillions of bacteria make up your gut microbiome, helping you digest food and holding harmful microbes in check. Only a minority are pathogens that cause disease.

Archaea, meanwhile, are famous as extremophiles — many thrive in conditions that would kill almost anything else: near-boiling hot springs, water saltier than the sea, and airless mud. A group called methanogens produces methane gas and lives in marshes, in landfills, and in the guts of cattle and even humans. (Not every archaeon is an extremist — many live in ordinary soil and ocean — but the extreme-dwellers are how the domain first drew attention.)

💪 WHY THIS MATTERS

Bacteria and Archaea are the foundation the rest of the household of life is built upon. They recycle the dead, feed the soil, fill the air with oxygen, and live where nothing else can. Their simple cells hide an enormous chemical range — and the quiet differences in their walls and membranes are what marked them, in the end, as two of the three great domains. Next we cross into Eukarya and meet its most bewildering members: the protists.
