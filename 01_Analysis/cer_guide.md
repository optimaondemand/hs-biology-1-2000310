# CER Framework Guide — High School Biology

## What CER Is and Why It Matters

CER stands for Claim, Evidence, Reasoning. It is not just an assignment format — it is the logic of scientific argument made visible. Scientists argue in CER. Lawyers argue in CER. Historians argue in CER. When students learn to write in CER, they are learning to think.

In the classical tradition, this maps onto Aristotelian rhetoric:
- Claim = Thesis (what you assert)
- Evidence = Proof (what you can point to)
- Reasoning = Logos (why the proof supports the thesis)

The goal is not to answer a question but to make an argument that could persuade a skeptical, intelligent reader.

---

## The Three Components

### Claim
A clear, specific, arguable statement — not a question, not a procedure, not a vague generalization. A good claim is something that could be wrong. If there is no possible counterevidence, it is not a claim; it is a definition.

Weak: "Photosynthesis is important for life."
Strong: "The rate of photosynthesis in aquatic plants increases as light intensity increases, up to a saturation point."

### Evidence
Specific observations, data, experimental results, historical accounts, or documented examples. Evidence must be relevant — not just interesting facts about the topic. Multiple pieces strengthen the argument. Students must explain what the evidence shows, not just name it.

Weak: "Scientists have shown plants need light."
Strong: "In the spinach leaf disk assay, disks in high light floated significantly faster than disks in low light (8 disks vs. 2 disks floating after 10 minutes), indicating higher rates of oxygen production and photosynthesis."

### Reasoning
The logical bridge between evidence and claim. Reasoning explains WHY the evidence supports the claim — not just THAT it does. Strong reasoning addresses the underlying scientific principle and engages at least one counterargument or limitation.

Weak: "This shows photosynthesis uses light."
Strong: "Because more disks floated faster in high-light conditions, photosynthesis must be producing more oxygen at higher light intensities. This is consistent with the light-dependent reactions requiring photons to energize electrons in the thylakoid membrane. The counterargument that temperature differences caused the results is unlikely because both conditions were at room temperature."

## Scaffolding Levels

### Level 1 — Guided (new concept or early in course)
Provide the claim. Ask students to find evidence and write reasoning.
"Claim (given): Cell membranes are selectively permeable. Find two pieces of evidence for this. Then explain the reasoning: WHY does selective permeability matter for cell survival?"

### Level 2 — Semi-structured
Provide the question. Students write all three components.
"Question: Why does meiosis produce more genetic variation than mitosis? Write a full CER with at least two pieces of evidence."

### Level 3 — Open inquiry
Provide a scenario, data set, or historical episode. Students identify the question and write the full CER.
"You are given data from Rutherford's gold foil experiment. Most alpha particles pass straight through gold foil; a small number deflect at large angles; a very small number bounce almost straight back. What claim about atomic structure does this evidence support?"

### Level 4 — Contested question
A genuinely open or debated question. Students argue a position and engage the strongest counterargument.
"Some scientists argue the origin of life required a warm shallow pond. Others argue it occurred near deep-sea hydrothermal vents. Others propose panspermia. Choose one hypothesis, defend it with evidence, and address the strongest objection."

## CER in Historical Context

One powerful variation: ask students to write a CER AS IF they were a historical scientist, using only the evidence available at that time.

"Write the CER Gregor Mendel might have written after his pea plant experiments. Remember: Mendel had no knowledge of DNA, chromosomes, or meiosis. Use only what he could observe."

This forces students to understand what evidence a scientist actually had, prevents smuggling in modern knowledge, and models intellectual humility.

## Florida Biology EOC and CER

The Florida Biology EOC does not use CER format directly, but CER thinking is the best preparation for EOC questions that ask students to:
- Analyze experimental data and draw conclusions (evidence + reasoning)
- Evaluate a scientific claim (claim + evidence)
- Explain why something happens at a mechanistic level (reasoning)
- Compare two scenarios and predict outcomes (claim + reasoning)

Students who can write a strong CER will perform better on EOC constructed-response and data analysis items than students who only memorized vocabulary.

## Scoring Guide for CER Assignments

| Component | Strong (3) | Developing (2) | Beginning (1) |
|-----------|-----------|----------|------------|
| Claim | Specific, arguable, directly addresses the question | Somewhat specific but vague or incomplete | Restates the question or too general |
| Evidence | Two or more pieces, specific, accurately described, each explained | One or two pieces with partial explanation | Generic or unexplained |
| Reasoning | Explains the scientific principle; addresses counterargument or limitation | Connects evidence to claim but gaps in logic | Restates evidence without logical bridge |
| Integration | Flows as a coherent argument | Components present but loosely connected | Listed independently without connection |

## All 10 Unit CER Prompts

| Unit | CER Driving Question |
|------|---------------------|
| 1: Nature of Science | "Should a scientific theory that has never been falsified be considered a fact?" |
| 2: Macromolecules | "Why is water essential to life? Use at least three chemical properties as evidence." |
| 3: Cell Theory | "Evaluate the evidence for endosymbiotic theory. Is it convincing?" |
| 4: Photosynthesis/Respiration | "Is cellular respiration simply the reverse of photosynthesis?" |
| 5: Plant Structure | "Using plant hormones and tropisms, argue that plants can 'sense' and 'respond' to their world." |
| 6: Genetics | "Is cancer a genetic disease? Use evidence from the cell cycle and mutations." |
| 7: Evolution | "Which line of evidence for evolution do you find most compelling? Defend using three specific pieces." |
| 8: Ecology | "Using food webs and bioaccumulation, evaluate whether Carson's DDT claim was scientifically sound." |
| 9: Biotechnology | "Is CRISPR gene editing a natural extension of selective breeding, or fundamentally different?" |
| 10: Body Systems | "Using evidence from immunology, explain why a vaccine does not cause the disease it prevents." |

## Tips for On-Demand and Asynchronous Settings

For on-demand students who cannot participate in class discussions:
- Provide the CER prompt as a written or video assignment with a clear rubric
- Allow students to respond in written or recorded video format
- Consider a "peer review" step: students exchange CERs and evaluate using the scoring guide
- Use discussion boards for asynchronous "CER discussions" where students post claims and respond with counterarguments
