# Unit Reference Guide — High School Biology

Each unit includes: core idea, key historical figures, human/historical thread, connection backward, connection forward, assignment/assessment recommendations, EOC readiness notes, and a CER prompt seed.

---

## Unit 1: Nature of Science

**Core idea:** Science is a disciplined process of human inquiry — not a body of settled facts, but a method of asking questions, testing ideas, and revising understanding based on evidence.

**Key figures:** Francis Bacon (empiricism, 1620), Karl Popper (falsifiability), Thomas Kuhn (paradigm shifts)

**Human/historical thread:** The Scientific Revolution was partly a revolt against inherited authority. Bacon argued that observation and experiment should replace received wisdom — a radical act. History is full of dissenters who were eventually vindicated: Wegener on continental drift, Semmelweis on handwashing, Marshall and Warren on ulcers. Teaching students *how* science works, including its social dynamics and self-correction, is more durable than any single fact.

**Connection forward:** Every unit that follows IS nature of science in action. Return to this unit when a new discovery or historical controversy appears. It is a lens for the entire year.

**Assignments & Assessments:**
- Lab safety contract and lab notebook setup
- Experimental design practice: identify IV, DV, constants, controls from a scenario
- CER #1: Evaluate a real-world claim (e.g., "Does drinking coffee stunt growth?")
- Short essay: "Describe a time you changed your mind because of evidence."
- On-demand adaptation: recorded 2-minute video response

**EOC Readiness:** Nature of science questions appear throughout the EOC. Students must distinguish hypothesis, theory, and law; identify experimental design flaws; evaluate claims. EOC practice: "A student claims plants grow better with music. Design an experiment to test this."

**CER Seed:** "Should a scientific theory that has never been falsified be considered a fact? Defend your answer using the definitions of hypothesis, theory, and law."

**Reflective seed:** What is the difference between being skeptical and being closed-minded? Why does science need both?

## Unit 2: Macromolecules and Properties of Water

**Core idea:** Life is chemistry. The four macromolecules — carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids — are the building blocks of every living thing, and water's unique properties make life on Earth possible.

**Key figures:** Friedrich Wohler (synthesized urea from inorganic materials, 1828 — shattered vitalism), Linus Pauling (protein structure), Rosalind Franklin and Watson & Crick (DNA structure — with important ethical dimensions about credit)

**Human/historical thread:** For centuries, scientists believed organic molecules could only be made by living things — they required a "vital force." Wohler's synthesis of urea in 1828 was a quiet earthquake: he made a biological molecule in a flask from non-living materials, demolishing vitalism and opening the door to all of modern biochemistry. The story of DNA's structure involves questions of credit — Franklin's X-ray crystallography was used by Watson and Crick without her full knowledge.

**Connection backward:** From nature of science — this unit shows how a paradigm (vitalism) was overturned by a single well-designed experiment.

**Connection forward:** Macromolecules are the working parts of every cell. Proteins are enzymes driving photosynthesis and respiration (Unit 4). Nucleic acids carry the genetic code (Unit 6). Lipids form the cell membrane (Unit 3).

**Assignments & Assessments:**
- Water properties lab: surface tension, cohesion, adhesion, specific heat
- Food label macromolecule analysis: identify macromolecules in 3-5 real food products
- Macromolecule chart: monomer, polymer, function, example, biological role
- Indicator lab (Benedict's/Biuret/Lugol's) or virtual equivalent
- CER #2: "Why is water essential to life? Use at least three chemical properties as evidence."

**EOC Readiness:** Students must know the four macromolecules, monomers/polymers, and functions. Water properties (polarity, cohesion, adhesion, specific heat) are frequently tested. Common misconception: lipids are not polymers in the same sense as others. EOC practice: "Which property of water allows it to moderate temperature changes in living organisms?"

**CER Seed:** "A student argues that fats are unhealthy because they contain more energy than carbohydrates. Using macromolecule structure and function, evaluate this claim."

**Reflective seed:** Every macromolecule in your body was once food. What does that tell you about your relationship to the world around you?

## Unit 3: Cell Theory, Structure and Function

**Core idea:** All living things are made of cells, all cells come from pre-existing cells, and the cell is the fundamental unit of life. Cell structures are precisely matched to their functions.

**Key figures:** Robert Hooke (coined "cell," 1665), Anton van Leeuwenhoek (first microorganisms, 1670s — a Dutch draper with no formal training), Schleiden and Schwann (cell theory, 1838-39), Rudolf Virchow (all cells from cells, 1855), Lynn Margulis (endosymbiotic theory, 1967 — rejected by journals, eventually confirmed by DNA evidence)

**Human/historical thread:** Leeuwenhoek was a cloth merchant who ground his own lenses out of curiosity and became the first person to see bacteria. He wrote to the Royal Society and was initially doubted — then vindicated. Margulis proposed endosymbiotic theory and was rejected by more than a dozen journals. Her evidence was confirmed when scientists sequenced mitochondrial DNA and found it resembled bacterial DNA. Both stories: transformative science often comes from unexpected people and is initially resisted.

**Connection backward:** Macromolecules (Unit 2) are the building blocks of cell structures — phospholipid bilayer, enzyme proteins, DNA as nucleic acid.

**Connection forward:** Cell structure is the foundation for photosynthesis and respiration (Unit 4), cell division (Unit 6), and all body systems (Unit 10). Every organ system is a coordinated community of specialized cells.

**Assignments & Assessments:**
- Microscopy lab: onion cells, Elodea, cheek cells — draw, label, compare
- Cell theory timeline: visual tracing five contributors across 200 years
- Organelle function chart: structure, function, city/school analogy
- Cell analogy project: student-choice comparison with written justification
- CER #3: "Evaluate the evidence for endosymbiotic theory. Is it convincing? What would strengthen it?"
- Unit quiz with labeled diagram

**EOC Readiness:** Cell structure and function is heavily tested. Students must know prokaryotic vs. eukaryotic, plant vs. animal cell differences, organelle functions, fluid mosaic model, and cell transport (diffusion, osmosis, active transport). Common misconception: cell wall and cell membrane are often confused. EOC practice: "Which organelle packages and ships proteins within a cell?"

**CER Seed:** "Virchow's contribution 'all cells from pre-existing cells' was the most important addition to cell theory. Do you agree? Defend with evidence from the history of cell biology."

**Reflective seed:** You began as a single cell. Every cell in your body descended from that one cell. What does that suggest about growth, identity, or continuity?

## Unit 4: Photosynthesis and Cellular Respiration

**Core idea:** Life runs on energy transformations. Photosynthesis captures solar energy and stores it in glucose; cellular respiration releases that energy as ATP for cellular work. These two processes are deeply interconnected.

**Key figures:** Jan Baptista van Helmont (willow experiment, 1648 — first quantitative plant growth study), Joseph Priestley (plants restore air, 1771), Antoine Lavoisier (respiration is controlled combustion), Hans Krebs (citric acid cycle, Nobel 1953)

**Human/historical thread:** Van Helmont planted a willow tree, weighed everything over five years — tree gained 164 pounds; soil lost only 2 ounces. He concluded (incorrectly) that mass came from water. His method was revolutionary even if his conclusion was wrong — one of the first quantitative biology experiments. Priestley discovered plants restore air without knowing what oxygen was. Lavoisier's insight that respiration is a slow combustion — that life burns fuel, just slowly — was a profound reframing of what it means to be alive.

**Connection backward:** Chloroplasts and mitochondria are endosymbiotic organelles (Unit 3). Enzymes driving both processes are proteins built from amino acids studied in Unit 2.

**Connection forward:** Every food web in ecology (Unit 8) is a map of energy originally captured by photosynthesis. ATP powers DNA replication (Unit 6) and all body system functions (Unit 10).

**Assignments & Assessments:**
- Spinach leaf disk assay: measure photosynthesis rate under different light conditions
- Cellular respiration lab: yeast and sugar (CO2 production) or germinating seeds
- Photosynthesis/respiration diagram: annotate inputs, outputs, location in cell
- Compare/contrast chart: photosynthesis vs. cellular respiration
- CER #4: "Is cellular respiration simply the reverse of photosynthesis? Use specific evidence from both processes."

**EOC Readiness:** Both processes are major EOC topics. Students must know reactants, products, and stages (light reactions vs. Calvin cycle; glycolysis, Krebs, ETC). ATP's role is heavily tested. Common misconception: plants only photosynthesize and do not respire. EOC practice: "Which stage of cellular respiration produces the most ATP?"

**CER Seed:** "Trace the path of a carbon atom from the atmosphere through photosynthesis, into glucose, and through cellular respiration into ATP in a muscle cell."

**Reflective seed:** Lavoisier said life is a slow combustion. Does that feel right — or does it leave something out? What does it mean to reduce life to chemistry?

## Unit 5: Plant Structure and Function

**Core idea:** Plants are complex organisms with specialized tissues that allow them to capture energy, transport materials, reproduce, and respond to their environment — without a nervous system.

**Key figures:** Marcello Malpighi (plant anatomy, 1670s), Stephen Hales (plant water movement, 1727), Charles Darwin (phototropism experiments), Frits Went (discovered auxin, 1926)

**Human/historical thread:** Darwin's last major scientific work was not about evolution — it was about plants. In *The Power of Movement in Plants* (1880), he showed that grass seedlings bend toward light and the signal comes from the shoot tip, not where bending occurs. He had no idea what the signal was. Frits Went isolated auxin 46 years later. Darwin's careful observation opened a question that took nearly half a century for someone else to answer — a model of patient, honest science.

**Connection backward:** Plant cells have chloroplasts and cell walls (Unit 3). Photosynthesis (Unit 4) occurs in leaves; its rate depends on leaf structure. Xylem and phloem are made of specialized cells.

**Connection forward:** Plants are the foundation of every food web (Unit 8). Plant hormones parallel the endocrine system in human body systems (Unit 10).

**Assignments & Assessments:**
- Transpiration lab: celery or carnations in colored water; measure water uptake
- Leaf cross-section diagram: label epidermis, mesophyll, stomata, xylem, phloem
- Flower dissection: identify and label all reproductive parts
- Tropism investigation: grow plants in different light/gravity conditions; document with photos
- CER #5: "How does cohesion-tension theory explain water transport to the top of a tall tree? Is this mechanism sufficient?"

**EOC Readiness:** Plant tissues (dermal, ground, vascular) and functions are tested. Students must know xylem vs. phloem, stomata and transpiration, and basic plant organ functions. Common misconception: xylem carries water up; phloem carries sugars (often reversed). EOC practice: "Which tissue transports sugars produced during photosynthesis throughout the plant?"

**CER Seed:** "Plants respond to their environment without a nervous system. Using evidence from plant hormones and tropisms, argue that plants can 'sense' and 'respond' to their world."

**Reflective seed:** Darwin spent years watching plants move. What does that patient observation tell us about what it means to be a scientist?

## Unit 6: Genetics, Mitosis, Meiosis, and the Cell Cycle

**Core idea:** Life reproduces by copying and distributing genetic information. The cell cycle, mitosis, and meiosis are the mechanisms; genetics is the logic — how traits are inherited and why variation exists.

**Key figures:** Gregor Mendel (inheritance laws, 1866 — ignored for 35 years), Walter Flemming (mitosis, 1879), Nettie Stevens (sex chromosomes, 1905 — credit largely denied during her lifetime), Thomas Hunt Morgan (chromosomes carry genes), Barbara McClintock (transposable elements, Nobel 1983 — dismissed for decades)

**Human/historical thread:** Mendel spent 8 years crossing tens of thousands of pea plants in a monastery garden. He published in 1866; almost no one read it. He died in 1884 believing his work had failed. In 1900, three scientists independently rediscovered his paper. McClintock's "jumping genes" were so ahead of their time that she presented them to silence for 30 years — until molecular biology caught up with her. Both stories: the gap between doing good science and being recognized for it can be enormous.

**Connection backward:** DNA structure (Unit 2) is central. The nucleus (Unit 3) is where DNA lives. Cell division requires energy from ATP (Unit 4).

**Connection forward:** Meiosis generates variation — the raw material for evolution (Unit 7). Mutations connect to biotechnology (Unit 9) and genetic diseases in body systems (Unit 10).

**Assignments & Assessments:**
- DNA model build: physical (pipe cleaners, beads) or virtual
- Mitosis ordering activity: sort images of cells in different phases
- Meiosis modeling: colored pipe cleaners to simulate crossing over and independent assortment
- Punnett square practice: mono and dihybrid crosses, sex-linked traits
- Pedigree analysis: trace a genetic condition through a family tree
- CER #6: "Is cancer a genetic disease? Use evidence from the cell cycle, mutations, and cell division."
- Genetics unit test (EOC-style format)

**EOC Readiness:** This is one of the heaviest EOC units. Students must master DNA structure, replication, mitosis vs. meiosis, Mendelian genetics, and non-Mendelian patterns. Common misconception: meiosis produces 4 genetically identical cells (wrong — they are genetically unique). EOC practice: "A woman who is a carrier for color blindness has children with a man with normal vision. What is the probability their son will be color blind?"

**CER Seed:** "Mendel discovered the rules of inheritance without knowing about DNA, chromosomes, or meiosis. What does this tell us about how scientific understanding can be correct before it is complete?"

**Reflective seed:** You share approximately 50% of your DNA with each of your parents, and some percentage with every human alive. What does that do to how you think about identity or individuality?

## Unit 7: Evolution, Classification, and Origins of Life

**Core idea:** All life shares common ancestry and has diversified through natural selection and other mechanisms over billions of years. Classification reflects evolutionary relationships. The origin of life remains genuinely open.

**Key figures:** Carl Linnaeus (classification, 1735), Darwin and Wallace (natural selection, 1858-59), Ernst Mayr (modern synthesis), Carl Woese (three-domain system, 1977 — ignored for years), Stanley Miller and Harold Urey (abiogenesis experiment, 1953), Louis Pasteur (disproved spontaneous generation, 1859)

**Human/historical thread:** Darwin delayed publishing for 20 years — gathering evidence obsessively, anticipating objections. When Wallace sent a nearly identical theory, Darwin faced a genuine dilemma. Woese proposed in 1977 that life has three domains based on rRNA sequences. He was ignored for years. When molecular evidence became overwhelming, the entire tree of life had to be redrawn. The origin of life remains genuinely unsolved — Miller and Urey showed amino acids can form spontaneously, but the path from chemistry to a self-replicating cell is still actively debated.

**Connection backward:** Genetic variation from meiosis and mutation (Unit 6) is the raw material evolution acts on.

**Connection forward:** Ecological niches (Unit 8) are shaped by millions of years of natural selection. The universality of the genetic code — evidence of common ancestry — is what makes biotechnology (Unit 9) possible.

**Assignments & Assessments:**
- Evidence for evolution organizer: fossil record, comparative anatomy, biogeography, molecular evidence, observed change
- Comparative anatomy lab: examine homologous forelimb structures across vertebrates
- Cladogram construction: build a phylogenetic tree from given characteristics
- Natural selection simulation: bird beak lab (tools as beaks picking up different "seeds")
- Human evolution timeline: hominid fossil record, key species, traits
- CER #7: "Which line of evidence for evolution do you find most compelling? Defend using at least three specific pieces of evidence."
- Evolution unit test

**EOC Readiness:** Evolution is a major EOC domain. Students must know mechanisms (natural selection, mutation, genetic drift, gene flow), evidence, Hardy-Weinberg, speciation, and hominid evolution. Common misconception: "survival of the fittest" means strongest (fitness = reproductive success in a specific environment). EOC practice: "A population of beetles lives on sandy beach. Birds eat brown beetles more than tan beetles. After 10 generations, predict the beetle population."

**CER Seed:** "The genetic code is nearly universal — the same codons code for the same amino acids in bacteria, plants, and humans. What does this evidence suggest about the origins of life? What does it NOT tell us?"

**Reflective seed:** Darwin was reluctant to publish for 20 years. When is patience a scientific virtue and when does it become avoidance?

## Unit 8: Ecology

**Core idea:** Living things exist in webs of relationship with each other and their physical environment. Energy flows through ecosystems; matter cycles; populations fluctuate; ecosystems are dynamic.

**Key figures:** Alexander von Humboldt (father of ecology — saw nature as interconnected, early 1800s), Charles Elton (food chains and niches, 1927), Aldo Leopold (land ethic, 1949), Rachel Carson (*Silent Spring*, 1962)

**Human/historical thread:** Rachel Carson traced disappearing songbirds to DDT moving through food webs — concentrating at each trophic level until it thinned the eggshells of predatory birds. Her book was attacked by the chemical industry, which called her hysterical and unqualified. She continued working while dying of cancer, testifying before Congress. Her evidence was right. DDT was banned. She is one of the most consequential scientists of the 20th century — not for a lab discovery, but for communicating science in a way that changed policy and saved species.

**Connection backward:** Every food web originates in photosynthesis (Unit 4). Organisms filling ecological niches were shaped by evolution (Unit 7).

**Connection forward:** Human ecological impact is the ethical context for biotechnology decisions (Unit 9). Ecosystem health is inseparable from human health — a point Carson demonstrated.

**Assignments & Assessments:**
- Food web construction: given species list, build food web; remove keystone species and trace effects
- Population growth graph analysis: exponential vs. logistic; identify carrying capacity
- Biogeochemical cycle diagram: carbon and nitrogen cycles with human impacts
- Local ecosystem observation journal: document one area over several weeks
- Keystone species case study: wolves in Yellowstone trophic cascade
- Biome research project: each student researches one biome
- CER #8: "Carson argued DDT was causing a 'silent spring.' Using food webs and bioaccumulation, evaluate whether her claim was scientifically sound."
- Ecology unit test

**EOC Readiness:** Ecology is a major EOC domain. Students must know levels of organization, energy flow (10% rule), trophic levels, biogeochemical cycles, population growth, carrying capacity, interspecific interactions, succession, and biodiversity. Common misconception: energy is recycled through ecosystems (wrong — matter cycles; energy flows through and is lost as heat). EOC practice: "If a deer population grows exponentially, what environmental factors are likely absent?"

**CER Seed:** "Using food webs and the 10% energy rule, argue that a plant-based diet has a smaller ecological impact than a diet based primarily on meat."

**Reflective seed:** Leopold wrote that "a thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community." Is that scientific, ethical, or both? Do you agree?

## Unit 9: Biotechnology, PCR, and DNA

**Core idea:** The same DNA that carries genetic instructions in every living cell can now be read, copied, cut, spliced, and edited. Biotechnology has transformed medicine, agriculture, and forensics — and raises profound ethical questions.

**Key figures:** Frederick Sanger (DNA sequencing, 1977), Kary Mullis (PCR, Nobel 1993 — conceived while driving a California highway), Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier (CRISPR-Cas9, Nobel 2020), Marshall Nirenberg and Har Gobind Khorana (cracked the genetic code, Nobel 1968)

**Human/historical thread:** Mullis conceived PCR not in a laboratory but while driving Highway 128 through the California redwoods, imagining exponential DNA copying. The idea seemed almost too simple. It won the Nobel Prize and made DNA forensics, genetic testing, and COVID-19 testing possible. Doudna and Charpentier's CRISPR-Cas9 (2012) has been called the most important biological advance of the 21st century — and immediately raised the question: what should we edit, who decides, and what are the limits? CRISPR is actually a bacterial immune system — bacteria evolved it to fight viruses, and we borrowed it to edit human genes.

**Connection backward:** This unit depends on DNA structure (Unit 2/6), transcription and translation (Unit 6), and mutation. PCR uses the same base-pairing rules and enzyme activity from earlier units. CRISPR edits the genome using the same molecular machinery cells use naturally.

**Connection forward:** Biotechnology connects to body systems (Unit 10) through gene therapy and personalized medicine.

**Assignments & Assessments:**
- Gel electrophoresis simulation: virtual or physical — separate and analyze DNA fragments
- DNA fingerprinting activity: solve a "crime" scenario using gel results
- PCR concept mapping: trace steps using knowledge of DNA replication
- CRISPR debate/position paper: "Should CRISPR be used to edit the human germline to eliminate inherited diseases?"
- GMO case study: evaluate one GMO application using CER format
- CER #9: "Is CRISPR gene editing a natural extension of selective breeding, or is it fundamentally different?"
- Biotechnology unit test

**EOC Readiness:** Biotechnology is tested on the Florida Biology EOC. Students must understand gel electrophoresis, restriction enzymes, recombinant DNA, cloning, and applications in medicine and agriculture. Common misconception: gel electrophoresis separates DNA by charge (wrong — DNA fragments separate by size). EOC practice: "A scientist cuts DNA with restriction enzymes and runs gel electrophoresis. Which fragments migrate farthest from the wells?"

**CER Seed:** "CRISPR is a molecular tool bacteria evolved to fight viruses. What does the fact that we borrowed a bacterial immune system to edit human genes tell us about evolution, the universality of DNA, and the relationship between basic and applied science?"

**Reflective seed:** If you could eliminate a heritable disease from your family's future by editing an embryo's genome, would you? What questions does science answer — and what questions doesn't it answer?

## Unit 10: Human Body Systems

**Core idea:** The human body is a community of cells organized into tissues, organs, and organ systems that maintain homeostasis through constant coordination and feedback.

**Key figures:** William Harvey (blood circulation, 1628 — overturned 1,400 years of Galenic medicine), Edward Jenner (vaccination, 1796), Pasteur and Koch (germ theory, 1860s-1880s), Claude Bernard (internal environment concept, 1865), Walter Cannon (coined "homeostasis," 1926), Frederick Banting and Charles Best (insulin, 1921)

**Human/historical thread:** Harvey spent years measuring heart output and blood flow. His calculations showed the liver could not possibly produce blood fast enough to account for what the heart pumped — blood must circulate. He published in 1628 and was ridiculed. He was right. Banting was a small-town Canadian doctor with no research experience who convinced a skeptical senior scientist to give him a lab for the summer. Within months, they had isolated insulin. Within a year, diabetes had gone from a death sentence to a manageable disease. The human impact of that single discovery is incalculable.

**Connection backward:** Homeostasis depends on negative feedback loops rooted in chemistry. Blood type genetics (Unit 6) connects to cardiovascular system. The immune system uses antibody proteins (Unit 2). The digestive system breaks down macromolecules (Unit 2) using enzymes. Reproductive system depends on meiosis (Unit 6).

**Connection forward:** This is the capstone unit — the macroscopic human organism that is the product of every molecular, cellular, genetic, and evolutionary process studied all year. EOC review should explicitly trace these connections.

**Assignments & Assessments:**
- Heart rate lab: measure resting and active heart rate; graph and analyze
- Enzyme activity lab: amylase and starch digestion at different temperatures or pH
- Homeostasis diagram: negative feedback loop for blood glucose or body temperature
- Body systems integration project: trace one substance (oxygen, glucose, insulin, or a pathogen) through multiple systems
- Immune response diagram: innate vs. adaptive immunity; antigens and antibodies
- CER #10: "Using evidence from immunology, explain why a vaccine does not cause the disease it prevents."
- Human body systems unit test (EOC-style)
- Final reflection essay: "Which body system do you find most remarkable, and what does understanding it teach you about being human?"

**EOC Readiness:** Human body systems are heavily represented on the Florida Biology EOC. Key systems: circulatory, respiratory, digestive, excretory, nervous, endocrine, immune, reproductive, skeletal, muscular. Homeostasis and feedback loops are core. Common misconception: the immune system "remembers" through antibodies specifically (actually through memory B and T cells). EOC practice: "A person's blood glucose rises after a meal. Describe the negative feedback loop that returns it to normal."

**CER Seed:** "Harvey's calculation that blood must circulate was rejected for decades. What does this tell us about how new ideas are received in science, and what obligations does a scientific community have to evidence that challenges established views?"

**Reflective seed:** Every system in your body is working right now — heart beating, lungs exchanging gas, kidneys filtering, neurons firing. You are unaware of most of it. What does homeostasis suggest about the relationship between conscious and unconscious parts of being alive?

| From | To | The Bridge |
|------|-----|-----------|
| Nature of Science | All units | Every unit is a case study in scientific inquiry — use it as a lens all year |
| Macromolecules | Cell Structure | Phospholipids make membranes; proteins make enzymes; DNA is a nucleic acid |

| Photosynthesis/Respiration | Plant Structure | Leaves are photosynthesis organs; vascular tissue moves glucose and water |
| Cell Structure | Genetics | DNA lives in the nucleus; ribosomes make proteins from genetic instructions |
| Genetics | Evolution | Meiosis generates variation; mutation creates new alleles; variation + selection = evolution |

| Evolution | Ecology | Ecological niches are shaped by millions of years of natural selection |

| All units | Body Systems | The human body is the integrating capstone — every molecular, cellular, genetic, and ecological concept connects here |
